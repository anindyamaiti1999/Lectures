# Lichen Striatus — A Mini-Lecture

## 1. Setting the Scene: Why This Odd Little Rash Deserves Your Attention

Let me start with the clinical moment you'll actually encounter. A mother brings in her four-year-old with a narrow, slightly scaly, pinkish streak running down the back of one thigh. It appeared over about two weeks. It doesn't itch much. It isn't on the other leg. It doesn't follow a scratch, a burn, or a scar. It just… *runs*, like someone drew a line on the child with a dry paintbrush.

Or — and this is the version your exam question is asking about — a 32-year-old adult walks in with the same streak, but now it **itches relentlessly**, it's been there for eight months, and they are worried it's permanent.

Lichen striatus matters for three reasons:

1. **It is benign and self-limiting**, so recognising it saves the patient from months of unnecessary, sometimes harmful, therapy (think potent steroids on a child's face, or worse, a workup for something sinister).
2. **It is a beautiful natural demonstration of genetic mosaicism** — the skin is literally showing you the embryological migration pathways of its own cells. Few diseases teach embryology this vividly.
3. **It sits in a crowded differential of "linear rashes,"** and the ability to distinguish it from linear lichen planus, ILVEN, and linear psoriasis — all of which behave *completely differently* — is the mark of a competent dermatologist.

So the big picture: **lichen striatus is an immune attack on a genetically distinct stripe of skin, triggered by something environmental, that burns itself out within a year.** Everything else in this lecture is elaboration of that one sentence.

---

## 2. Unpacking the Name — and the Synonyms

**"Lichen"** in dermatology means small, flat-topped, closely-set papules — from the flat, crusty growths on rocks and tree bark. Whenever you see "lichen," picture *tiny flat plateaus*, and histologically expect a **lichenoid tissue reaction** (lymphocytes lined up at the dermo-epidermal junction, chewing on basal keratinocytes).

**"Striatus"** means striped. So the name is purely descriptive: *striped rash of tiny flat papules.*

The formal definition: **a benign, self-limiting, linear inflammatory (lichenoid) dermatosis following the lines of Blaschko**, characterised by the rapid eruption of 1–3 mm flat-topped papules that coalesce into continuous or interrupted linear bands.

You will meet several names for this and its relatives:

| Term | What it signals |
|---|---|
| **Lichen striatus** | The preferred, current term |
| **Linear lichenoid dermatosis** | A purely descriptive synonym stressing the lichenoid histology + Blaschkoid line |
| **Lichen striatus albus** | Used when the *resolving* phase leaves a prominent white (hypopigmented) streak — "albus" = white |
| **BLAISE** (Blaschkoid Acquired Inflammatory Skin Eruption) | A proposed *umbrella* term, not a separate disease. It acknowledges that several acquired inflammatory rashes (lichen striatus, some linear lichen planus, blaschkitis) may be variations on one theme: immune attack on a mosaic epidermal clone |

A quick word on **"blaschkitis"** since students always ask: some authors treat it as a distinct adult entity (more vesicular, wider bands, trunk-predominant, relapsing). Others consider it simply the adult end of the lichen striatus spectrum. For exams, mention that the distinction is debated; the management is the same.

## 3. The Absolute Core Concept: Blaschko's Lines

If you understand this section, the whole disease falls into place. If you skip it, lichen striatus will forever feel arbitrary.

**What Blaschko's lines are:** In 1901, the German dermatologist Alfred Blaschko mapped the linear patterns taken by hundreds of naevoid and linear skin diseases. He found they were *not* dermatomes, *not* blood vessels, *not* lymphatics. They formed a reproducible system: **V-shapes over the upper back, S-shaped whorls on the abdomen and flanks, and long linear streaks down the limbs**, stopping abruptly at the midline.

**What they represent:** the **migration pathways of ectodermal (keratinocyte and melanocyte) precursor cells during embryogenesis.** Early in development, a small number of founder cells in the ectoderm proliferate and stream outward as the embryo elongates and the trunk flexes. Each founder cell's descendants end up as a *ribbon* of skin — a clonal stripe.

**The analogy I want you to keep:** Think of the embryo as a balloon with a few dots of wet paint on it. As the balloon inflates and twists, each dot smears into a long streak. The final pattern of streaks looks bizarre, but it is entirely predictable from the geometry of the inflation. Your skin is that balloon; Blaschko's lines are the smears.

**The critical inference:** every person is a **mosaic** — a patchwork of clonal stripes. Normally these stripes are invisible because all the clones are genetically identical enough to look the same. But if one clone carries a post-zygotic (somatic) mutation or any distinguishing molecular marker, that stripe is *biologically different from its neighbours* — and it can be revealed, either by pigment (as in linear naevoid hypomelanosis) or by inflammation (as in lichen striatus).

**Key contrast for exams:** Blaschko's lines ≠ dermatomes. Dermatomes reflect *nerve* territories (posterior root ganglia, hence the band-like horizontal wrap of zoster). Blaschko's lines reflect *keratinocyte migration*. On a limb, they run **longitudinally** (up and down the long axis); dermatomes on a limb run more obliquely/circumferentially. A "zosteriform" variant of lichen striatus exists and is precisely where students get fooled.

## 4. Pathogenesis: A Two-Hit Model

Now let's build the mechanism as a cause → effect → outcome chain.

**Hit 1 — The silent mosaic clone.** During embryogenesis, one keratinocyte clone acquires a somatic alteration. It is functionally normal enough to make healthy skin, so for the first few years of life, nothing happens. It is a stripe of skin with a slightly different "molecular ID badge."

**Hit 2 — The immunological trigger.** Something perturbs the immune system: a viral infection, a vaccination, local trauma, pregnancy. During this perturbation, the immune system becomes activated and "audits" the tissue.

**The consequence.** **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes** recognise the mosaic clone's altered antigenic profile as foreign-ish and mount an attack. Because cytotoxic T cells target *antigen*, and the antigen is present only in the mosaic stripe, **the attack is confined with startling precision to that stripe — hence the unilateral, sharply linear, Blaschkoid eruption that stops at the midline.**

**The analogy:** imagine a company where one department was, years ago, issued slightly mis-printed ID badges. Nobody notices until a new head of security (the trigger) starts checking badges at every door. Suddenly *only* that department gets stopped, questioned, and escorted out — while the rest of the building carries on undisturbed. The security sweep eventually ends (self-limitation), and everyone returns to work. That's lichen striatus: a **time-limited immune audit of a mosaic stripe.**

This model elegantly explains four otherwise puzzling features:

| Observation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Strictly unilateral, midline-respecting | The target antigen exists only in one clonal stripe |
| Sudden onset after infection/vaccine | The trigger is immune activation, not clonal growth |
| Spontaneous resolution | Immune activation subsides; T cells become exhausted/tolerised; the "audit" ends |
| Rarity of recurrence in the same line | Tolerance is usually re-established (though occasional relapses are described, especially in adults) |

### Triggers you should be able to list

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Viral infection** | Varicella-zoster virus, Epstein–Barr virus (also reported with other common childhood viruses) |
| **Vaccination** | BCG, Hepatitis B, MMR |
| **Trauma** | Local injury or friction — a Koebner-like phenomenon, where local inflammation unmasks the clone |
| **Pregnancy** | Hormonal and immunological shifts (a recognised adult trigger) |
| **Host predisposition** | **Atopy — the strongest associated risk factor** |

### Why atopy matters so much

Roughly **50% of patients have an atopic diathesis** (atopic dermatitis, asthma, allergic rhinitis) — far above background. Two complementary explanations:

- An atopic immune system is intrinsically "twitchy" — a lower threshold for mounting cutaneous inflammatory responses against weak antigenic stimuli.
- Atopic skin has impaired barrier function, so more environmental antigen penetrates, more antigen presentation occurs, and the clone is more likely to be spotted.

This also explains a histological curiosity we'll come to: lichen striatus shows **spongiosis (eczema-like features) mixed with lichenoid change** — the eczematous flavour of an atopic host layered onto a cytotoxic lichenoid attack.

## 5. Epidemiology — and the Adult Difference

- **Age:** overwhelmingly a disease of **childhood**. Different textbooks quote slightly different windows — Rook's emphasises **5–15 years**, while other series report a **median onset around 2–3 years**. Don't be thrown by this; the defensible exam statement is *"predominantly young children, with most cases in the first decade and a reported range spanning toddlerhood to adolescence."*
- **Sex:** **female predominance, roughly 2:1.**
- **Adults:** **less common but well recognised** — and this is the crux of your question, because the adult form differs in three practically important ways:

| Feature | Children | Adults |
|---|---|---|
| **Symptoms** | Usually asymptomatic or mildly itchy | **Often intensely pruritic** |
| **Duration** | 6–12 months | Longer — up to **1–2 years** |
| **Treatment need** | Rarely more than reassurance | **Frequently requires active therapy** |

Why the difference? The honest answer is that it isn't fully settled, but the likely contributors are a more mature, more robust cytotoxic T-cell response in adults (a more vigorous "audit" producing more inflammation and therefore more itch), and greater psychological salience — adults notice, scrutinise, and are distressed by a visible streak in a way a toddler is not.

## 6. Clinical Features — What You Actually See

### Morphology

- **Individual lesions:** **1–3 mm** papules — small, **flat-topped (lichenoid)**, **pink to dull red, or brownish** in darker skin types. Often a fine, adherent scale.
- **Arrangement:** papules coalesce into **continuous or interrupted linear bands** along a Blaschko line. "Interrupted" is important — the band is often a dashed line rather than a solid one, because involvement along the clone is patchy.
- **Width:** anywhere from **2 mm to 2 cm**.
- **Length:** can be a few centimetres, or run the **entire length of a limb**.
- **Laterality:** **unilateral**, stopping at the midline. This is close to non-negotiable — bilateral disease should make you re-examine the diagnosis (though rare bilateral cases exist).
- **Sites:** **extremities are commonest (legs > arms)**; also neck, trunk, buttocks, and — importantly — **nails**. Facial involvement occurs and is cosmetically significant.

### Onset and course

- **Sudden onset**, evolving over **days to a few weeks** from a few papules to a full band. This rapid, *acquired* onset is one of your most useful discriminators (contrast ILVEN, which is congenital or very-early-onset).
- Then a **plateau phase** of weeks to months.
- Then **spontaneous involution**, usually leaving hypopigmentation.

### Symptoms

- Children: typically **asymptomatic**; the parents notice it, the child does not.
- Adults: **pruritus is often severe** — remember this, because it is the single fact that drives the entire adult management plan.

### Nail involvement — a high-yield pearl

When the affected Blaschko line runs through a **nail matrix**, the T-cell attack damages the matrix keratinocytes that manufacture the nail plate. Because the matrix is the nail's "factory floor," any injury there is transcribed into a structural defect in the plate that grows out distally.

Findings include:

- **Longitudinal ridging** and **splitting** (a damaged segment of matrix produces a grooved or fissured plate)
- **Onycholysis**
- **Trachyonychia** ("rough nail" — sandpapered, lustreless surface)
- Occasionally total loss of the plate segment

**The classic teaching point: unilateral, solitary (single-digit) nail dystrophy — especially in a child — should raise lichen striatus, even with no skin lesions at all.** The nail may be the *only* manifestation, or may persist after the skin has cleared. Think about it mechanistically: if the mosaic stripe happens to include the matrix but little visible skin, the nail is the only place the attack becomes visible.

Contrast this with other causes of nail dystrophy: lichen planus and psoriasis usually affect **multiple nails, bilaterally**; onychomycosis is asymmetrical but shows subungual hyperkeratosis and discolouration; and a solitary dystrophic nail with a longitudinal groove should also prompt you to exclude a **subungual/matrix tumour or myxoid cyst** in adults. Unilateral + single digit + child + linear = lichen striatus.

### Clinical variants

| Variant | Description |
|---|---|
| **Parallel / multiple** | Several parallel bands along adjacent Blaschko lines |
| **Zosteriform** | Arranged so as to mimic a dermatome — the great imitator of herpes zoster. Distinguished by absence of vesicles, absence of pain/prodrome, chronicity over weeks, and flat-topped lichenoid papules |
| **Bilateral / extensive** | **Rare (this is what the ambiguous "l/c" in your notes means: "less common")** — bilateral or very extensive involvement |
| **Lichen striatus albus** | The hypopigmented, resolving phase, sometimes so prominent it is the presenting complaint |

## 7. Histopathology — Reading the Mechanism Under the Microscope

Here's the principle I want you to internalise: **you are looking at the histological fingerprint of a cytotoxic T-cell attack on a *specific clone of cells*, in a host who is often atopic.** Every feature follows from that.

| Feature | What it looks like | Why it's there |
|---|---|---|
| **Lichenoid interface dermatitis** | Lymphocytes at the dermo-epidermal junction; **basal vacuolar degeneration** with **focal liquefactive necrosis** of basal keratinocytes | CD8⁺ T cells are killing target keratinocytes; dying cells swell and vacuolate, and the junction liquefies |
| **Dyskeratotic keratinocytes** | Scattered brightly eosinophilic, shrunken apoptotic cells (Civatte-type bodies) | The individual casualties of cytotoxic killing |
| **Spongiosis** | **Intercellular oedema**, with visible **stretching of tonofilament–desmosome complexes** | Inflammatory mediators increase permeability; fluid forces keratinocytes apart, pulling their desmosomal "rivets" into visible strands. The eczematous (often atopic) component |
| **Irregular acanthosis** | Thickened, uneven epidermis | Reactive proliferation in response to chronic injury |
| **Alternating parakeratosis and hyperkeratosis** | Patchy retained nuclei in the horny layer next to compact orthokeratosis | Focal disruption of orderly maturation where the epidermis is inflamed, alternating with unaffected segments |
| **Infiltrate distribution** | **Superficial *and* deep, perivascular, interstitial, and perieccrine** lymphohistiocytic infiltrate | Not just a junctional band — the T cells are tracking a *clone* that extends down adnexal structures |
| **Infiltrate composition** | Lymphohistiocytic, with **CD8⁺ > CD4⁺ T cells** | Confirms a cytotoxic, not merely helper-driven, process |
| **Dermal changes** | Dermal oedema, sometimes pigment incontinence (melanin-laden macrophages) | Inflammatory exudation; melanin released from dying basal cells is swallowed by dermal macrophages |

### 🔑 The single most examinable fact: the perieccrine infiltrate

**Lymphohistiocytic inflammation cuffing the eccrine sweat glands (and often the deep dermis) is the crucial histological sign that separates lichen striatus from linear lichen planus.**

Why does this make mechanistic sense? Because **eccrine glands are epidermal derivatives** — they develop by downgrowth from the same surface ectoderm, and therefore belong to the **same clone** as the overlying mosaic stripe. If the T cells are hunting a clone-specific antigen, they must pursue it wherever the clone goes: up into the epidermis *and* down into the sweat glands. Linear lichen planus, by contrast, is driven by antigen expressed at the **interface of the epidermis itself**, so its infiltrate remains a **tight, band-like, superficial** one and does not chase the eccrine coils.

**That's the whole trick:** *deep + perieccrine = lichen striatus; superficial band only = lichen planus.*

**The diagnostic triad to memorise:**
1. **Perieccrine (and deep perivascular) lymphohistiocytic infiltrate**
2. **Interface/lichenoid dermatitis** (vacuolar change, dyskeratosis)
3. **Spongiosis** coexisting with the lichenoid change

Lichenoid + spongiotic together, extending deep and around sweat glands, in a Blaschkoid streak — that combination is effectively diagnostic.

## 8. Differential Diagnosis — Reasoning, Not Memorising

The unifying question for any linear rash: **Was it there from birth or acquired? Does it itch? Will it go away? And what is the infiltrate doing on biopsy?**

| Differential | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|
| **Linear lichen planus** | Violaceous, polygonal papules with **Wickham's striae**; **wedge-shaped hypergranulosis**, **saw-tooth rete ridges**, dense **band-like superficial** infiltrate **without perieccrine involvement**; **chronic and persistent**, does not self-resolve; may be accompanied by oral/genital LP |
| **ILVEN** (inflammatory linear verrucous epidermal naevus) | **Present at birth or in early infancy**, **persistent for life, notoriously treatment-resistant**; verrucous/psoriasiform rather than lichenoid; histologically **alternating ortho- and parakeratosis** (columns with/without a granular layer) **without significant spongiosis or interface change** |
| **Linear psoriasis** | **Silvery, micaceous scale**; **Auspitz sign**; histology shows **Munro microabscesses** and **spongiform pustules of Kogoj**, regular parakeratosis, thinned suprapapillary plates; **chronic**, often with psoriasis elsewhere |
| **Linear porokeratosis** | **Thread-like raised keratotic border**; histology shows the **cornoid lamella** — a column of parakeratotic cells over a zone lacking a granular layer; persistent, with malignant-transformation potential |
| **Linear/segmental vitiligo** | **Pure depigmentation, no papules, no scale**; histology shows **absent melanocytes without inflammatory infiltrate**; progressive rather than resolving |
| **Naevoid (linear) hypomelanosis / hypomelanosis of Ito** | **Congenital or early**, hypopigmented **whorls and streaks along Blaschko's lines**, **non-inflammatory**, no preceding papular phase, permanent |
| **Herpes zoster** (for the zosteriform variant) | Painful prodrome, **grouped vesicles on an erythematous base**, **dermatomal** not Blaschkoid, evolves and crusts within 2–3 weeks |
| **Lichen simplex / lichenified eczema in a linear scratch pattern** | Follows scratching habit, not Blaschko geometry; marked lichenification, excoriations |

**The most important pairwise distinction — lichen striatus vs. linear lichen planus:**

| | Lichen striatus | Linear lichen planus |
|---|---|---|
| Typical age | Child | Adult |
| Colour | Pink / dull red / brown | Violaceous |
| Wickham's striae | Absent | Present |
| Itch | Mild (child) / severe (adult) | Usually marked |
| Course | **Self-limiting, <1–2 years** | **Chronic, persistent** |
| Infiltrate depth | **Superficial *and* deep, perieccrine** | **Superficial band only** |
| Spongiosis | **Present** | Absent/minimal |
| Hypergranulosis | Irregular/focal | **Wedge-shaped** |
| Mucosal disease | No | May coexist |

And the most important **prognostic** distinction — lichen striatus vs. ILVEN: one you can promise will disappear, the other you must warn will not. Getting this wrong means either falsely alarming a family or falsely reassuring them.

## 9. Natural History, the "Albus" Phase, and Prognosis

- **Overall:** benign and self-limiting. **Most cases resolve within a year.**
- **Children:** **6–12 months** is the usual window.
- **Adults:** more prolonged — commonly quoted as **1–2 years** — and more symptomatic, more likely to need therapy, and occasionally relapsing.
- **Prognosis: excellent.** No scarring, no systemic association, no malignant potential.

### Why the white streak appears — and why it isn't vitiligo

During the active phase, cytotoxic lymphocytes destroy basal keratinocytes, and melanocytes sitting in that same basal layer are caught in the crossfire — functionally stunned rather than annihilated. Pigment already made is released into the dermis and swallowed by macrophages (**pigment incontinence**). The net effect after the inflammation settles is **post-inflammatory hypopigmentation** — the streak turns white. Hence **lichen striatus albus**.

Two clinical consequences:

1. **This can persist for many months after the papules are gone**, so tell patients up front. Otherwise they conclude treatment "failed."
2. **It is hypopigmentation, not depigmentation.** Under Wood's lamp it does not show the stark, chalky, sharply-demarcated glow of vitiligo. The melanocytes are still present — they are just temporarily under-producing — so repigmentation is the rule.

This matters enormously in **darker skin phototypes**, where the contrast is far more conspicuous and the cosmetic distress much greater than the trivial inflammatory phase would suggest.

## 10. Management — with Emphasis on Adults

### Investigations

- **Diagnosis is usually clinical** — a rapidly acquired, unilateral, Blaschkoid band of lichenoid papules in a child needs nothing more.
- **Skin biopsy** is confirmatory and is the investigation to use when the presentation is atypical, when the patient is an adult, when the course is unusually prolonged, or when ILVEN/linear psoriasis/linear LP must be excluded (because those diagnoses change the prognosis and the treatment plan). Look for the **perieccrine infiltrate with interface change and spongiosis**.
- **Dermoscopy** can help (non-specific but supportive) and is useful to confirm the absence of Wickham's striae or a cornoid lamella.
- No routine blood tests are needed. No systemic workup is needed. Resist the temptation.

### The governing principle

**Lichen striatus resolves on its own. Therefore treatment is *symptomatic and cosmetic*, never curative — and the risk of treatment must never exceed the burden of the disease.** In children, that usually means doing nothing. In adults, it usually means doing something, because the itch is genuinely troublesome and the course is longer.

### Stepwise approach in adults

| Step | Therapy | Rationale and practical notes |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Reassurance and education** | Explain the benign, self-limiting nature; the expected 1–2 year course; and specifically that a **white streak will follow and will also fade**. | This is genuinely first-line therapy, not a formality. Most patient distress comes from fear of permanence. Pre-empting the albus phase prevents both panic and futile treatment escalation. Add gentle emollients and avoidance of irritation/scratching. |
| **2. Potent topical corticosteroids** | e.g. **clobetasol propionate 0.05%** or **betamethasone dipropionate**, short courses to the affected band. | Suppresses the CD8⁺ T-cell infiltrate and the inflammatory mediators driving itch — i.e., attacks the actual mechanism. Fastest relief of pruritus. **Caution:** limit duration; potent steroids on a limb band for months risk **atrophy, telangiectasia, striae, and — ironically — steroid-induced hypopigmentation that worsens the very cosmetic problem you're trying to treat.** Avoid on face, flexures, and genitals. |
| **3. Topical calcineurin inhibitors** | **Tacrolimus 0.1% ointment** or **pimecrolimus 1% cream**, twice daily. | **Preferred for adults needing prolonged therapy, and first choice for facial involvement.** They inhibit calcineurin → block NFAT → suppress T-cell IL-2 transcription → shut down the cytotoxic T-cell attack — *without* the collagen- and melanocyte-suppressing effects of steroids. Hence **no atrophy and no steroid hypopigmentation**, making them especially valuable during the **hypopigmented (albus) phase**, where they can be continued safely to hasten repigmentation. Counsel about transient burning/stinging in the first few days and sun protection. |
| **4. Antipruritics** | Oral antihistamines; menthol/pramoxine-containing emollients. | Adjunctive for the severe itch of the adult form, particularly for night-time scratching; helps break the itch–scratch cycle that can Koebnerise and prolong the lesion. |
| **5. Refractory disease** | **Photodynamic therapy (PDT)** is reported for **refractory adult cases** unresponsive to topicals. Phototherapy (NB-UVB) and targeted excimer light have also been used, mainly for the residual hypopigmentation. | Reserved, not routine. Justified only by the adult combination of severe symptoms plus long duration. |
| **6. Nail involvement** | **Potent TCS under occlusion** applied over the **proximal nail fold** (to reach the underlying matrix), and/or **intralesional triamcinolone acetonide 2.5–5 mg/mL** injected into the matrix region for persistent dystrophy. | The therapeutic target is the **matrix**, not the visible plate — the plate is already-manufactured, dead keratin and cannot be repaired. Counsel that improvement is only visible as the nail **grows out**: roughly **3 months for a fingernail, 6–12 months for a toenail.** Nail changes usually resolve spontaneously too, so weigh the discomfort of intralesional injections carefully. |

### 🔑 The Adults-vs-Children Pearl (your exam's central point)

**Children** essentially need **reassurance alone**, because the rash doesn't bother them and it will vanish within a year.

**Adults need active therapy** for three specific reasons:
1. **Significant pruritus** — the itch is genuinely disabling in a way it is not in children.
2. **Longer duration** — up to 2 years of symptoms is a long time to "wait and see."
3. **Greater cosmetic concern**, especially with a persistent hypopigmented streak on a visible site.

And the corollary: because adults are likely to need **months** of treatment, **topical calcineurin inhibitors are preferred over prolonged potent topical corticosteroids** to avoid cutaneous atrophy and steroid-induced pigment loss.

## 11. Common Misunderstandings — Let Me Head These Off

1. **"Linear means dermatomal."** No. Blaschko's lines are embryological keratinocyte migration routes, not nerve territories. A zosteriform *appearance* is a recognised variant, but the underlying logic is mosaicism, not neurotropism.

2. **"The white streak means the disease is getting worse / is becoming vitiligo."** No. Hypopigmentation is the *signature of resolution* — melanocytes temporarily under-functioning after inflammatory injury. It fades. It is hypo-, not de-pigmentation.

3. **"If treatment doesn't clear it in two weeks, it's the wrong diagnosis."** No. Treatment controls symptoms; it does not shorten the natural history much. Judge success by *itch relief*, not by disappearance of the streak.

4. **"Lichen striatus is a form of lichen planus."** No. They share a lichenoid reaction pattern, but differ in age, colour, symptoms, mucosal involvement, prognosis, and — decisively — in the **depth and perieccrine extension** of the infiltrate. Lichen planus persists; lichen striatus resolves.

5. **"Bilateral streaks are fine."** Rarely they occur, but bilaterality should push you to re-examine the diagnosis first, since the mosaic model predicts unilaterality.

6. **"A single dystrophic nail in a child needs a fungal workup and nothing else."** Remember lichen striatus can present with **isolated unilateral single-digit nail dystrophy**. Examine the whole limb along the Blaschko line for a faint or resolved band.

7. **"Since it's self-limiting, adults don't need treatment either."** This is the trap in your question. Self-limiting ≠ untreated. Adults itch, for longer, and care more about appearance.

## 12. A Note on Two Confusing Lines in the Source Notes

Because these could genuinely mislead you, let me flag them:

- Your notes read **"along the lines Blaschko"** — the correct phrasing is **"along the lines of Blaschko"** (or "Blaschko's lines"), after **Alfred Blaschko, 1901**.
- Your notes read **"If asymptomatic (adults): TCS and calcineurin inhibitor."** Taken literally this is self-contradictory — you do not treat an asymptomatic patient with active anti-inflammatories. The intended meaning, consistent with Rook's and Bolognia, is **"If *symptomatic* (adults): topical corticosteroids and/or a topical calcineurin inhibitor."** Asymptomatic patients — child or adult — need reassurance and observation only.
- **"Bilateral extensive (l/c)"** simply abbreviates **"less common."**
- **"Band-like infiltrate of LH"** — **LH = lymphohistiocytic.** And note that in lichen striatus the infiltrate is not *only* band-like: it is band-like at the junction **plus** deep, perivascular and perieccrine. That "plus" is the diagnostic gold.
- **"Dermatoses"** should read **"dermatosis"** — it is a single disease entity.

## 13. The Core Idea in One Picture

Picture a child's leg with a narrow pink dotted line running down it, like a seam. That seam is a **clonal stripe of skin laid down in the embryo along a Blaschko line** — a strip whose cells carry a slightly different molecular identity. For years nobody notices. Then a virus, a vaccine, or a bump activates the immune system, **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells spot the mismatched stripe, and attack it along its entire length — including down into the eccrine sweat glands that belong to the same clone** (which is exactly what the pathologist sees, and exactly what separates it from lichen planus). The attacked epidermis becomes lichenoid *and* spongiotic, dying keratinocytes spill their melanin, and if the stripe crosses a nail matrix, that one nail grows out ridged and rough. Then, within **6–12 months in a child or 1–2 years in an adult, the immune audit ends**, leaving a fading white streak — *lichen striatus albus* — as the only trace.

Because the ending is guaranteed, **management is about comfort and confidence, not cure.** In children: explain and wait. In adults — who itch hard, wait longer, and mind the cosmesis — add a **short burst of potent topical steroid for control**, then shift to a **topical calcineurin inhibitor for the long haul** (protecting skin thickness and pigment), treat the nail through the **proximal nail fold**, and reserve **photodynamic therapy** for the stubborn few.

**One sentence to carry away:** *Lichen striatus is a self-limiting cytotoxic T-cell attack on a mosaic epidermal clone along a Blaschko line — deep and perieccrine on biopsy, benign in outcome — which children simply outgrow and adults need treating for itch and appearance while they wait.*
