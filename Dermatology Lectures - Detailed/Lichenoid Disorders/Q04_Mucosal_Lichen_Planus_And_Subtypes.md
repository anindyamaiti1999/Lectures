# Mucosal Lichen Planus — A Mini-Lecture

---

## Part 1 — The Big Picture: Why Does This Topic Exist?

Let me start with a framing thought, because I think this is where most students go wrong.

When you first learn lichen planus (LP), you learn it as a **skin** disease: those violaceous, flat-topped, polygonal, pruritic papules with fine white lines on top — the classic "4 P's plus Wickham's striae." That's a perfectly good starting point. But here is the crucial conceptual leap:

> **Lichen planus is not fundamentally a disease of skin. It is a disease of *stratified squamous epithelium*.**

And stratified squamous epithelium doesn't stop at your skin. It lines your mouth, your tongue, your oesophagus, your larynx, your vulva and vagina, your glans penis, your anal canal — even your conjunctiva, nails, and hair follicles (which are just invaginated skin). So wherever that type of epithelium exists, lichen planus can set up shop.

This is why **30–50% of all LP patients have mucous membrane involvement**, and — this is the part that catches clinicians off guard — **roughly 15% have mucosal disease with *no* skin lesions at all.** That patient walks into your clinic with a sore, burning mouth and completely normal skin. If you don't have mucosal LP in your mental catalogue, you will miss them, or you will treat them for months as "recurrent candidiasis."

**A quick terminology note before we go further**, because examiners love it: it is **"mucous membrane,"** not "mucus membrane." *Mucus* is the noun — the slimy substance. *Mucous* is the adjective — the membrane that makes it. Similarly, the vulval condition that overlaps with LP is **lichen sclerosus**, not "lichen sclerosis." *Sclerosis* means hardening in general (as in systemic sclerosis, multiple sclerosis); *lichen sclerosus* is a specific, distinct named entity. These are small things, but they signal whether you actually know the field.

## Part 2 — The Mechanism: What Is Actually Happening in That Tissue?

You cannot reason about the subtypes, the pain, the scarring, or the cancer risk unless you understand the core immunological event. So let's build it step by step.

### The cause → effect → outcome chain

**Step 1 — The target is marked.**
For reasons still not fully understood (viral antigens, drugs, dental metals, self-antigens altered by some trigger), the **basal keratinocytes** — the stem-cell layer sitting on the basement membrane — begin displaying an antigen that the immune system interprets as foreign.

**Step 2 — Cytotoxic T cells arrive.**
**CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes** are recruited and accumulate in a dense, **band-like ("lichenoid") infiltrate** hugging the epithelial–connective tissue junction. Under the microscope it looks like a battle line drawn right along the basement membrane. This is the defining architecture of *all* lichenoid disease.

**Step 3 — The basal layer is destroyed.**
The T cells kill basal keratinocytes by apoptosis. Dead, shrunken keratinocytes appear as eosinophilic globules — **Civatte bodies / colloid bodies**. The basal layer becomes vacuolated and degenerate. This is called **interface dermatitis**, because the damage is precisely at the *interface* between epithelium and connective tissue.

**Step 4 — Everything else follows from Step 3.**

Here's the beautiful part — every clinical feature is a downstream consequence of that one event:

| Consequence of basal layer destruction | Clinical result |
|---|---|
| The epithelium loses its regenerative stem-cell layer → thins out | **Atrophic (red, raw) lesions** |
| Thinned epithelium separates or breaks down completely | **Erosive / ulcerative lesions** |
| Fluid accumulates at the damaged interface | **Bullous lesions** (rare) |
| The epithelium tries to compensate by over-proliferating and over-keratinising | **White reticular striae, plaques, papules** |
| Chronic damage repaired with fibrosis | **Scarring, adhesions, strictures, loss of architecture** |
| Decades of injury + repair + proliferation = replication errors | **Squamous cell carcinoma risk** |

**The single most useful analogy:** think of the epithelium as a **brick wall standing on a foundation**, and the basal layer as that foundation. The cytotoxic T cells are a demolition crew attacking *only the bottom row of bricks*.

- Where the crew works slowly and the wall keeps patching itself with extra plaster on top → you get **thick white lines** (reticular/plaque/papular forms).
- Where the crew outpaces repair → the wall gets **thin and you see the red plumbing behind it** (atrophic form).
- Where it collapses → a **hole** (erosive form).
- Where the patching goes on for twenty years → the patching cells eventually make a **mistake** (dysplasia → carcinoma).

Same disease, same mechanism, different balance between destruction and compensatory repair. **The "subtypes" are not different diseases — they are different points on a single destruction-versus-repair spectrum.** Hold onto that; it makes the whole table below feel obvious rather than memorised.

## Part 3 — Where It Strikes: The Anatomical Map

Mucosal LP has strong site preferences, and they follow the distribution of stratified squamous epithelium:

- **Buccal mucosa and tongue** — by far the most common; buccal mucosa is the single most characteristic site (and notably, often **bilateral and symmetrical**, which is itself a diagnostic clue).
- **Gingiva** — often as desquamative gingivitis.
- **Lips** — vermilion and inner labial mucosa.
- **Genital** — glans, prepuce, penile shaft, scrotum in men; vulva and vagina in women.
- **Perianal**.
- **Larynx** — hoarseness, and potentially airway compromise.
- **Oesophagus** — dysphagia, strictures.
- (Also recognised: conjunctiva, external ear canal, bladder, though these are rare.)

**Why does this map matter clinically?** Because these sites are *functional* sites. Skin LP itches and is cosmetically distressing. Mucosal LP interferes with **eating, swallowing, speaking, breathing, urinating, and sexual intercourse.** That's why mucosal LP is generally the more disabling form of the disease, even though it looks less dramatic on a photograph.

## Part 4 — Oral Lichen Planus in Detail

### The signature sign: Wickham's striae

The hallmark of oral LP is **white streaks on the buccal mucosa arranged in a delicate, branching, lace-like or fern-like network** — **Wickham's striae**.

*Why are they white?* Normal buccal mucosa is thin, non-keratinised, and moist, so the red vascular connective tissue beneath shows through — hence the pink colour. In LP, the epithelium responds to chronic injury by **thickening and keratinising** (hyperkeratosis/parakeratosis). Keratin is opaque and, when wet, appears white. So a white patch in the mouth is essentially **"abnormal keratin where there shouldn't be any."**

*Why lace-like?* Because the lymphocytic attack is patchy and focal, not uniform. The hyperkeratotic response follows those focal bands of inflammation, producing a reticulated pattern rather than a solid sheet. Think of **frost forming along the veins of a leaf**, or **the white foam patterns left on sand as a wave retreats** — irregular, branching, delicate.

Where you'll see them:
- Inner surface of the **cheeks** (classic)
- **Gingival margins**
- **Lips**

### On the tongue — a different look

On the tongue, LP behaves differently: instead of lace, you typically get **fixed white plaques that sit slightly depressed below the surrounding mucosa**, often with loss of the normal papillae.

*Why the difference?* The dorsal tongue is already a keratinised, papillated surface, mechanically abraded every time you eat and speak. Superimpose chronic lichenoid inflammation and you get **flattening, plaque formation, and atrophy of papillae** rather than delicate striae. Analogy: **draw a fine lace pattern on smooth glass and it shows beautifully; draw the same pattern on rough carpet and it just becomes a flat smudged patch.**

### Prevalence figures — and the trap

Two numbers are quoted and students routinely collide them:

- **30–50%** of LP patients have **some** mucosal involvement.
- **~15%** of LP patients have **isolated mucosal LP** (mucosa only, no skin disease).

⚠️ **Common misunderstanding:** these are not contradictory and they are not the same statistic. The first is *"how often is mucosa involved at all,"* the second is *"how often is mucosa the only thing involved."* If you're asked, say both, and explain the difference — that's what separates an answer from a recitation.

### Associated conditions (and *why* they associate)

| Association | The reasoning |
|---|---|
| **Diabetes mellitus** | Classically described as part of **Grinspan syndrome** (oral LP + diabetes + hypertension). Whether this is true causation or coincidence plus drug-induced lichenoid reactions (antihypertensives, hypoglycaemics) is debated — mention that nuance; it shows sophistication. |
| **Candidiasis** | Secondary, not primary. The altered, eroded epithelium plus topical/inhaled/systemic steroid therapy creates a perfect niche for *Candida*. Crucially, superimposed candidiasis **worsens the burning**, so it's a treatable cause of "treatment failure." Always look for it before escalating immunosuppression. |
| **Vulval / oral lichen sclerosus overlap** | Two interface/lichenoid diseases can coexist in the same patient, and their clinical and histological features blur into one another — a genuine diagnostic headache (discussed further under genital LP). |
| **Hepatitis C** (worth adding) | A well-documented association in Mediterranean and Japanese populations, weaker elsewhere. Reasonable to screen in endemic areas. |

## Part 5 — The Subtypes of Oral LP

This is the heart of the question. Remember the destruction-versus-repair spectrum — **the left-hand column of this table is essentially "how much epithelium is left."**

| Subtype | What it looks like | What's happening mechanistically | Symptoms & significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reticular** | Classic lace-like network of Wickham's striae | Repair *wins* — compensatory hyperkeratosis dominates; epithelium is intact and thickened | **Most common** subtype. Usually **asymptomatic** — often an incidental dental finding. Because the epithelium is intact, nerve endings are protected |
| **Papular** | Small discrete white papules | Earliest focal lesions; the individual "pixels" that later coalesce into striae | **Early / initial presentation**; easily missed |
| **Hypertrophic (plaque-like)** | Thick, homogeneous white plaques | Maximal hyperkeratotic/acanthotic response | **Mimics leukoplakia** — this is the great impostor. Biopsy is often needed to distinguish. Commonly on dorsal tongue |
| **Atrophic** | Red, glazed, thinned mucosa, often with striae at the periphery | Destruction *outpaces* repair — epithelium thins, submucosal vessels show through | **Painful**; burning on hot/spicy food. Often coexists with erosive disease. On gingiva → **desquamative gingivitis** |
| **Erosive / ulcerative** | Shallow, painful eroded areas with **radiating white striae at the margins** | Epithelium has broken down entirely; raw connective tissue and nerve endings exposed | **Most debilitating subtype.** Severe pain, impaired eating, weight loss. **Highest malignant transformation risk** |
| **Bullous** | Rare; tense vesicles/bullae that rupture and leave erosions | Fluid accumulates at the damaged interface, lifting epithelium off its base | Rare. Must be distinguished from autoimmune blistering disease (pemphigus vulgaris, mucous membrane pemphigoid) — **DIF is the discriminator** |

### 🔑 Clinical pearls worth stating explicitly

1. **Subtypes coexist and interconvert in the same mouth.** A patient may have reticular striae on the left cheek, an atrophic patch on the gingiva, and an erosion on the right buccal mucosa — simultaneously. Don't think of these as boxes; think of them as **weather across a landscape**.
2. **The peripheral striae are your diagnostic lifeline.** When you see a painful oral erosion, *look at its edges*. If there are **radiating white striae**, you are almost certainly dealing with erosive LP rather than aphthous ulceration, trauma, or pemphigus. This is the single most practically useful sign in the topic.
3. **Pain tracks epithelial thinness, not lesion "severity" in the visual sense.** The white lesions (which look alarming) are painless; the red lesions (which look trivial) are agonising. Students consistently get this backwards.

## Part 6 — The Reddish / Dysplastic Variant, and the Cancer Question

This deserves its own section, because it's the clinically most consequential part of mucosal LP.

### The variant

There is a described **reddish (erythroplakia-like) dysplastic variant** of oral LP characterised by:
- **Increased epithelial cell proliferation** compared with the reticular form
- **Moderate-to-severe epithelial dysplasia** on histology
- Consequently, the **highest malignant potential**

### The mechanism of malignant transformation

Here is the logic chain, and please learn it as a chain rather than a fact:

**Chronic T-cell attack → continuous keratinocyte death → continuous compensatory proliferation to replace losses → decades of accelerated cell division in an inflamed microenvironment (reactive oxygen species, cytokines, NF-κB activation, p53 dysregulation) → accumulated DNA replication errors → dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → invasive squamous cell carcinoma.**

The analogy I like: a **photocopier making copies of copies, under constant pressure to keep up, for thirty years.** Every generation carries forward small errors. Eventually one error escapes the quality-control checkpoint.

### The numbers

- Overall malignant transformation rate for oral LP: approximately **1–2%** over 5–10 years of follow-up (Rook's, Bolognia).
- The **erosive and atrophic** forms carry the greatest risk — precisely the forms where destruction and turnover are highest.
- The **WHO classifies oral lichen planus as an "oral potentially malignant disorder" (OPMD).**

### What this means practically

- **Long-term surveillance is mandatory**, not optional — periodic clinical review, with **biopsy of any lesion that changes**: becomes indurated, develops a nodule, ulcerates without provocation, fails to heal, or becomes fixed and non-resolving.
- **Tobacco and alcohol are multiplicative co-carcinogens** here. Cessation advice is not a soft lifestyle suggestion; it's cancer prevention.
- In **darkly pigmented individuals**, mucosal LP frequently shows **striking post-inflammatory pigmentation** — sometimes dramatic brown-black mottling. ⚠️ Do not mistake this for melanoma, and equally, do not let the pigment obscure an underlying ulcer. Pigmentation reflects melanin incontinence after basal layer injury (melanocytes sit in the basal layer, so when it's destroyed, their pigment spills into the connective tissue and is taken up by macrophages — **melanophages**).

## Part 7 — Complications Beyond the Mouth

| Complication | Mechanism | Clinical presentation |
|---|---|---|
| **Oesophageal LP → strictures** | Circumferential erosion heals by fibrosis; scar tissue contracts and narrows the lumen | **Progressive dysphagia**, food impaction. Typically **proximal** oesophagus. Requires endoscopy ± dilatation. Predominantly middle-aged women |
| **Laryngeal LP** | Same process on vocal cords and supraglottis | **Hoarseness**, odynophagia; rarely airway compromise |
| **Squamous cell carcinoma** | As above | New non-healing ulcer, induration, nodule, fixation |
| **Symptomatic burden** | Exposed nerve endings + loss of barrier | **Burning, irritation, dysphagia**, inability to tolerate spicy/hot/acidic food, weight loss, secondary depression |

**The unifying principle for all the scarring complications:** any tube or fold lined by squamous epithelium, when it erodes and heals by fibrosis, will either **narrow (stricture)** or **stick together (adhesion/synechia)**. This single idea explains oesophageal strictures, vaginal synechiae, phimosis, conjunctival symblepharon, and nail anonychia in LP. One mechanism, many organ-specific names.

## Part 8 — Differential Diagnosis: How to Reason Through a White Oral Lesion

Don't memorise this table — *use* it. The question you're always answering is: **"Is this abnormal keratin, abnormal organism, abnormal genetics, or abnormal drug reaction?"**

| Differential | How to tell it apart | Underlying logic |
|---|---|---|
| **Leukoplakia** | **Homogeneous** white patch; **no Wickham's striae**; typically unilateral/focal; higher dysplasia risk; strongly tobacco-linked | A true premalignant keratosis, not an inflammatory disease. Note: plaque-type LP and leukoplakia can be genuinely indistinguishable clinically — **biopsy settles it** |
| **Gingivitis** | Inflammatory, bleeds on probing, plaque-related, **no reticular pattern**; responds to oral hygiene alone | Bacterial/plaque-driven, not lymphocyte-driven. Contrast with LP's **desquamative gingivitis** — shiny, red, peeling, and hygiene-resistant |
| **Smoker's patches / nicotine stomatitis** | Tobacco history; **palatal predilection**; **resolves on cessation** | Thermal/chemical keratosis — remove the insult, remove the lesion. LP does not obey this |
| **Chronic candidiasis** | Pseudomembranes **scrape off** leaving a red base; **KOH/smear positive**; responds to antifungals | The white material is *fungus and debris*, not keratin — hence it wipes away. LP's white striae **cannot be scraped off.** This bedside test is diagnostic gold |
| **White sponge nevus** | **Autosomal dominant**; **bilateral, diffuse, symmetrical**; **onset in childhood**; thick spongy white mucosa; **asymptomatic, lifelong, non-progressive** | A genetic keratin defect (*KRT4/KRT13*) — the epithelium is built abnormally from birth. No inflammation, no treatment needed, no cancer risk |
| **Lichenoid drug eruption / oral lichenoid contact reaction** | Lesion sits **directly adjacent to dental amalgam** or follows a drug; **resolves on removing the trigger**; **eosinophils and deeper, more diffuse infiltrate** on histology | A hypersensitivity reaction *mimicking* LP. The key is topographical correlation and reversibility. Culprit drugs: NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, antimalarials, thiazides, gold, penicillamine |

Add two more for safety in an exam: **pemphigus vulgaris and mucous membrane pemphigoid** (for erosive/bullous LP — separated by **direct immunofluorescence**), and **lupus erythematosus** (which can produce oral striae, but they're usually more radiating from a central atrophic area with telangiectasia, and DIF/serology differ).

## Part 9 — Genital Lichen Planus

Mucosal LP below the waist follows all the same rules, but the consequences are different because the anatomy is different — **folds and canals scar shut**.

### In men

- Typically affects **young men**
- Sites: **glans penis, prepuce, penile shaft, scrotum**
- Presentation spans the full spectrum: from **subtle reticulate papules** (often an annular pattern on the glans) to **severe erosive disease**
- **Complications:** erosive balanitis, **phimosis** from preputial scarring, meatal stenosis, adhesion of glans to prepuce
- The male counterpart of the female syndrome below is the **peno-gingival syndrome** — erosive LP of the glans plus gingiva

### In women — Vulval LP

This is the more severe and more disabling presentation, and it is **chronically underdiagnosed**.

Clinical features:
- **Erosive vulvitis** — raw, painful, glazed red erosions, often periclitoral and at the introitus, with **white reticulate striae at the borders** (again, look at the edges!)
- **Scarring and loss of normal vulvar architecture** — resorption of the labia minora, burying of the clitoris under fused clitoral hood, **narrowing of the introitus**
- **Vaginal involvement**: erosions, desquamative vaginitis, serosanguineous discharge, and — most consequentially — **synechiae (adhesions) that can obliterate the vaginal canal entirely**
- **Symptoms: dyspareunia (often apareunia) and pruritus**, burning, post-coital bleeding

**The diagnostic difficulty — and why it's examinable:**
Vulval LP frequently **coexists with, or is difficult to distinguish from, vulval lichen sclerosus**, and that patient may simultaneously have **oral lichenoid lesions**. Three overlapping entities, one patient. Distinguishing points: lichen sclerosus tends to produce **ivory-white, crinkled, "cigarette-paper" atrophic change** with a figure-of-eight perianal extension and does *not* typically involve the vagina; LP produces **erosions with violaceous/reticulate borders** and *does* involve the vagina. But overlap is real, and biopsy plus clinicopathological correlation is often required.

> ⭐ **High-yield eponym:** **Pelisse's vulvovaginal-gingival syndrome** (Pelisse *et al.*, 1982) — the classic **triad of erosive lichen planus affecting the vulva, vagina, and gingiva.** Recognising it matters because it tells you that a woman with erosive gingivitis needs a **genital examination**, and a woman with erosive vulvitis needs her **mouth examined**. Patients rarely volunteer both complaints to the same doctor; the syndrome exists to remind you to ask.

## Part 10 — Histopathology: Mucosal LP Is *Not* Just "Skin LP in the Mouth"

This is a favourite exam discriminator, and it becomes easy once you ask: **"How is mucosa structurally different from skin?"**

Answer: normal buccal mucosa is **non-keratinised (or parakeratinised), thinner, has no granular layer, and is permanently wet.** So when the same disease process hits it, the histology reads differently.

| Feature | Cutaneous LP | Mucosal LP | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Epithelial thickness** | Acanthotic (thickened) | Often **thinned-out/atrophic** | Mucosa has less reserve; destruction outpaces repair more easily |
| **Keratinisation** | **Orthokeratosis** (compact keratin, no retained nuclei) with **hypergranulosis** | **Parakeratosis** (retained nuclei in the keratin layer), often **without a granular layer** | Mucosa is normally non-keratinised; when forced to keratinise under inflammatory pressure, it does so **incompletely and hurriedly** — nuclei don't get fully broken down. Analogy: **bread baked too fast — the crust forms but the inside isn't finished** |
| **Metaplastic change** | N/A | Epithelium may **resemble epidermis** — i.e. it acquires genuine skin-like keratinisation | Chronic irritation drives **squamous metaplasia** toward a tougher, skin-like phenotype. The tissue is literally trying to become skin to protect itself |
| **Infiltrate** | Band-like, predominantly **lymphocytes** | Band-like, but **plasma cells often prominent** | Mucosa is an immunologically active, antigen-exposed surface with resident plasma cells (think IgA-secreting mucosal immunity) — so plasma cells join the party |
| **Colloid (Civatte) bodies** | Numerous | **Fewer** | The thinner, faster-turnover mucosal epithelium sheds apoptotic cells into the oral cavity rather than retaining them |
| **Saw-tooth rete ridges** | Classic | Less pronounced / often absent | Mucosa lacks the distinct rete-ridge architecture of epidermis |

⚠️ **Common misunderstanding:** students write "orthokeratosis" for oral LP because they learned it for skin LP. **Reverse it: skin = ortho, mucosa = para.** Mnemonic: **"Mucosa is moist, so its keratin stays immature — Mucosa = Para."**

Also remember: **direct immunofluorescence** in LP shows **shaggy fibrinogen deposition at the basement membrane zone** and colloid-body IgM — useful mainly to *exclude* pemphigus/pemphigoid in erosive cases, not to confirm LP.

## Part 11 — Management: Treating the Mechanism, Not Just the Lesion

Frame management around three goals: **(1) reduce the T-cell attack, (2) remove aggravating co-factors, (3) prevent and manage scarring and cancer.**

### A. Education and lifestyle — do not skip this in an exam answer

- **Educate about the chronic course.** Oral LP is typically a **chronic, relapsing–remitting disease lasting years to decades** (unlike cutaneous LP, which often self-resolves within 1–2 years). Cutaneous LP burns out; oral LP smoulders.
- **Set realistic expectations.** The goal is **symptom control and mucosal healing, not cure.** (This is the correct reading of the source's ambiguous phrase "direct remission" — you are *directing the patient's expectations* about remission, not promising immediate remission.) Patients who expect cure become non-adherent and demoralised.
- **Avoid tobacco and alcohol** — both aggravate inflammation and, critically, **multiply the SCC risk** in an already potentially malignant lesion.
- **Avoid spicy, acidic, hot, and rough foods/drinks** — citrus, tomato, chilli, carbonated drinks, crisps. Eroded mucosa has exposed nerve endings; these foods are direct chemical and mechanical irritants. Analogy: **lemon juice on a graze.**
- **Meticulous oral hygiene** — plaque worsens gingival LP, and a clean mouth reduces secondary candidiasis. Use a soft brush and **SLS-free toothpaste** (sodium lauryl sulphate is itself an irritant).
- **Professional dental care** — to identify sharp/rough restorations causing mechanical trauma, and to assess **amalgam restorations** adjacent to lesions (possible lichenoid contact reaction — replacement may be curative in those cases).

### B. Topical therapy — first line

| Agent | Rationale / notes |
|---|---|
| **Topical corticosteroids** (e.g. clobetasol/betamethasone in adhesive base, oral gels, mouthwashes) | **Mainstay first-line.** Suppress the T-cell infiltrate locally with minimal systemic exposure. Monitor for **secondary candidiasis** — consider prophylactic antifungal |
| **Intralesional triamcinolone acetonide** | For **localised, thick, or refractory** lesions — delivers a high local depot dose |
| **Topical calcineurin inhibitors — tacrolimus 0.1% ointment, pimecrolimus 1% cream** | Inhibit calcineurin → block NF-AT → prevent IL-2 transcription → **shut down T-cell activation at its source.** Extremely useful as **steroid-sparing agents**, especially for erosive disease and genital sites where steroid-induced atrophy is a real concern. ⚠️ Note these are a **distinct drug class** from steroids — never conflate them. Expect transient burning on application; counsel patients or they'll stop |
| **Topical retinoids — retinoic acid 0.05%, isotretinoin gel, tazarotene gel** | Normalise keratinocyte differentiation and turnover → best suited to **hyperkeratotic/plaque-type** lesions, where the problem is excess abnormal keratin rather than raw erosion. Note: the source lists these under "oral retinoids," but as *retinoic acid 0.05%, isotretinoin gel and tazarotene gel* these are **topical** preparations applied intraorally — the "oral" refers to route of application, not systemic dosing |

### C. Systemic therapy — for widespread, erosive, or refractory disease

| Agent | Rationale / notes |
|---|---|
| **Prednisolone soluble tablet mouthwash** — 5 mg dissolved in 15 mL water, **"swish and spit" three times daily for ~6 weeks** | A brilliant middle ground: delivers steroid to **every mucosal surface** including hard-to-reach areas, with **minimal systemic absorption because it's spat out, not swallowed.** Ideal for diffuse oral disease. Emphasise "spit, don't swallow" to patients |
| **Systemic corticosteroids** | For severe, acutely erosive, or multi-site disease. Effective but limited by toxicity — use as a **bridge** to a steroid-sparing agent, not as long-term therapy |
| **Acitretin** (~8-week course) | Systemic retinoid; modulates keratinocyte differentiation. Useful in hypertrophic/widespread disease. ⚠️ **Strongly teratogenic** — contraception for 3 years after stopping in women of childbearing potential; monitor lipids and liver function |
| **Methotrexate** | Antimetabolite/immunomodulator; good steroid-sparing agent for chronic erosive disease |
| **Ciclosporin** | Calcineurin inhibitor, systemically — same mechanism as topical tacrolimus but body-wide. Rapid onset; limited by nephrotoxicity and hypertension |
| **Apremilast** | PDE4 inhibitor → ↑ intracellular cAMP → ↓ TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23 → dampens the inflammatory cascade. A newer, better-tolerated option with emerging evidence |
| *(Worth mentioning)* **Hydroxychloroquine, mycophenolate mofetil, biologics** | Reported in refractory cases; evidence limited |

### D. Anogenital-specific management — the anti-scarring agenda

This is where LP management becomes **physical as well as pharmacological**, because you are racing against fibrosis.

- **Vaginal dilators** — used regularly to **maintain patency and prevent synechiae formation.** The principle: fibrous tissue contracts as it heals; if you hold the canal open mechanically during healing, it cannot seal shut. Analogy: **a splint on a healing joint to prevent contracture**, or **keeping a door wedged open while the paint dries.** This is prevention, and prevention is far easier than surgical correction.
- **Topical steroids/tacrolimus in appropriate vehicles** — ointments and vaginal suppositories/pessaries for internal disease.
- **Foreskin retraction exercises**, and **circumcision (removal of foreskin)** in men where phimosis or non-resolving preputial disease develops.
- **Surgery for adhesions** — but with a critical caveat: **operate only after complete resolution of active inflammation.** Cutting into actively inflamed lichenoid tissue invites **koebnerisation** (disease induced at sites of trauma) and re-scarring. Analogy: **you don't repave a road while it's still flooding.** Control the disease first, then reconstruct.
- **Dilatation for oesophageal strictures**, with concurrent medical suppression to prevent recurrence.
- **Address sexual function and psychological impact explicitly** — dyspareunia, apareunia, and genital disfigurement carry a heavy psychosocial burden that patients will not raise unless you do.

## Part 12 — Common Misunderstandings, Collected

Let me gather the traps in one place, because these are exactly what examiners probe:

1. **"Mucus membrane"** → it's **mucous** membrane. And **lichen sclerosus**, not sclerosis.
2. **"Mucosal LP is just a minor extension of skin LP."** Wrong — it's often the *only* manifestation (~15%), it's more chronic, more disabling, and it's the form with cancer risk.
3. **"The white lesions are the dangerous ones because they look worse."** Backwards. The **red and eroded** lesions hurt and carry the malignant risk; the white reticular ones are typically asymptomatic.
4. **"Oral LP histology = orthokeratosis + hypergranulosis."** No — that's *cutaneous*. Mucosal LP shows **parakeratosis**, often with **thinned epithelium** and **prominent plasma cells**, and **fewer colloid bodies**.
5. **"Cutaneous LP resolves in a year, so oral LP will too."** No. Oral and genital LP are **markedly more persistent** — plan for years of follow-up.
6. **"A white patch that won't scrape off could still be candidiasis."** No — **scrapeability is the discriminator.** Candidal pseudomembrane wipes off; LP's keratin does not.
7. **"If topical steroids stop working, escalate immunosuppression."** First **exclude superimposed candidiasis**, mechanical trauma from a sharp restoration, a lichenoid drug reaction, and — most importantly — **malignant transformation.** Failure to respond is a biopsy indication, not automatically a dose indication.
8. **"LP and lichenoid drug eruption are the same."** Clinically similar, but lichenoid reactions are **reversible on removing the trigger** (drug or amalgam), often unilateral/adjacent to the trigger, and show **eosinophils and a deeper infiltrate** histologically.
9. **"Treat the mouth, forget the rest."** Any patient with mucosal LP needs a **full mucocutaneous survey**: mouth, genitals, skin, nails, scalp — and a symptom enquiry about **dysphagia and hoarseness**. Remember Pelisse's syndrome.

## Part 13 — The Core Idea in One Picture

If you remember nothing else, remember this:

> **Lichen planus is a cytotoxic T-cell attack on the basal layer of stratified squamous epithelium. Wherever that epithelium exists — mouth, oesophagus, larynx, genitals, anus — the disease can occur, and *every* clinical feature is a downstream consequence of losing that basal layer.**
>
> **Where repair outpaces destruction → white lesions (reticular, papular, plaque) → painless.**
> **Where destruction outpaces repair → red and raw lesions (atrophic, erosive, bullous) → painful, scarring, and potentially malignant.**
>
> **Mucosal LP therefore matters for three reasons: it can be the *only* sign of the disease (~15%), it *scars functional structures* shut (oesophageal strictures, vaginal synechiae, phimosis), and it is a WHO-recognised *potentially malignant* disorder (~1–2% SCC over 5–10 years, highest in erosive and atrophic forms).**
>
> **So management has three arms: suppress the T cells (topical steroids → calcineurin inhibitors → systemic agents), remove co-factors (tobacco, alcohol, trauma, candida, amalgam, irritant foods), and mechanically preserve architecture while surveilling for cancer (dilators, timed surgery, lifelong review with biopsy of anything that changes).**

One mechanism. Six subtypes. Three imperatives. That's the whole topic.
