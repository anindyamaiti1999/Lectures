# Papillon–Lefèvre Syndrome: A Mini-Lecture

## Part 1 — The Big Picture: Why This Syndrome Deserves Your Attention

Let me start with a scene, because this condition is one of those rare instances where dermatology and dentistry collide in a way that is almost impossible to forget once you've seen it.

Imagine a four-year-old child brought to clinic. The parents are worried about two things that seem completely unrelated. First, the child's palms and soles are thick, yellowish, and scaly — and unlike ordinary callus, the thickening doesn't stop politely at the edge of the palm. It *spills over* onto the back of the hands and the tops of the feet, as though the keratin were liquid that overflowed its container. Second — and this is the part that makes the parents frantic — the child's gums are swollen, red, and bleeding, the breath is foul, and the baby teeth are becoming loose. Within a year, those teeth will fall out. The permanent teeth will erupt on schedule, look perfectly normal, and then be destroyed all over again, so that by the teenage years this child may be entirely edentulous.

That combination — **transgradient palmoplantar keratoderma plus aggressive, destructive periodontitis in a child** — is Papillon–Lefèvre syndrome. Its alternative name, **keratoderma periodontitis**, is really just the two cardinal features stapled together, and it's arguably the more honest name because it tells you what you're looking for.

Now, why does this matter beyond being a board question? Three reasons:

1. **It is treatable in a time-sensitive way.** If you diagnose it early — specifically *before the permanent teeth erupt* — retinoid therapy can preserve the dentition. Diagnose it late, and you are managing dentures in an adolescent. Few genodermatoses offer such a clear reward for prompt recognition.
2. **It teaches you a beautiful piece of immunology.** The syndrome is a natural experiment in what happens when neutrophils lose their ability to *arm their own weapons*.
3. **It reframes how you think about periodontitis.** Severe periodontal disease in a young child is not "poor brushing." It is a red flag for systemic immune dysfunction, and PLS sits near the top of that differential.

And where does it fit in the broader taxonomy? PLS belongs to the **disorders of keratinization**, and more specifically to the **palmoplantar keratodermas (PPKs)**. Within the PPKs, it is classified as a **syndromic, transgradient, autosomal recessive** form. Holding those three adjectives in mind is half the diagnosis.

## Part 2 — The Genetics: One Gene, One Enzyme, Two Organ Systems

### The mutation

PLS is caused by **homozygous loss-of-function mutations in the *CTSC* gene**, which encodes the enzyme **cathepsin C**, also known historically as **dipeptidyl peptidase I (DPP-I)**.

Two words in that sentence do a lot of work:

- **Homozygous** — you need two broken copies. This is classic **autosomal recessive** inheritance, which explains why PLS clusters in populations with high rates of **consanguinity**, why parents are typically unaffected carriers, and why the recurrence risk for siblings is 25%. Always ask about parental consanguinity and draw a pedigree.
- **Loss-of-function** — the enzyme isn't merely altered, it's essentially absent or inactive. There is no dominant-negative trickery here; it's a straightforward deficiency state.

### What cathepsin C actually is — and the key analogy

Here is the concept that unlocks everything else.

Cathepsin C is a **lysosomal cysteine protease**. It has two jobs: general **protein degradation** inside lysosomes, and — far more importantly for this syndrome — **proenzyme activation**. It is the enzyme that snips the inhibitory pro-peptide off the front of several other proteases, converting them from inert precursors into active enzymes.

**The analogy: cathepsin C is the armourer.**

Think of a neutrophil as a soldier who arrives at the battlefield carrying a bag of weapons. But every weapon in that bag ships with a **safety pin** still in place — a short pro-peptide that keeps it inactive so it doesn't destroy the cell that made it. Cathepsin C is the armourer standing at the door of the armoury, pulling the pins as the weapons are loaded.

Remove the armourer, and the soldier still deploys. He still marches to the site of infection. He still carries his bag. But **every weapon in the bag is still pinned and useless.**

Which weapons? All of them are **serine proteases**, and they are worth memorising as a set:

| Pro-enzyme activated by cathepsin C | Cell of origin | Function when active |
|---|---|---|
| **Neutrophil elastase** | Neutrophil | Degrades bacterial proteins, extracellular matrix; key to intracellular killing and NET formation |
| **Cathepsin G** | Neutrophil | Direct microbicidal activity, chemotaxis modulation |
| **Proteinase 3** | Neutrophil | Antimicrobial, also the ANCA antigen in GPA |
| **Granzymes A and B** | Cytotoxic T lymphocytes, NK cells | Induce apoptosis in infected or transformed target cells |

Notice the shape of that list. The first three are **neutrophil bactericidal machinery**. The last two are **lymphocyte cytotoxic machinery**. So a single enzyme deficiency simultaneously cripples innate antibacterial killing *and* cell-mediated cytotoxic surveillance. Hold that thought — it will explain both the infections and the cancer risk.

### Where cathepsin C is expressed

Cathepsin C is expressed in:

- **Immune cells** — neutrophils, macrophages, mast cells, cytotoxic T lymphocytes (these are the clinically decisive sites)
- **Lungs**
- **Kidneys**
- **Epithelial tissues**, including the palmoplantar epidermis and the gingival epithelium

That last point matters for a common student question: *if this is an immune enzyme, why is there a skin phenotype?* Because cathepsin C isn't exclusively immune. It is also expressed in high-turnover epithelia, where it participates in the proteolytic processing that accompanies terminal keratinocyte differentiation and orderly desquamation. When that processing fails, corneocytes are not shed properly, they pile up, and you get **hyperkeratosis** — worst at sites of mechanical stress, hence the palms and soles. So the gene explains both halves of the syndrome; it isn't a coincidence that one mutation causes both.

## Part 3 — Pathogenesis: Following the Causal Chains

Let's trace cause → effect → outcome through each affected system. This is where most students lose marks, because they memorise the features without the linking logic.

### Chain 1: The immune consequence

***CTSC* mutation → absent cathepsin C → serine proteases remain as inactive pro-enzymes → neutrophils cannot kill ingested bacteria efficiently → recurrent pyogenic infection.**

A few important refinements to this chain, because the defect is broader than "neutrophils don't work":

- **Impaired phagocytic killing.** Neutrophils can still chemotax and engulf, but intracellular degradation of the organism is defective. The bug gets swallowed and survives. This is a *killing* defect, not a *recruitment* defect — which is why PLS differs from leukocyte adhesion deficiency.
- **High oxidative stress markers and mitochondrial dysfunction.** Frustrated, ineffective neutrophils keep generating reactive oxygen species without resolving the infection. The result is a smouldering, tissue-damaging inflammatory state in which the *host* response causes much of the destruction. This is critical for understanding the periodontium: much of the bone loss is not bacterial digestion of bone but **collateral damage from dysregulated inflammation**.
- **Impaired NK cell cytotoxicity.** Because granzymes A and B are never activated, NK cells and CTLs can dock onto a target and deliver their granules — but the granules are inert. The kill signal never fires.

### Chain 2: The dental consequence

**Defective neutrophil killing at the gingival margin → failure to control colonising organisms → overgrowth of *Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans* → gingivitis → aggressive periodontitis → deep periodontal abscesses → destruction of periodontal ligament and alveolar bone → tooth exfoliation.**

Why the mouth, of all places? Because the gingival sulcus is the single most biologically hostile epithelial frontier in the body — a permanently breached barrier, bathed in a dense polymicrobial biofilm, held in check *minute by minute* by a continuous stream of neutrophils crawling out of the junctional epithelium. There is no organ more dependent on functional neutrophils for its structural integrity. So when neutrophils fail, the periodontium is the first thing to dissolve.

**A note on the organism.** The classic pathogen is a gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic rod. You will see it written both ways in textbooks:
- Older name: ***Actinobacillus actinomycetemcomitans***
- Current name: ***Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans*** (reclassified in 2006 by Nørskov-Lauritsen & Kilian)

Same organism. Use the modern name, but recognise the old one. It is also the signature pathogen of localised aggressive periodontitis generally, and it produces a **leukotoxin** that kills neutrophils — so in PLS you have a double insult: neutrophils that can't kill, facing a bacterium that kills neutrophils.

### Chain 3: The cancer consequence

***CTSC* loss → granzyme A/B never activated → cytotoxic T cell and NK cell killing impaired → loss of immune surveillance against transformed cells → reported increased risk of melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma.**

**Be appropriately cautious here.** This association is described in the literature and is mechanistically plausible, but it rests largely on case reports and is a **minor and debated** association. It should not displace periodontitis and pyoderma as the dominant clinical concerns. Mention it as a recognised risk, not as a defining feature.

## Part 4 — Clinical Features: The Detailed Walkthrough

### 4a. The palmoplantar keratoderma

**Transgradient palmoplantar keratoderma.** This single word, *transgradient*, is worth a paragraph.

"Transgradient" (also called "transgressive") means the keratoderma **crosses the boundary** of the palm or sole and extends onto the dorsal surfaces of the hands, feet, wrists, and ankles. Compare this with **non-transgradient** keratodermas, which stop at a sharp margin — often with an erythematous rim — precisely at the palmoplantar border.

*The analogy:* imagine spilling thick paint onto the palm. A non-transgradient PPK behaves as if there were a raised lip around the palm, containing the spill neatly. A transgradient PPK has no such lip — the paint runs over the edges and down the back of the hand.

Why does this matter clinically? Because transgradience is one of your **fastest triage tools** in the PPK clinic. It immediately narrows a long differential toward a handful of conditions: PLS, Haim–Munk, Greither syndrome, Olmsted syndrome, mal de Meleda, and Vohwinkel syndrome, among others.

**Punctiform accentuation along the palmoplantar creases.** Layered on top of the diffuse thickening, you see discrete dot-like or punctate emphasis of keratin *following the flexural creases* of the palms and soles. This is a lovely, specific morphological clue — the keratin is not uniformly distributed but is exaggerated where mechanical folding concentrates stress. Look for it on close inspection with good side-lighting.

**Timing.** The keratoderma typically appears in the first one to four years of life, often around the same time or shortly before the gingival changes — so the two hallmark features tend to announce themselves together in the toddler years.

### 4b. Skin beyond the palms and soles

The disease is not confined to acral skin. Two patterns are described:

**Psoriasiform plaques** over the **Achilles tendon, knees, and elbows.** These are well-demarcated, erythematous, scaly plaques at sites of pressure and friction. They can be genuinely misleading — a child with scaly plaques on knees and elbows is very easily labelled "psoriasis," and if you don't examine the palms, soles, and *gums*, you will miss the diagnosis. This is one of the commonest diagnostic traps in PLS.

**Pseudoainhum** — constricting fibrous bands around digits, accompanied by **erythematous plaques over the knees, elbows, interphalangeal joints (IPJ), and dorsum of the feet.**

Let me unpack "pseudoainhum," because it's a term that sounds intimidating and is actually straightforward. **Ainhum** is a spontaneous, idiopathic constricting band that forms around a toe (classically the fifth toe) and slowly saws through it until the digit auto-amputates. The word derives from a Nagô/Yoruba root meaning "to saw" or "to cut" — which is exactly what it looks like. **Pseudoainhum** is the same morphology occurring *secondary to another disease* — a keratotic, fibrous band that encircles a digit like a tightening ring, progressively strangling its blood supply until the distal part is lost. Both "pseudoainhum" and "pseudo-ainhum" are acceptable spellings.

**An important caveat, stated plainly:** pseudoainhum is an **uncommon and variable** feature of PLS. It is *not* part of the classical triad. It is far more characteristically associated with **Vohwinkel syndrome** (keratoderma hereditaria mutilans), where the starfish-shaped keratoses and constricting bands are cardinal. Include pseudoainhum in a comprehensive answer on PLS — it is described — but flag it as a minor feature, and if a question gives you pseudoainhum as the *leading* clue, think Vohwinkel first.

### 4c. Hyperhidrosis and odour

**Hyperhidrosis, particularly of the soles**, is common, and it produces a characteristic **fetid (foul) odour**.

The mechanism is simple and satisfying. Thick, macerated keratin plus abundant sweat plus an occlusive shoe creates a warm, wet, protein-rich culture medium. Resident bacteria — especially corynebacteria and micrococci — metabolise the keratin and sweat components, releasing volatile short-chain fatty acids and sulphur compounds. That is the smell. It also predisposes to **secondary bacterial and fungal (dermatophyte) superinfection**, which in an immunocompromised child is not trivial.

Practical consequence: managing the hyperhidrosis and maceration is genuine therapy, not cosmetic fussing. It reduces both odour and secondary infection.

### 4d. The dental features — the diagnostic centrepiece

This is where PLS becomes unmistakable, and the timeline is the examinable detail.

- **Gingivitis and periodontitis**, both **aggressive and early in onset.** The gums become boggy, erythematous, oedematous, and bleed on minimal contact. This begins essentially as soon as the deciduous teeth have erupted.
- **Loss of the deciduous (primary) teeth by around 5 years of age.** The teeth themselves are structurally *normal* — this is critical. The enamel and dentine are fine. What is destroyed is the **supporting apparatus**: periodontal ligament and alveolar bone. The teeth are not decayed out; they are *undermined* out.
- Once the deciduous teeth are lost, the **gingiva typically returns to a near-normal appearance** — a striking and diagnostically useful remission.
- Then the **permanent teeth erupt**, the biofilm re-establishes, and the identical destructive process repeats, so the patient is frequently **edentulous by the teenage years**.
- **Deep periodontal abscesses** caused by **gram-negative bacteria**, principally *A. actinomycetemcomitans*.

*The analogy for the whole dental picture:* the teeth are perfectly sound fence-posts, but the soil they are set in is being continuously washed away. Replacing the posts (permanent teeth) does nothing, because the soil is still eroding.

That cyclical pattern — destruction with deciduous teeth, quiescence in between, destruction again with permanent teeth — is one of the most characteristic natural histories in all of paediatric dentistry, and it is precisely the window in which treatment can intervene.

### 4e. Systemic and pyogenic infections

**Recurrent pyogenic infections of the skin and internal organs.**

- **Cutaneous:** recurrent furuncles, abscesses, pyoderma, cellulitis.
- **Visceral:** the literature documents **pyogenic liver abscesses** and **brain abscesses**, among others.

These reflect the same neutrophil killing defect described above, now expressed outside the mouth. Clinically, this means a child with PLS who presents with fever and abdominal pain or neurological signs deserves a low threshold for imaging. Don't anchor on the skin and teeth and miss a liver or brain abscess.

## Part 5 — Haim–Munk Syndrome: The Allelic Cousin

Haim–Munk syndrome is best understood not as a separate disease but as a **variant on the same genetic theme**. It is caused by mutations in the **same *CTSC* gene**, which makes it an **allelic variant** of PLS — the two conditions differ in which specific mutation is present and, presumably, in the residual functional consequences.

It was originally described by **Haim and Munk in 1965** in the **Cochin Jewish community of Kerala, South India** — a small, genetically isolated population with high rates of consanguinity, exactly the demographic setting in which recessive founder mutations become visible.

**Shared with PLS:** transgradient palmoplantar keratoderma, aggressive periodontitis, recurrent pyogenic infection.

**Additional, distinguishing triad:**

| Feature | What it means, in plain terms |
|---|---|
| **Onychogryphosis** | Nails become grossly thickened, opaque, and curved — the classic description is a **ram's horn** or an **oyster shell**. |
| **Arachnodactyly** | Abnormally long, slender, "spider-like" fingers (from Greek *arachne*, spider). |
| **Acro-osteolysis** | Progressive **resorption of the distal phalanges** — the bone at the fingertips literally dissolves, producing shortened, tapered digits on examination and a characteristic "pencilled" or absent terminal phalanx on X-ray. |

**How to keep them straight:** think *PLS + bones and nails = Haim–Munk*. If you are given transgradient PPK and periodontitis, and then the question adds ram's-horn nails, spidery fingers, or dissolving fingertips, shift your answer to Haim–Munk.

**Acro-osteolysis as a standalone sign** is worth knowing because it has its own differential: **Haim–Munk syndrome, progeria, vinyl chloride disease (occupational), hyperparathyroidism, pyknodysostosis, and scleroderma.** Encountering acro-osteolysis should trigger that list.

## Part 6 — Investigations: Histopathology and Electron Microscopy

Let me be candid about something important: **PLS is a clinical diagnosis**, confirmed genetically. The histology is *supportive and non-specific*; it will not distinguish PLS from many other keratodermas. Know it for exams and for completeness, but do not imagine a biopsy makes the diagnosis.

### Light microscopy (HPE)

| Finding | What it is and why it's there |
|---|---|
| **Hyperkeratosis** | A thickened stratum corneum — the direct histological correlate of the clinically palpable keratoderma, caused by failed/retarded desquamation. |
| **Focal irregular parakeratosis** | Patchy areas where corneocytes **retain their nuclei** instead of losing them during terminal differentiation. Normal cornification is anuclear ("orthokeratosis"); retained nuclei signal **accelerated or disordered maturation**, i.e., cells being pushed up before they finish differentiating. |
| **Moderate perivascular infiltration** | Inflammatory cells cuffing the dermal blood vessels — evidence that PLS skin is not merely thick but **genuinely inflamed**, consistent with the underlying immune dysregulation. |

### Electron microscopy

Electron microscopy is where the *mechanism* becomes visible, and each finding maps neatly onto a defect in terminal differentiation.

| EM finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Reduced tonofilaments** | Tonofilaments are bundled keratin intermediate filaments — the internal steel cables giving keratinocytes their tensile strength and anchoring desmosomes. Fewer tonofilaments = a mechanically weaker, structurally abnormal cornified envelope. |
| **Lipid-like vacuoles in corneocytes and granulocytes** | Abnormal lipid handling. In normal cornification, lamellar bodies discharge their lipid into the intercellular space to build the permeability barrier — the "mortar" between corneocyte "bricks." Here, lipid appears to be **retained inside the cells as vacuoles** instead of being exported. That means a defective barrier *and* impaired desquamation, because the lipid-processing enzymes that dissolve corneodesmosomes can't do their job. Strikingly, these vacuoles appear in **granulocytes too** — reminding you this is a systemic enzyme defect, not a skin-limited one. |
| **Irregular keratohyalin granules** | Keratohyalin granules in the granular layer contain **profilaggrin**, the precursor of filaggrin, which aggregates keratin filaments into dense bundles during cornification. Irregular granules indicate disrupted profilaggrin processing — and this makes perfect mechanistic sense in a **protease-deficiency** disease, since profilaggrin must be proteolytically cleaved into functional filaggrin. |

Notice how coherent this is: a lysosomal protease is missing, so proteolytic processing steps across the epidermis — filaggrin maturation, lipid processing, corneodesmosome dissolution — all go wrong together. The EM findings are not a random list; they are three views of one failure.

## Part 7 — Differential Diagnosis

The formal differentials given are:

- **Hypotrichosis–osteolysis syndrome**
- **Periodontitis–PPK syndrome (HOPP)** — the **H**ypotrichosis–**O**steolysis–**P**eriodontitis–**P**almoplantar keratoderma complex

Both of these are recognised as overlapping with or closely related to the PLS spectrum, and both present with the same core problem: **keratoderma plus early periodontal destruction**. The differentiating features are the *additional* components — **hypotrichosis** (sparse hair) and **osteolysis** (bone resorption).

In real practice, your working differential should also include:

- **Haim–Munk syndrome** — as discussed, the same gene plus nails, digits, and bone.
- **Other transgradient PPKs** — **mal de Meleda** (transgradient, with a characteristic glove-and-stocking pattern and perioral erythema, *but no periodontitis*), **Olmsted syndrome** (mutilating PPK with **periorificial keratotic plaques**), **Greither syndrome**, **Vohwinkel syndrome** (honeycomb PPK, **starfish keratoses**, pseudoainhum).
- **Other causes of severe childhood periodontitis** — here the key alternatives are immunodeficiencies: **leukocyte adhesion deficiency**, **Chédiak–Higashi syndrome**, **chronic/cyclic neutropenia**, **hypophosphatasia**, and **Langerhans cell histiocytosis**. Asking "is there a keratoderma?" cleanly separates PLS from all of these.

**The single most useful discriminating question** in a transgradient PPK: *Have the teeth been affected?* If yes → PLS or Haim–Munk. If no → mal de Meleda, Greither, and the rest.

## Part 8 — Treatment: Where Timing Is Everything

### 1. Systemic retinoids — the cornerstone

**Oral retinoids (acitretin or isotretinoin)** are the mainstay, and they serve three distinct purposes:

**(a) For the keratoderma.** Retinoids normalise keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation, thin the stratum corneum, and restore more orderly desquamation. Clinically this means softer, less fissured, less painful palms and soles, and improved mobility and hand function.

**(b) For the periodontal disease — and this is the headline.** If systemic retinoid therapy is initiated **before the eruption of the permanent teeth**, it may **preserve normal dentition**. This is the most important single therapeutic fact about PLS.

How does a skin drug save teeth? Through two plausible, complementary mechanisms:
- It **normalises keratinisation of the gingival and junctional epithelium**, restoring the integrity of that critical barrier at the gingival sulcus and making it harder for pathogens to invade the periodontium.
- It **modulates the inflammatory response** within the periodontium, damping the dysregulated, tissue-destructive inflammation that drives alveolar bone loss.

The clinical corollary is stark: **early diagnosis is not academic — it is the treatment.** A child diagnosed at age three and started on acitretin may keep their adult teeth. A child diagnosed at fourteen has already lost them irreversibly. This is why the dermatologist who notices a toddler's thick palms and asks to look at the gums is doing something genuinely consequential.

**(c) For prevention of autoamputation.** Where **pseudoainhum** — those constricting fibrous bands — has developed, retinoids can soften and reduce the keratotic band, relieving the strangulating pressure and **preventing autoamputation of the digit**. Left untreated, the band tightens until the distal digit is lost.

*Practical caveats you should always state:* retinoids are potent **teratogens**, demanding rigorous contraception counselling in females of childbearing potential (with acitretin's famously long post-treatment abstinence requirement due to etretinate re-esterification). In children, monitor for **premature epiphyseal closure and other skeletal toxicity** with long-term use, plus the routine concerns of hyperlipidaemia, hepatotoxicity, mucocutaneous dryness, and pseudotumour cerebri if combined with tetracyclines.

### 2. Elective extraction of affected teeth

**Deliberately extracting severely affected teeth** to **prevent excessive alveolar bone resorption.**

This feels counterintuitive — why remove a tooth you're trying to save? The logic is one of damage control. A tooth with advanced periodontal destruction functions as a **permanent, non-clearable reservoir of pathogenic biofilm**, sustaining the inflammatory process that eats alveolar bone. Since alveolar bone loss is **progressive and irreversible**, and since an adequate alveolar ridge is essential for any future prosthesis or implant, removing the infected focus early **preserves bone for the future** even though it sacrifices that particular tooth.

*The analogy:* you demolish a burning building to create a firebreak — not because the building was worthless, but because letting it burn will take the whole street.

### 3. Systemic antibiotics

For the **recurrent pyogenic infections**, both cutaneous and visceral. In periodontal management specifically, targeted antibiotic therapy against *A. actinomycetemcomitans* — commonly regimens including **amoxicillin plus metronidazole**, or **tetracyclines** in older children — is used alongside mechanical debridement. Aggressive treatment of skin abscesses and a high index of suspicion for deep-seated abscesses (liver, brain) are essential.

### 4. Supportive and coordinated care

- **Topical keratolytics and emollients** — urea, salicylic acid, lactic acid — plus mechanical paring for symptomatic keratoderma.
- **Management of hyperhidrosis and maceration**, which reduces both odour and secondary infection.
- **Treatment of secondary bacterial and dermatophyte superinfection.**
- **Meticulous, lifelong oral hygiene** with frequent professional scaling and periodontal review.
- **Genetic counselling** — recurrence risk 25% for siblings, discussion of consanguinity, and *CTSC* testing for confirmation and family screening.
- **Long-term skin surveillance** for melanoma and SCC, given the reported (if debated) malignancy risk.

**The overarching principle: this is a shared-care disease.** Dermatological and dental management must be *coordinated*. The dermatologist controls the retinoid; the periodontist controls the biofilm; neither succeeds alone. If you take one management message away, let it be that a PLS diagnosis should generate an immediate referral in the other direction.

## Part 9 — Common Misunderstandings, Addressed Directly

Let me pre-empt the errors I most often see.

**"The teeth are abnormal."** No. The teeth are **structurally normal**. Enamel and dentine are fine. What fails is the **supporting periodontium** — ligament and alveolar bone. This is destruction *around* the tooth, not *of* the tooth.

**"Cathepsin C directly digests bacteria."** No. Cathepsin C is the **activator** — the armourer. The actual killing is done by the serine proteases it unlocks (elastase, cathepsin G, proteinase 3). Losing the activator disarms all of them at once, which is why the phenotype is so severe.

**"PLS is a skin disease with dental complications."** Misleading framing. It is a **single systemic enzyme deficiency** with parallel epithelial and immune consequences. The keratoderma and the periodontitis are siblings, not parent and child.

**"Pseudoainhum is a hallmark of PLS."** No. It is **uncommon and variable** in PLS. It is a hallmark of **Vohwinkel syndrome**. Mention it for completeness; don't lead with it.

**"Haim–Munk is a different disease."** No. It is an **allelic variant** — same *CTSC* gene, different mutation, additional skeletal and nail features.

**"Retinoids can be started whenever."** Emphatically no. The therapeutic window closes with the eruption of the permanent teeth. **Before eruption = dentition may be saved. After destruction = irreversible.** Timing is the therapy.

**"Neutrophils don't reach the site."** Not the defect here. They arrive and they phagocytose; they simply cannot **kill** what they've engulfed. PLS is a *killing* defect, unlike leukocyte adhesion deficiency, which is a *recruitment* defect.

**"Melanoma and SCC are major features."** Overstated. This is a **minor, debated** association. The dominant clinical realities are periodontitis and pyoderma.

**"*Actinobacillus* and *Aggregatibacter* are different bugs."** Same organism, renamed in 2006. Use *Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans*; recognise the older name in older texts.

## Part 10 — The Core Idea in One Picture

If you retain nothing else, retain this chain:

> **One gene (*CTSC*) → one missing enzyme (cathepsin C, the "armourer") → two failures.**
>
> **Failure of epithelial protein processing** → filaggrin and lipid handling go wrong → retained corneocytes → **transgradient palmoplantar keratoderma** that overflows onto the dorsa, with punctate accentuation along the creases, psoriasiform plaques on knees/elbows/Achilles, and sweaty, malodorous soles.
>
> **Failure of serine protease activation in immune cells** → neutrophils that engulf but cannot kill, and NK/T cells whose granzymes never fire → at the body's most exposed epithelial frontier, the **gingival sulcus**, *Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans* runs unchecked → **aggressive periodontitis**, deep abscesses, and alveolar bone loss → **deciduous teeth lost by age 5, permanent teeth by the teens** → plus recurrent **pyodermas** of skin and viscera and, arguably, reduced tumour surveillance.
>
> **Add ram's-horn nails, spidery fingers, and dissolving fingertips, and you have renamed it Haim–Munk.**
>
> **And the whole reason to recognise it fast: start acitretin before the permanent teeth erupt, and you may save them.**

That is Papillon–Lefèvre syndrome — thick palms, lost teeth, recurrent boils, one missing enzyme, and a therapeutic window that closes in childhood.

| **Treatment** | **Systemic retinoids (acitretin/isotretinoin)** — before permanent tooth eruption to preserve dentition, and to prevent autoamputation from pseudoainhum; **systemic antibiotics**; **elective extraction** to spare alveolar bone; keratolytics/emollients; oral hygiene; genetic counselling |
| **Single most important point** | **Diagnose early — the therapeutic window for saving the permanent teeth closes at their eruption.** |
