# X‑Linked Ichthyosis (Steroid Sulfatase Deficiency)
### A mini‑lecture: from one missing enzyme to brown scales, a "dirty neck," and a labour that never starts

---

## 1. The Big Picture — Where does this disease sit?

Let me start by orienting you, because "ichthyosis" is a crowded family and students get lost in it.

The **ichthyoses** are disorders of **keratinization** — diseases in which the outermost layer of skin, the **stratum corneum**, is built wrongly or shed wrongly, so the skin accumulates visible scale (*ichthys* = fish, in Greek). Within that family there is a small group of **common, non‑syndromic, "retention" ichthyoses**, and the two heavyweights are:

1. **Ichthyosis vulgaris (IV)** — the commonest, due to *filaggrin* (FLG) loss, semidominant, linked to atopy.
2. **X‑linked ichthyosis (XLI)** — the **second commonest**, due to loss of a single **lipid‑processing enzyme**, **steroid sulfatase (STS)**.

Here is why XLI deserves a whole lecture of its own rather than a line in a table:

- It is one of the very few skin diseases whose **pathogenesis is a clean, single‑enzyme biochemistry problem** — you can literally draw it as a metabolic arrow and predict every clinical sign from it.
- It is the only ichthyosis with a **prenatal / obstetric signature**: the *mother* of an affected fetus shows abnormal hormone levels and may **fail to go into labour**. Dermatology reaching into the labour ward is rare and therefore heavily examined.
- Because the culprit gene sits in a **deletion‑prone region of the X chromosome (Xp22.31)**, the deletion often takes **neighbouring genes with it**, producing **contiguous gene deletion syndromes**. So the "simple ichthyosis" child may also have **anosmia, hypogonadism, ADHD, seizures, or developmental delay** — and you are the doctor who is supposed to notice.

Keep those three hooks in mind; everything else hangs off them.

## 2. Definition and Nomenclature

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **X‑linked ichthyosis (XLI)** | The standard modern name |
| **X‑linked recessive ichthyosis (XRI)** | Emphasises inheritance |
| **Steroid sulfatase deficiency (STS deficiency)** | Emphasises the biochemical lesion |
| **Placental sulfatase deficiency** | The obstetricians' name for the same thing, seen from the mother's side |

All four names describe **one disease**: a **X‑linked recessive genodermatosis** caused by deficiency of the enzyme **steroid sulfatase**, encoded by the *STS* gene at **Xp22.31**.

**Epidemiology:**
- **1 in 2,000 – 9,500 male births** (commonly quoted as ~1:2000–1:6000; it is genuinely common).
- **Affects males essentially exclusively.**
- **Females are asymptomatic carriers** — clinically normal skin.

---

### A subtlety worth understanding: *why* are carrier females truly normal?

This is where students blur two ideas. In most X‑linked recessive diseases, carrier females are normal because random X‑inactivation leaves enough cells expressing the good copy — and sometimes you *do* see patchy/mosaic disease (think Blaschko lines in X‑linked dominant conditions).

In XLI something more elegant happens: **the *STS* gene lies in a region of the X chromosome that largely *escapes* X‑inactivation.** So a carrier female expresses *STS* from **both** X chromosomes' territory, ends up with roughly half‑normal enzyme activity — and **half of normal STS activity is plenty**. Hence: no mosaic streaks, no scaling, no clue on the skin.

But — and here is the beautiful consistency — **half‑normal is *not* quite enough everywhere**. In the **cornea**, carriers accumulate just enough abnormal material to produce **asymptomatic corneal opacities** (see §7). And in the **placenta**, if the *fetus* is affected, the placenta is fetal tissue and therefore enzyme‑deficient, which is why the *mother's* hormones go wrong.

> **Teaching anchor:** carrier females are "silent on the skin, whispering in the cornea." That single sentence contains a lot of genetics.

## 3. Pathogenesis — The Core Chain, Step by Step

The source gives a vertical flowchart. Let me walk it with the *reasoning* filled in at each arrow, because that is what turns it from memorisation into understanding.

```
Deletion of STS gene at Xp22.31 (≈90% of cases; rest = point mutations)
          ↓
Steroid sulfatase (STS) enzyme deficiency
          ↓
Impaired hydrolysis of cholesterol sulfate and DHEAS
          ↓
Accumulation of cholesterol 3-sulfate in the epidermis
          ↓
(a) Inhibition of transglutaminase-1   (b) Inhibition of serine proteases (KLK5/KLK7)
          ↓                                        ↓
Defective cornified envelope              Corneodesmosomes not cleaved
          ↓                                        ↓
        Impaired epidermal barrier  +  RETENTION HYPERKERATOSIS
          ↓
Adherent, dark, polygonal scales
```

### Step 1 — The genetic lesion

*STS* sits at **Xp22.31**. Roughly **90% of patients have a complete deletion** of the gene; the remainder have point mutations or partial deletions. Why so many deletions? Because that stretch of the X chromosome is flanked by **low‑copy repeat sequences** (VCX/VCY‑related repeats) that misalign during meiosis and allow **non‑allelic homologous recombination** — the chromosome essentially "slips a stitch" and loops out the intervening DNA.

> **Cause → effect you must carry forward:** because the mechanism is *deletion*, not point mutation, the lesion can be **large**, and large lesions take **neighbours** with them. That single fact is the whole explanation for contiguous gene syndromes (§8). It also explains why **CGH / FISH / MLPA** are the diagnostic tests of choice — you are hunting a missing chunk of DNA, not a single letter change.

### Step 2 — What STS normally does

**Steroid sulfatase** is a **microsomal sulfatase** that removes sulfate groups from **sulfated steroids and sterols**. Its two substrates that matter to us:

1. **Cholesterol sulfate** → free **cholesterol** (this is the *skin* story)
2. **Dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS)** → free **DHEA** (this is the *placenta* story)

One enzyme, two organs, two completely different clinical pictures. This is why XLI is such a satisfying disease to teach.

### Step 3 — The skin story: cholesterol sulfate as "molecular glue"

Picture the stratum corneum with the classic **"bricks and mortar"** model: corneocytes are the bricks, intercellular lipid lamellae are the mortar. The mortar is a precise blend — roughly **ceramides : cholesterol : free fatty acids**. Corneocytes are also riveted together by protein studs called **corneodesmosomes**.

Now, **cholesterol sulfate** is a *normal, deliberate* ingredient in this system, present in small amounts, and it behaves like a **sticky, water‑loving glue** that helps hold cells together in the lower stratum corneum. Normally, as a corneocyte rises toward the surface, **STS progressively desulfates cholesterol sulfate → free cholesterol**. Two things happen at once:

- The glue dissolves → the cell can let go and flake off (**normal desquamation**).
- Free cholesterol is released into the mortar → the **barrier lipid mix stays correct**.

So STS is the **timer on the glue**. It is what allows the skin to shed on schedule.

In XLI, that timer is missing. Cholesterol sulfate accumulates to roughly **10–20 times normal** levels in the stratum corneum, and free cholesterol is relatively deficient. Now trace the consequences:

**(a) Corneodesmosomes are not dismantled.** Desquamation requires **kallikrein‑related serine proteases (KLK5, KLK7)** to chew through corneodesmosomal proteins (desmoglein‑1, corneodesmosin). **Cholesterol sulfate is a potent inhibitor of these serine proteases.** Inhibit the proteases and the rivets stay in. The corneocytes pile up not because the skin is *making* too much keratin, but because it cannot **let go** of what it has made.

> This is the definition of **retention hyperkeratosis** — as opposed to **proliferative hyperkeratosis** (as in epidermolytic ichthyosis or lamellar ichthyosis), where the basal layer is churning out cells too fast. XLI is a **shedding failure, not an overproduction problem.** Keep this straight: it explains the normal (not thickened) epidermal turnover, the normal-to-thick granular layer, and the *adherent* quality of the scale.

**(b) Transglutaminase‑1 is inhibited.** TG‑1 is the enzyme that cross‑links **involucrin, loricrin, small proline‑rich proteins** and attaches ceramides to build the **cornified cell envelope** — the tough, insoluble shell around each corneocyte and the scaffold on which the lipid lamellae are organised. Cholesterol sulfate inhibits TG‑1, so the envelope is assembled imperfectly.

**(c) Barrier lipids are miscomposed.** Sulfated cholesterol is charged and hydrophilic; free cholesterol is neutral and hydrophobic. Swap one for the other in the lamellar mortar and the **lipid bilayers stack imperfectly** → increased **transepidermal water loss (TEWL)** → the skin senses dryness → compensatory hyperkeratosis. A vicious cycle.

**Net clinical result:** a stratum corneum that is **too thick, too adherent, and too leaky** — exactly the adherent, plate‑like, "stuck‑on" scale you see.

**Analogy to lock in:** Imagine roof tiles held with a glue that is *designed to dissolve on a timer*. Remove the timer enzyme, and the tiles never release. New tiles keep arriving underneath at the normal rate, so the roof grows thicker and thicker with layers of tiles that should have fallen off months ago. That is XLI. (Contrast: in the *proliferative* ichthyoses, the factory is making tiles too fast.)

### Step 4 — Why are the scales *dark brown*?

This puzzles students, because "hyperkeratosis" usually looks white or grey. Two reasons:

1. **Retained melanosomes.** Electron microscopy shows corneocytes in XLI stuffed with **melanosomes** — pigment granules that were never degraded, because the cells that should have shed them are still sitting there. Thick, pigment‑laden corneocyte stacks look **brown**.
2. **Optical and environmental effects.** Thick, adherent, greasy scale traps dirt and oxidises, adding to the "unwashed" appearance.

This directly explains the most famous sign of the disease (§6).

## 4. The Placental Story — Dermatology in the Labour Ward

This is the highest‑yield, most conceptually pleasing part of XLI. Let me build the physiology first, then break it.

### Normal fetoplacental estriol synthesis (the "fetoplacental unit")

The placenta **cannot** make steroids from scratch — it lacks 17α‑hydroxylase. It is a **conversion factory** that depends on precursors delivered by the fetus:

```
Fetal adrenal cortex (fetal zone)
   → produces large amounts of DHEA-SULFATE (DHEAS)
        ↓ (travels in fetal blood to the placenta)
PLACENTAL STEROID SULFATASE removes the sulfate
        ↓
        DHEA (free)
        ↓ (3β-HSD, aromatase in placenta)
      ESTRONE → ESTRADIOL → (16α-hydroxylation) → ESTRIOL (E3)
        ↓
Estriol rises steeply through the third trimester in maternal blood and urine
```

Estriol is the **quintessential pregnancy oestrogen** — it is almost entirely of fetoplacental origin, which is why obstetricians historically used **maternal urinary estriol** as a readout of fetal wellbeing.

Notice the rate‑limiting step: **the placenta must desulfate DHEAS before it can do anything with it.** The sulfate group is a chemical "safety cap," and **STS is the only enzyme that removes it.**

### Now delete STS from the fetus

The **placenta is fetal tissue**. An affected male fetus therefore has an **STS‑deficient placenta**. Consequence chain:

1. Fetal adrenal DHEAS arrives at the placenta normally — in fact it **accumulates**, because it cannot be processed.
2. **No DHEA is liberated** → no substrate for aromatisation.
3. → **Estriol production collapses.** Maternal **serum and urinary estriol are low or absent**, and amniotic fluid estrogen is low.
4. → Unprocessed **sulfated steroids spill into the maternal circulation and appear in maternal urine** — the "**non‑hydrolysed sulfated steroids in maternal urine**" that the source lists as a diagnostic clue.
5. → **Failure of spontaneous initiation of labour.** Oestrogens are the great "pro‑labour" hormones: they upregulate **myometrial oxytocin receptors, gap junctions (connexin‑43), prostaglandin synthesis, and cervical ripening**. Strip out oestrogen and the uterus never becomes "electrically coupled" and contraction‑competent. The pregnancy therefore tends to go **post‑dates with a long, unripe cervix, failure to progress, and a requirement for induction or caesarean section.**

> **High‑yield clinical vignette to recognise instantly:** *"A primigravida at 41+ weeks has a persistently unripe cervix and failed induction. Her second‑trimester quadruple screen had shown a strikingly low unconjugated estriol with otherwise normal markers. The baby, a boy, develops generalised peeling and later dark scaling."* → **X‑linked ichthyosis / placental steroid sulfatase deficiency.**

### Don't over‑read a low estriol

Low maternal unconjugated estriol (uE3) on the triple/quad screen has a short differential, and you should be able to recite it:
- **Placental (steroid) sulfatase deficiency = XLI** ← our disease
- **Smith–Lemli–Opitz syndrome** (defective cholesterol synthesis → no substrate at all)
- **Fetal demise, anencephaly, adrenal hypoplasia** (no fetal adrenal DHEAS supply)
- **Maternal corticosteroid therapy** (suppresses the fetal adrenal)

So low uE3 is a *gateway* finding; the diagnosis is then confirmed molecularly or biochemically.

**A caution students often miss:** most affected male infants are *not* diagnosed prenatally, and the pregnancy/labour abnormalities are **variably present** — plenty of mothers labour normally. Low estriol is a **clue, not a rule.**

## 5. Genetic Counselling (the logic, not just the rules)

Because this is **X‑linked recessive**:

- **Carrier mother × normal father** → 50% of sons **affected**, 50% of daughters **carriers**; daughters clinically normal.
- **Affected father × normal mother** → **all daughters are obligate carriers** (he can only give them his single, mutant X); **no sons affected** (he gives sons a Y). This "**no male‑to‑male transmission, but all daughters carry**" pattern is the classic pedigree giveaway.
- Because ~90% are deletions, **carrier detection is easy and reliable** by FISH/CGH/MLPA on the mother — and **slit‑lamp examination of the mother's cornea** is a cheap bedside adjunct (~25% of carriers show opacities).
- Do not forget to ask about the **mother's own obstetric history** (prolonged pregnancy, failed induction) and to screen an affected boy's pedigree for **anosmia, infertility, and learning difficulties** — clues to a larger deletion in the family.

## 6. Clinical Features — Reading the Skin as a Story of Failed Shedding

### (a) The newborn

- **Mild erythroderma** with **generalised peeling**
- **Exfoliation of large, translucent scales**

Notice the mechanism: at birth, the baby has been sitting in amniotic fluid, so the stratum corneum is hydrated and the retained corneocytes come off in **big, translucent sheets** rather than brown plates. The pigment and adherence develop later.

> **Critical negative:** XLI newborns do **NOT** present as a **collodion baby** (that is the neonatal phenotype of **autosomal recessive congenital ichthyosis** — lamellar ichthyosis / CIE / Harlequin spectrum, and occasionally Netherton or Gaucher type 2). If the question stem says "collodion membrane," you are not in XLI territory. XLI's neonatal picture is *mild peeling*, and a fair number of affected boys are barely noticed at birth at all.

### (b) Infancy and childhood — the mature phenotype

The scale **darkens, thickens and becomes adherent** over the first weeks to months, producing the signature:

- **Large, polygonal, dark brown, tightly adherent scales** — often described as "**plate‑like**" or "**dirty**"
- **Symmetrical** distribution over **extremities (extensor surfaces especially), trunk (including abdomen), and neck**
- **Fine scaling of the scalp**, which **diminishes over time**

### (c) The famous sign: "**Dirty neck disease**"

Dark brown, adherent scaling concentrated on the **lateral and posterior neck**, giving the appearance of a child who has simply not washed. It is **virtually pathognomonic** and is *the* clinical vignette phrase for XLI. The mechanism ties back to §3: thick retained corneocytes + retained melanosomes + a flexural site prone to friction and grime.

**Distribution rules — what is spared and what is involved:**

| Site | XLI |
|---|---|
| **Palms and soles** | **Spared** (no keratoderma, no hyperlinearity) |
| **Face** | Largely **spared** — **except the preauricular area**, whose involvement is a distinctive pointer (in IV, the preauricular area is characteristically *spared*) |
| **Neck** | Prominently involved — "dirty neck" |
| **Flexures** | *Per this source:* **unaffected** |
| **Trunk/abdomen, extensor limbs** | Involved |

> **An honest nuance, because you will meet it in a viva:** different major texts handle the flexures differently. This source (and many exam keys) state **"flexures unaffected."** Other standard references emphasise that XLI **may involve the antecubital and popliteal fossae**, and use that as a point of contrast with ichthyosis vulgaris. The safest way to answer is to give the source's statement and then add the discriminators that *nobody* disputes: **XLI spares palms/soles and lacks palmar hyperlinearity and keratosis pilaris; it may involve the preauricular face; it does not improve with age.** Those three are rock solid.

### (d) Temporal behaviour — a small detail with big diagnostic power

- **Improves in summer / humid climates**, worsens in winter. Why? Retention hyperkeratosis is highly **hydration‑dependent**: humidity swells corneocytes, mechanically loosens the retained layers, and supports residual protease activity. Dry cold air does the opposite.
- **Does NOT improve with age.** This is the discriminator from **ichthyosis vulgaris**, which classically **improves around and after puberty**.

> **Exam trap:** "ichthyosis that improves at puberty" = **ichthyosis vulgaris**. "Ichthyosis that improves in summer but persists lifelong" = **XLI**. Students routinely swap these.

## 7. Extracutaneous Manifestations (isolated STS deletion)

These are the features caused by **STS loss itself**, before we even consider neighbouring genes.

### (a) Corneal opacities — the carrier‑detection tool

- **Asymptomatic**, **comma‑shaped or dot‑like** opacities on or near **Descemet's membrane** / deep posterior cornea.
- Seen on **slit‑lamp examination**; found in a substantial minority of affected males and in roughly **25% of heterozygous carrier females**.
- **Do not impair vision** — never present as "blurred vision."

**Mechanism (reason it back out):** the cornea is another cholesterol‑sulfate‑handling tissue; without STS, sulfated sterol deposits accumulate in the deep stroma/endothelial region. And because the cornea is more sensitive to *partial* enzyme reduction than skin is, **carriers show it while their skin stays normal** — which makes the slit lamp a genuinely useful, non‑invasive **carrier screening** step in a family.

### (b) Cryptorchidism → testicular malignancy risk

- **Undescended testis in ~10–20%** of affected males (higher when the deletion is large).
- Carries the standard **increased risk of testicular germ cell tumour** and of **subfertility/hypogonadism** that accompanies cryptorchidism from *any* cause.
- **Important mechanistic point:** the malignancy risk is attributable to the **maldescended testis itself**, not to a direct oncogenic effect of STS deficiency.
- **Action item:** examine the scrotum in every boy with XLI; refer for **early orchidopexy**; counsel about self‑examination in adolescence.

### (c) Deuteranopia (green colour blindness) — with an honest caveat

Listed classically as a **rare** association. But think critically: the **red–green opsin genes (OPN1LW/OPN1MW) lie at Xq28**, far away on the opposite arm from Xp22.31. So this is **not** a contiguous‑gene effect. The association is best explained by **co‑segregation within particular families** carrying larger X‑chromosome structural variants, or by **ascertainment bias** (families with one X‑linked trait get tested for others). It is **not a consistently reported or mechanistically explained feature** in the major references — quote it, but flag it as a weak association rather than a core feature.

### (d) Other reported associations

- Increased frequency of **attention and behavioural problems** and **cryptorchidism‑related endocrine issues** even in some patients without demonstrable large deletions (attributed partly to STS's role in neurosteroid — DHEA/DHEAS — metabolism in the brain, though this remains an area of active research).

## 8. Contiguous Gene Deletion Syndromes — "The Deletion That Took the Neighbours"

This is the concept that converts XLI from a skin disease into a **whole‑patient problem**.

**The logic, in one sentence:** because the commonest lesion is a **deletion**, and deletions vary in size, a large deletion at Xp22.31 removes *STS* **plus adjacent genes**, adding their phenotypes onto the ichthyosis.

**Analogy:** think of Xp22.31 as a terrace of houses. A small demolition takes out only the *STS* house — isolated ichthyosis. A larger demolition takes out the houses on either side too, and now you also lose the "smell and gonadotropin" house, the "neurodevelopment" house, and so on.

| Deleted gene / region | Resulting feature |
|---|---|
| ***STS*** | X‑linked ichthyosis (the core phenotype) |
| ***KAL1 / ANOS1*** (anosmin‑1) | **Kallmann syndrome** — **hypogonadotropic hypogonadism + anosmia** (failed migration of GnRH neurons and olfactory axons) |
| ***NLGN4X*** (neuroligin‑4) and neighbours | **ADHD**, autism spectrum features, **developmental delay**, intellectual disability, **seizures** |
| ***SH3KBP1*** region | **Pyloric (infantile hypertrophic) stenosis** |
| Multiple contiguous genes / larger rearrangements | **Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia**, **abdominal wall defects**, chondrodysplasia punctata (when the deletion extends to involve *ARSE/ARSL*) |

**Reported phenotypes in the literature, as listed in the source:** seizures, ADHD, developmental delay, pyloric hypertrophy, acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, abdominal wall defects.

> **The clinical rule to walk away with:** *Any male with X‑linked ichthyosis who also has neurological, developmental, behavioural, or endocrine abnormalities must be evaluated for a contiguous gene deletion syndrome* — with **chromosomal microarray / CGH** rather than targeted single‑gene testing. Specifically: ask about **sense of smell** and check for **pubertal development** (Kallmann), screen for **learning/attention problems**, and examine the **testes**.

**The Kallmann link also explains a differential diagnosis entry:** Kallmann syndrome appears in the DDx of XLI not because they look alike, but because they can be **two manifestations of one deletion**.

## 9. Investigations — and the Reasoning Behind Each Test

Most XLI is diagnosed **clinically** (male, brown adherent scale, dirty neck, spared palms, family history). Investigations serve four purposes: (i) confirmation, (ii) distinguishing from IV, (iii) carrier detection/counselling, (iv) prenatal diagnosis.

### (a) Histopathology (HPE) — *the single most examined point*

- **Compact hyperkeratosis** (± **parakeratosis**) overlying a **normal or slightly thickened granular layer**
- **Follicular hyperkeratosis** may be present
- Epidermis otherwise near‑normal in thickness; no significant acanthosis; mild or absent inflammation

**Why is the granular layer normal or thick?** Because XLI is a **retention** disorder, not a **differentiation‑initiation** disorder. Keratinocytes form keratohyalin granules perfectly well; the failure is downstream, at the moment of *letting go*. Corneocytes stack up, so the granular layer is preserved and may even look plumped.

**Contrast with ichthyosis vulgaris:** IV is caused by **loss‑of‑function mutations in filaggrin (*FLG*)**. Filaggrin's precursor, **profilaggrin, IS the keratohyalin granule**. No profilaggrin → **no granules** → the histology shows a **markedly reduced or absent granular layer** beneath the hyperkeratosis.

> **Memorise this pairing as one unit:**
> **XLI → granular layer normal/thickened.**
> **IV → granular layer absent/reduced.**
> The mechanism (retention vs. missing granule protein) makes it impossible to forget or reverse.

### (b) Electron microscopy

The source reports:
- **Increased size and number of "B4 granules"** (i.e., **membrane‑coating granules**)
- **Retention of desmosomes in the stratum corneum**
- **Numerous melanosomes** within corneocytes

**A terminology correction worth knowing:** the printed phrase "**B4 granules**" is almost certainly a typographic/OCR rendering of an older numeric designation, and the structures meant are the **membrane‑coating granules (MCGs)** — better known today as **lamellar bodies** or **Odland bodies**. These are the lysosome‑related organelles in granular‑layer keratinocytes that package the barrier lipids (glucosylceramides, phospholipids, cholesterol) and their processing enzymes, and secrete them at the granular/corneal interface. In XLI, EM shows **increased and abnormal lamellar bodies with persistence of incompletely processed lipid material in the intercellular spaces** of the stratum corneum — the ultrastructural fingerprint of the cholesterol‑sulfate traffic jam.

**Map each EM finding onto a clinical sign — this is the payoff:**

| EM finding | Clinical correlate |
|---|---|
| Retained **desmosomes/corneodesmosomes** in stratum corneum | Scales are **adherent**, not powdery (KLK inhibition by cholesterol sulfate) |
| Abundant **melanosomes** in corneocytes | Scales are **dark brown**; "dirty neck" |
| Abnormal, persistent **lamellar body / lipid material** | **Barrier failure**, high TEWL, xerosis, winter worsening |

### (c) Molecular genetic testing

- **Comparative genomic hybridisation (CGH) / chromosomal microarray** — the workhorse; detects the **Xp22.31 deletion** and, crucially, **defines its size** (which tells you whether contiguous genes are involved). This is why microarray beats single‑gene testing.
- **FISH** — confirms absence of the *STS* locus; historically the standard.
- **PCR / MLPA / sequencing** — detects deletions and the ~10% of cases due to **point mutations** that array methods will miss.

### (d) Biochemical confirmation — **steroid sulfatase enzyme assay**

Measure STS enzymatic activity in:
- **Leukocytes** (easiest, a simple blood draw)
- **Cultured fibroblasts**
- **Keratinocytes**
- **Placental tissue** (definitive if available after delivery)

Findings: **absent or markedly reduced activity in affected males; intermediate (~50%) in carrier females.** The assay is valuable precisely because it is **mutation‑agnostic** — it will be abnormal whether the cause is a deletion or a point mutation.

### (e) Serum lipoprotein analysis

**Lipoprotein electrophoresis / chromatography / spectrophotometry** shows that the accumulated **cholesterol sulfate migrates with the LDL fraction**, producing a characteristic abnormal band and an apparent increase in LDL‑associated sterol. This is a historical but conceptually instructive test: it demonstrates that the metabolic defect is **systemic**, not merely cutaneous.

### (f) Prenatal diagnosis

| Method | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Chorionic villus sampling (CVS)** | Molecular testing (deletion) and/or STS assay on chorionic tissue |
| **Amniocentesis / amniotic fluid** | **STS activity assay**; low amniotic oestrogen |
| **Maternal serum** | **Low unconjugated estriol** — often the incidental first clue on the triple/quad screen |
| **Maternal urine** | **Non‑hydrolysed (sulfated) steroids** present — the "backed‑up substrate" signature |

Modern practice increasingly picks this up via **low uE3 on aneuploidy screening**, or incidentally on **chromosomal microarray / NIPT‑prompted** testing done for other reasons.

## 10. Differential Diagnosis

### (a) Ichthyosis vulgaris — the one you must never confuse

| Feature | **X‑linked ichthyosis** | **Ichthyosis vulgaris** |
|---|---|---|
| Gene / protein | ***STS*** (Xp22.31), steroid sulfatase | ***FLG***, filaggrin |
| Inheritance | X‑linked recessive (males only) | Autosomal **semidominant** (both sexes) |
| Onset | Birth / first weeks | Usually **after 3 months**, often 1st year |
| Scale | **Large, polygonal, dark brown, adherent** | **Fine, white–grey, branny** |
| Neck | **"Dirty neck"** — prominently involved | Relatively spared |
| Preauricular face | May be **involved** | Characteristically **spared** |
| Palms/soles | **Normal** | **Palmoplantar hyperlinearity**, keratoderma |
| Keratosis pilaris | Absent/uncommon | **Characteristic** |
| Atopy | Not a feature | **Strongly associated** (atopic dermatitis, asthma) |
| Course with age | **Persists lifelong** (better in summer) | **Improves at/after puberty** |
| **Granular layer on HPE** | **Normal or thickened** | **Absent or markedly reduced** |
| Extracutaneous | Corneal opacities, cryptorchidism, obstetric history | None |

### (b) Other entries in the differential

- **X‑linked chondrodysplasia punctata (CDP)** — two flavours, and students conflate them:
  - **X‑linked *recessive* CDP (CDPX1)**, due to ***ARSL/ARSE*** (arylsulfatase E/L) at **Xp22.3**, sits **right next to *STS***. A large deletion can remove both → **ichthyosis + stippled epiphyses, nasomaxillary hypoplasia, short stature**. So this is genuinely a contiguous‑gene differential.
  - **X‑linked *dominant* CDP (CDPX2, Conradi–Hünermann–Happle)**, due to ***EBP*** at **Xp11.23**, affects **females**, and shows **Blaschko‑linear ichthyosiform scaling, follicular atrophoderma, patchy alopecia, cataracts, asymmetric limb shortening**. This is a *different* gene and a *different* chromosome band — related biochemically (cholesterol pathway) but not the same locus.
  > **Exam pearl:** "linear/Blaschko ichthyosis in a *girl* with stippled epiphyses" = CDPX2. "Plate‑like brown ichthyosis in a *boy*, possibly with stippled epiphyses if the deletion is large" = XLI ± CDPX1.
- **Kallmann syndrome** — as discussed, either a differential *or* a co‑existing contiguous deletion; look for **anosmia + delayed puberty with low LH/FSH**.
- **Rud syndrome** — a **historical/eponymous** designation for *ichthyosis + hypogonadism + seizures/intellectual disability*. Most reported "Rud syndrome" cases are now recognised as **XLI with a contiguous gene deletion** (or Sjögren–Larsson). Quote it as historical, and explain that modern genetics has largely dissolved it — that demonstrates real understanding.
- **Acquired ichthyosis** — worth a line in any ichthyosis differential: new‑onset generalised scaling in an adult should prompt evaluation for **lymphoma (especially Hodgkin), HIV, hypothyroidism, sarcoidosis, malnutrition, and drugs**. Age of onset is the discriminator.

## 11. Treatment

XLI treatment is **symptomatic and mechanism‑driven**: the scale is retained, so **hydrate it and dissolve the glue**.

**1. Topical humectants / hydration**
- **Propylene glycol** (classically 40–60% in water, sometimes under overnight occlusion) — a powerful humectant that draws water into the corneum, swelling and loosening retained corneocytes.
- **Urea (5–20%)** — dual action: humectant *and* keratolytic (it disrupts hydrogen bonding in keratin).
- Bland emollients/occlusives (petrolatum) to reduce TEWL.
- **Frequent bathing with gentle mechanical debridement** (soft cloth, pumice) after soaking — cheap and genuinely effective, because the problem is mechanical adherence.

**2. Keratolytics — "effective alone or in combination"**
- **Salicylic acid**, **lactic acid** (and other alpha‑hydroxy acids), **urea**; often combined (e.g., salicylic acid + lactic acid + urea in an emollient base).
- **Mechanism:** these agents reduce corneocyte cohesion and pH‑dependently loosen corneodesmosomes — pharmacologically doing the job the missing KLK activity cannot.
- ⚠️ **Safety caution:** **salicylic acid over large body surface areas in infants and young children risks percutaneous absorption and salicylism** (tinnitus, tachypnoea, metabolic acidosis, confusion). Also watch **urea** in neonates and lactic acid stinging on fissured skin. In young children, favour urea/lactic acid, limit BSA, and avoid occlusion with salicylates.

**3. Retinoids — rarely needed**
- **Systemic retinoids (acitretin)** are reserved for **severe, refractory** disease. They normalise terminal differentiation and thin the stratum corneum.
- Remember the standard retinoid caveats: **teratogenicity** (less critical here since patients are male, but relevant to household/blood donation counselling), **mucocutaneous dryness, hyperlipidaemia, hepatotoxicity, and long‑term skeletal effects** (DISH, premature epiphyseal closure in children).
- Topical retinoids (tazarotene, tretinoin) can help localised areas but are often irritating.

**4. Mechanism‑based/experimental**
- Because the defect is a **relative deficiency of free cholesterol plus an excess of cholesterol sulfate**, there is a conceptual rationale for **topical cholesterol** (replace what's missing) **± a topical statin such as lovastatin** (reduce synthesis of the precursor that is being sulfated). This combination is **established practice in CHILD syndrome** and has been explored in XLI and other cholesterol‑pathway ichthyoses; treat it as a rational adjunct/emerging option rather than standard care.

**5. Whole‑patient management — do not stop at the skin**
- **Slit‑lamp examination** of the patient and of **potential carrier females** in the family.
- **Testicular examination**; refer for **orchidopexy** if cryptorchid; adolescent counselling on self‑examination.
- **Developmental, behavioural and olfactory assessment**; if abnormal → **chromosomal microarray** for a contiguous gene deletion; refer to endocrinology if pubertal delay (Kallmann).
- **Genetic counselling** for the family, including **obstetric counselling** for carrier women about possible **failure of labour onset**.
- **Psychosocial support** — "dirty neck" in a school‑age boy invites teasing and accusations of poor hygiene; explicitly explaining the condition to families and schools is real therapy.

> **A useful contrast for exams:** in the **collodion baby** (Q18), keratolytics are **contraindicated** because the membrane is a massively compromised barrier over a large surface area in a neonate — absorption and toxicity are near‑guaranteed. In **established XLI**, keratolytics are **the mainstay**, because the barrier, while abnormal, is intact and mature. Same drug class, opposite advice — and the reason is *barrier integrity and body surface area*, not the diagnosis per se.

## 12. Common Misunderstandings — Let's Clear Them Up

1. **"XLI is a proliferative ichthyosis."** No. It is a **retention** ichthyosis — the epidermis is not hyperproliferative; the corneocytes simply will not detach. This is why the epidermis is not markedly acanthotic and the granular layer is preserved.

2. **"The granular layer is reduced in all ichthyoses."** Reduced/absent granular layer is the hallmark of **ichthyosis vulgaris** (no profilaggrin → no keratohyalin granules). In **XLI it is normal or thickened**. This single line earns marks.

3. **"Carrier females have mild scaling."** They are **clinically normal**, because *STS* **escapes X‑inactivation** and ~50% activity suffices for skin. They may, however, have **asymptomatic corneal opacities**.

4. **"The low estriol is a maternal problem."** It is **fetal/placental**. The *placenta* (fetal tissue) lacks STS, so the mother's oestrogen levels fall. The mother's own enzyme status is irrelevant to the estriol level.

5. **"XLI presents as a collodion baby."** No — that is **ARCI (lamellar ichthyosis/CIE)**. XLI newborns show **mild peeling with translucent scale**, and may be missed entirely.

6. **"It improves with age like ichthyosis vulgaris."** It **improves in summer** but **persists lifelong**. IV is the one that improves at puberty.

7. **"Deuteranopia is a core feature."** It is a **weak, mechanistically unexplained** association (the opsin genes are at **Xq28**, not Xp22.31). Mention it as a rare association, not a defining one.

8. **"The testicular cancer risk is caused by STS deficiency."** No — it is mediated by **cryptorchidism**, which is more frequent in XLI. Fix the cryptorchidism and you address the risk.

9. **"Ichthyosis in a boy = XLI, full stop."** Always ask the extra questions — smell, puberty, development, seizures — because the **deletion may be bigger than one gene**.

10. **A textual footnote from the source worth flagging:** the gene "**SULTZBI**" listed on the same page belongs to the **lamellar ichthyosis (ARCI)** gene panel, not to XLI, and is a typographic rendering of ***SULT2B1*** (sulfotransferase family 2B member 1) — the enzyme that, interestingly, performs the **opposite reaction to STS**: it *adds* sulfate to cholesterol. Two genes, one metabolite, two different ichthyoses — a lovely illustration that **cholesterol sulfate homeostasis must be balanced in both directions** for normal desquamation.

## 13. The Core Idea in One Picture

> **X‑linked ichthyosis is a disease of one missing pair of molecular scissors.**
>
> **Steroid sulfatase** normally snips the **sulfate cap** off two molecules. In the **skin**, it snips it off **cholesterol sulfate**, dissolving the glue that holds corneocytes together and supplying free cholesterol to the barrier mortar. In the **placenta**, it snips it off **DHEAS**, liberating the precursor for **estriol**.
>
> Delete *STS* at **Xp22.31** — which happens easily, because the region is deletion‑prone — and both snips fail at once:
>
> - **Skin:** cholesterol sulfate piles up → **serine proteases (KLK5/7) and transglutaminase‑1 are inhibited** → corneodesmosomes are never cut and the cornified envelope is never properly built → **retention hyperkeratosis** with **adherent, melanosome‑rich, dark brown polygonal scale**, worst on the **neck ("dirty neck")**, sparing **palms and soles**, better in **summer**, **never** outgrown, with **normal/thickened granular layer** on biopsy and **retained desmosomes + abundant melanosomes + abnormal lamellar bodies** on EM.
> - **Placenta:** DHEAS piles up → **no DHEA → no estriol** → **low maternal estriol**, **sulfated steroids in maternal urine**, and a uterus that never becomes contraction‑competent → **failure of spontaneous labour**.
> - **Neighbourhood:** if the deletion is large, the neighbours go too → **Kallmann (anosmia + hypogonadism), ADHD/developmental delay/seizures, pyloric hypertrophy, ALL, abdominal wall defects, chondrodysplasia punctata.**
>
> **Treatment therefore does mechanically what the enzyme cannot do chemically:** **hydrate** (propylene glycol, urea, emollients) and **dissolve the glue** (keratolytics), with **acitretin** held in reserve — plus **slit lamp, testes, development, and genetic counselling** for the whole patient and the whole family.

## 14. Rapid‑Fire Revision Lines

- **Gene/locus:** *STS*, **Xp22.31**; ~**90% complete deletions** (hence CGH/FISH as first‑line).
- **Incidence:** 1 in 2,000–9,500 males; second commonest ichthyosis after IV.
- **Metabolite:** **cholesterol 3‑sulfate** accumulates (10–20×); inhibits **TG‑1** and **KLK5/KLK7**.
- **Signature sign:** **"dirty neck disease"**; **preauricular** involvement; **palms/soles spared**.
- **Course:** better in summer, **no improvement with age**.
- **Histology:** hyperkeratosis with **normal/thickened granular layer** (vs **absent** in IV).
- **EM:** retained desmosomes, abundant melanosomes, abnormal **lamellar (membrane‑coating) bodies**.
- **Eye:** comma‑shaped/dot **Descemet membrane opacities**, asymptomatic, in ~25% of **carriers** → carrier screening.
- **Gonads:** cryptorchidism 10–20% → testicular cancer risk → **orchidopexy**.
- **Obstetrics:** **low maternal estriol**, sulfated steroids in urine, **failure of spontaneous labour**.
- **Contiguous genes:** *KAL1/ANOS1* → **Kallmann**; *NLGN4X* → ADHD/delay/seizures; *SH3KBP1* → pyloric hypertrophy; *ARSL/ARSE* → CDPX1; also ALL, abdominal wall defects.
- **DDx:** IV, CDPX1/CDPX2 (Conradi–Hünermann), Kallmann, "Rud syndrome" (historical), acquired ichthyosis.
- **Treatment:** propylene glycol, urea/lactic/salicylic acid keratolytics (**beware salicylism in children**), acitretin rarely; ± topical cholesterol/statin (mechanistic).

If you can draw the two arrows out of steroid sulfatase — one to the skin, one to the placenta — and then narrate every clinical sign, every biopsy finding, every test, and every treatment as a consequence of those two arrows, you understand X‑linked ichthyosis better than most textbooks present it.
