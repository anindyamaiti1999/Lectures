# 🎓 Mini‑Lecture: Pityriasis Rubra Pilaris (PRP)

*"The disease that looks like psoriasis, behaves like an ichthyosis, and is coloured like a sunset."*

---

## 1. The Big Picture — Where Does PRP Sit in Dermatology?

Let me start by orienting you, because PRP is one of those diseases students memorize as a list and then fail to *recognize* in a clinic.

Imagine the great family of **papulosquamous disorders** — diseases whose signature is *papules (bumps) plus squames (scales)*. This family includes psoriasis, lichen planus, pityriasis rosea, secondary syphilis, and the seborrhoeic dermatitides. PRP is the eccentric cousin in this family: **rare, inflammatory, papulosquamous, and of unknown etiopathogenesis** — a *group* of disorders (note the plural thinking) that share a common clinical and histopathological signature but differ enormously in age of onset, distribution, and prognosis.

Why does it matter?

1. **It mimics psoriasis** — and treating it as psoriasis can waste months and expose the patient to unnecessary phototherapy that may actually *worsen* the disease.
2. **It can become erythrodermic** — and erythroderma is a dermatological emergency, capable of causing high‑output cardiac failure, hypothermia, sepsis, and metabolic catastrophe.
3. **It can be a marker of internal disease** — HIV infection, hypothyroidism, autoimmunity, and malignancy.
4. **It teaches you a beautiful lesson in genetics** — the *CARD14* story links PRP to psoriasis at the molecular level, even though the two diseases look and behave differently.

### Decoding the name (always do this — names are free knowledge)

| Word | Meaning | What it tells you clinically |
|---|---|---|
| **Pityriasis** | Greek *pityron* = bran | The scale is fine, dry, **bran‑like** — not thick silvery plates |
| **Rubra** | Latin = red | The disease is **erythematous**, tending to erythroderma |
| **Pilaris** | Latin *pilus* = hair | The primary lesion is centred on the **hair follicle** |

So the name itself is a three‑word clinical description: *bran‑like scaling + redness + follicular involvement*. If a patient in front of you has all three, PRP must be on your list.

### Eponyms (examiners love these)

- **Devergie's disease** — after Marie‑Guillaume‑Alphonse Devergie, who described it in **1857**.
- **Lichen ruber acuminatus** — Kaposi's term, emphasizing the **pointed (acuminate) follicular papules**, distinguishing it from *lichen ruber planus* (flat‑topped = lichen planus). Notice how elegantly the old nomenclature encoded morphology: *planus* = flat, *acuminatus* = pointed.

## 2. Epidemiology — Who Gets It, and Why the Two Peaks?

- **Rare**: roughly 1 in 5,000 to 1 in 50,000 dermatology outpatients — you may see only a handful in a career.
- **Bimodal age distribution**: a **juvenile peak** (first decade) and an **adult peak in the 5th–6th decades**.
- **Familial form**: rare, autosomal dominant, begins in **early childhood**.
- **M = F** — no sex predilection.

**Why bimodal?** This is the conceptual key to the whole topic. The two peaks reflect **two different biological engines**:

- The **early‑onset/familial cases** are largely **genetically driven** — germline *CARD14* gain‑of‑function mutations present from birth, producing a lifelong, chronic, ichthyosiform disease.
- The **late‑onset/sporadic adult cases** are largely **acquired/immunologically triggered** — an inflammatory storm ignited by infection, drugs, UV, trauma, or an internal disease, which characteristically **burns out over ~3 years**.

Hold onto this idea, because it *predicts the entire Griffiths classification and its prognostic logic*: **genetic = chronic; acquired = self‑limited.**

---

## 3. Associations — PRP as a Window into the Body

PRP is not always "just skin." Documented associations:

| Association | Why you must look for it |
|---|---|
| **Hypothyroidism** | Thyroid hormone is required for normal keratinocyte differentiation; check TSH — cheap, treatable |
| **HIV** (and other infections) | Defines **Type VI**; changes prognosis and management entirely |
| **Autoimmune disease** | Shared Th17 immunobiology (e.g., myasthenia gravis, hypothyroidism, vitiligo, celiac disease reported) |
| **Malignancy** | PRP may be **paraneoplastic** (bronchogenic, renal, hepatocellular carcinomas, lymphoma reported) — age‑appropriate screening in abrupt adult‑onset, treatment‑refractory disease |

**Teaching point:** A 60‑year‑old with explosive, retinoid‑refractory PRP deserves a careful history, examination, and age‑appropriate malignancy screen — not just another prescription.

## 4. Pathophysiology — Cause → Effect → Outcome

We don't fully understand PRP (hence "unknown etiopathology"), but the pieces we do have form a coherent chain. Let me walk you through it step by step.

### Step 1 — The genetic spark: *CARD14* gain‑of‑function

**What it is:** *CARD14* (**CA**spase **R**ecruitment **D**omain family member **14**) encodes a scaffolding protein expressed predominantly in **keratinocytes**. Its job is to sit in the cytoplasm and, when appropriately stimulated, assemble a signalling complex that activates **NF‑κB**.

**Analogy:** Think of CARD14 as an *ignition switch* wired into the keratinocyte's alarm system. Normally it clicks on only when a real threat appears, then clicks off.

**What goes wrong:** A **gain‑of‑function (GOF) mutation** jams the switch in the "on" position. CARD14 now self‑aggregates and signals **constitutively**, with no stimulus at all.

**Where this matters clinically:** GOF *CARD14* mutations are described in **familial PRP** (classically the early‑onset **Type V** phenotype) — and, tellingly, in **familial psoriasis** at the **PSORS2 locus on 17q25.3**. One gene, two diseases. This is why PRP and psoriasis look like siblings.

### Step 2 — Sustained NF‑κB activation

**What NF‑κB does:** It is the master transcription factor of inflammation. Once in the nucleus, it switches on genes for cytokines, chemokines, and antimicrobial peptides.

**Effect:** The keratinocyte — normally a structural cell — becomes a **cytokine factory**, pumping out **IL‑1, IL‑6, IL‑8, IL‑23, CCL20** and more, *without any external trigger*. This is the essence of an **auto‑inflammatory keratinocyte disease**: the inflammation is being driven *from inside the epidermis outward*, not from the immune system inward.

### Step 3 — Recruitment of the IL‑23/Th17 axis

Keratinocyte‑derived **IL‑23** and **CCL20** recruit and polarize **Th17 lymphocytes** into the skin. These cells secrete **IL‑17** and **IL‑22**.

### Step 4 — The feed‑forward loop (the vicious cycle)

**IL‑17 and IL‑22 act right back on the keratinocyte**, causing it to:
- **Proliferate faster** → acanthosis (thickened epidermis), hyperkeratosis
- **Differentiate abnormally** → retained nuclei in the stratum corneum (**parakeratosis**), disordered granular layer
- **Produce yet more IL‑23, chemokines, and antimicrobial peptides** → recruiting more Th17 cells

**Analogy:** A microphone held too close to its own loudspeaker. A tiny initial sound (the CARD14 mutation, or a drug, or a virus) gets amplified into a deafening, self‑sustaining screech. Therapy works by either **turning down the amplifier** (retinoids normalizing keratinocyte differentiation) or **cutting the feedback cable** (anti‑IL‑12/23, anti‑IL‑17 biologics).

### The unanswered question

Note carefully: this loop is *almost identical* to psoriasis. So why don't PRP and psoriasis look the same? The honest answer is that **we don't know** — but the clues lie in the *pattern* of the response. In PRP the abnormality is **follicle‑centred** and **patchy/asynchronous** (hence the histological checkerboard and the clinical islands of sparing), and crucially **neutrophils are not recruited** (no Munro microabscesses, no pustules of Kogoj). Something in the downstream wiring — perhaps different IL‑17 isoform usage or different chemokine profiles — diverts PRP away from the neutrophilic, synchronous, confluent pattern of psoriasis.

### Triggers (the "acquired spark")

- **UV / photo‑aggravation** — clinically vital: **this disease can be made worse by light** (remember this when we reach treatment)
- **Trauma** — **koebnerization** (lesions appearing along lines of injury), shared with psoriasis and lichen planus
- **Drugs**
- **Infections** — bacterial and viral (including HIV, streptococcal, and post‑vaccination reports)

## 5. Clinical Features — Building the Classic Picture

Before we split PRP into six types, let me give you the **composite portrait of classic disease**, because every type is a variation on this theme. I'll describe the natural history as a *film*, not a snapshot.

### Scene 1 — The prodrome: a red patch on the head

The disease typically begins as **pink‑red, slightly scaly macules** on the **face, scalp, or neck**. Utterly non‑specific. Many patients are treated for seborrhoeic dermatitis at this stage.

### Scene 2 — The primary lesion: the keratotic follicular papule

This is the **diagnostic unit** of PRP. Each hair follicle develops a small, firm, **acuminate (pointed) keratotic papule** with a central horny plug, often with the hair shaft trapped inside.

**How to find it:** Run your fingertips over the **dorsa of the proximal phalanges** and the **extensor elbows and knees**. The sensation is unmistakable — a **"nutmeg‑grater"** roughness. This is the single most useful bedside manoeuvre in suspected PRP, especially in erythroderma, where the follicular papules may be the *only* remaining clue to the diagnosis.

### Scene 3 — Cephalocaudal spread

The papules **coalesce** and the erythema marches **downward from head to foot** over weeks. This **cephalocaudal progression** is a hallmark of the *classic* types — and its **absence** is a defining feature of atypical Type II.

**Analogy:** Picture red paint poured over the crown of the head, flowing steadily downward over the body.

### Scene 4 — The mature plaque: orange‑red with bran‑like scale

Confluence produces the characteristic **orange‑red / salmon plaques** with **fine, bran‑like scaling**.

> **Colour is a diagnostic sign.** Psoriasis is **salmon‑pink with thick, silvery‑white, micaceous scale**. PRP is **orange‑to‑sunset‑red with fine, powdery scale**. That orange hue arises from the combination of dermal erythema seen *through* a thick, compact, yellowish, carotene‑retaining hyperkeratotic stratum corneum. Once you have seen it, you don't forget it.

### Scene 5 — Islands of sparing (*nappes claires*)

As erythema becomes generalized, **sharply demarcated islands of completely normal skin** remain within the sea of redness.

**Analogy:** An archipelago of normal skin in a red ocean — or, as some describe it, a photographic negative of psoriasis.

**Why it matters:** In **erythroderma**, the differential is long (psoriasis, drug eruption, atopic eczema, cutaneous T‑cell lymphoma, ichthyoses). The combination of **islands of sparing + orange hue + follicular papules + waxy keratoderma** essentially makes the diagnosis at the bedside. Psoriatic erythroderma is characteristically **confluent, without sparing**.

**Why does sparing occur?** Because the pathology is **asynchronous and patchy** — different areas of epidermis are in different phases of the disturbed differentiation cycle. This is the *same* biology that produces the histological **checkerboard**. Clinically visible islands of sparing and the microscopic checkerboard are **two views of one phenomenon**: spatial heterogeneity of keratinization. That single insight ties clinic and microscope together.

### Scene 6 — Palms and soles: the "PRP sandals"

A **yellowish‑orange, waxy, diffuse palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK)** develops, thick enough to look and feel like the patient is wearing a pair of **wax sandals and gloves**. Deep painful fissures are common and are a major cause of disability — patients cannot walk or work.

### Scene 7 — Nails

Nail changes are of the **keratinization type**, not the psoriasis type:
- **Thickened nail plate**
- **Yellowish‑brown discoloration**
- **Subungual hyperkeratosis**
- Splinter haemorrhages may occur

> **Critical distinction:** PRP nails are **thickened, yellow‑brown, with subungual debris**. Psoriatic nails show **pitting, oil‑drop (salmon patch) discoloration, and onycholysis**. **Pitting is not a feature of PRP** — an exam‑worthy discriminator.

### Scene 8 — Face and eyes

**Erythroderma with facial redness**, and — because the thickened, inflamed, inelastic skin of the lower lid contracts and pulls it outward — **ectropion**.

**Mechanism:** Think of the taut, scaling facial skin as a shrinking shrink‑wrap. It tugs the delicate lower eyelid down and away from the globe, exposing the conjunctiva.

### Other features worth knowing
- **Pruritus** is variable but can be intense; burning and **skin tightness** are characteristic complaints
- **Ectropion, alopecia** (sparse body hair in Type II), **mucosal involvement is rare** (unlike lichen planus)
- **Koebner phenomenon** may be seen

## 6. Classification — The Griffiths Classification (1980)

W.A.D. **Griffiths** divided PRP into **six types** (Types I–V originally; **Type VI** added by **Miralles et al., 1995**, for HIV‑associated disease), based on three axes:

1. **Age of onset** (adult vs juvenile)
2. **Clinical presentation** (classic vs atypical vs circumscribed)
3. **Prognosis** (self‑limiting vs chronic)

**The organizing logic (learn this before the details):**

> **Classic types (I, III) = acquired, cephalocaudal, self‑limiting, good prognosis.**
> **Atypical types (II, V) = chronic, ichthyosiform, genetically driven, poor prognosis.**
> **Type IV = localized/circumscribed — neither classic nor atypical; unpredictable.**
> **Type VI = HIV‑driven — classic morphology, terrible prognosis.**

Notice the neat symmetry: **I = classic adult, II = atypical adult, III = classic juvenile, IV = circumscribed juvenile, V = atypical juvenile, VI = HIV.** Adults get I and II; children get III, IV and V; VI stands alone.

---

### **Type I — Classic Adult PRP**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** | **Most common — ~80%** of all cases |
| **Age** | **40–60 years** |
| **Onset** | Pink‑red, slightly scaly **macules on face, scalp, neck** |
| **Primary lesion** | Discrete **keratotic follicular papules** with **cephalocaudal spread**, coalescing |
| **Plaques** | **Orange‑red plaques** with **bran‑like scaling** and **islands of sparing** |
| **Palms/soles** | **Yellowish‑orange waxy PPK** — "**PRP sandals**" |
| **Nails** | **Thickened plate**, yellow‑brown discoloration, **subungual hyperkeratosis** |
| **Severe form** | **Erythroderma** with facial redness and **ectropion** |
| **Prognosis** | ⭐ **Best prognosis — resolution within ~3 years in ~80%** |

This is *the* type you must be able to describe fluently, because it is four out of every five patients. Everything in Section 5 above is Type I.

---

### **Type II — Atypical Adult PRP**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** | **~5%** |
| **Age** | **~20 years onward** (younger adult onset) |
| **Course** | **Chronic — no resolution after 3 years** (this 3‑year line is the formal definition separating it from Type I) |
| **Distribution** | Predilection for the **lower extremities** |
| **Morphology** | **Eczematous lesions** with **ichthyosiform scaling** |
| **PPK** | **Coarser PPK with lamellar scaling** (plate‑like, not waxy) |
| **Hair** | **Sparseness of body hair** (alopecia) |
| **Key negative** | **Cephalocaudal progression is ABSENT** |
| **Prognosis** | Poor — chronic, lifelong |

**How to remember Type II:** It has "lost" the classic features — no head‑to‑toe march, no waxy orange keratoderma, no orange plaques. Instead it borrows from *eczema and ichthyosis*: eczematous patches on the legs, lamellar scale, and thinning body hair. It behaves less like an inflammatory storm and more like a **disorder of keratinization** — which is exactly the chapter we are in.

---

### **Type III — Classic Juvenile PRP**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** | **~10%** |
| **Age** | **5–10 years** (often follows an infection) |
| **Morphology** | Same as **Type I**, but in a child |
| **Course** | **Resolution within 1–2 years**; rarely longer |
| **Evolution** | May **rarely evolve into Type IV** |
| **Relapse** | **Recurrence in later adult life** is described |
| **Prognosis** | Good — fastest resolution of all types |

**Concept:** Type III is "Type I in a child, only faster." It is often **post‑infectious** — consistent with our acquired‑trigger model.

---

### **Type IV — Circumscribed Juvenile PRP**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** | **~25%** — the **second most common type overall, and the most common in children** |
| **Age** | **Prepubertal children, < 12 years** |
| **Morphology** | **Well‑demarcated red plaques** composed of **grouped hyperkeratotic follicular papules** |
| **Sites** | **Elbows and knees** and other **bony prominences** |
| **Palms/soles** | **PPK present** |
| **Prognosis** | **Uncertain** — spontaneous **remissions and exacerbations**; roughly a third clear by adulthood |

**Teaching point:** This is the great impostor. A child with sharply demarcated, scaly, red plaques on the elbows and knees looks exactly like **plaque psoriasis**. The giveaways are: the plaques are made of **discrete follicular papules** on close inspection (use dermoscopy or magnification), there is **palmoplantar keratoderma**, and the biopsy shows a **checkerboard** rather than Munro microabscesses. "Circumscribed" is the key word — the disease stays localized and never generalizes or marches cephalocaudally.

---

### **Type V — Atypical Juvenile PRP**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** | **~5%** |
| **Onset** | **Very early — at or shortly after birth** |
| **Course** | **Chronic**, lifelong |
| **Morphology** | **Follicular hyperkeratosis** + **ichthyosiform scaling** |
| **Distinctive sign** | **Scleroderma‑like changes of palms and soles** (sclerodactyly‑like tightening, sometimes with digital constriction) |
| **Genetics** | This is the type most strongly linked to **familial *CARD14* GOF mutations** |
| **Prognosis** | Poor — chronic |

**Why Type V matters disproportionately:** It is the **genetic prototype**. Most familial PRP is Type V. A neonate or infant with follicular hyperkeratosis, ichthyosiform scaling, and scleroderma‑like palms and soles — especially with a family history — should prompt consideration of ***CARD14* sequencing**, and it raises the possibility of **targeted therapy** (IL‑12/23 or IL‑17 blockade), because we now know the molecular pathway that is stuck "on." This is precision dermatology in action.

---

### **Type VI — HIV‑Associated PRP**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Morphology** | Similar to **Type I** |
| **Distinctive features** | **Red follicular papules** with prominent **follicular plugging** and **keratotic spicules** |
| **Associations** | **Hidradenitis suppurativa**, **acne conglobata** (i.e., features of the **follicular occlusion tetrad**, which also includes dissecting cellulitis and pilonidal sinus) |
| **Prognosis** | **Poor**, with **resistance to antiretroviral therapy (ART)** alone |
| **Preferred therapy** | **Acitretin + UVA1**; also triple ART, retinoids, cautious immunomodulation |

**The conceptual message of Type VI:** PRP can be the **presenting sign of HIV**. Any adult presenting with PRP — *particularly* with prominent spicules, follicular plugging, or coexisting HS/acne conglobata — should be offered **HIV testing**. This single test changes prognosis, treatment, and the patient's life. Note also the unifying thread: PRP, HS, acne conglobata, and dissecting cellulitis are all **diseases of the follicular unit**, so their clustering in an immunodysregulated host is biologically coherent rather than coincidental.

---

### 🧠 High‑Yield Mnemonic and Summary Table

> **Classic (I, III)** = best prognosis → adult & juvenile
> **Atypical (II, V)** = chronic, poor prognosis → adult & juvenile
> **Circumscribed (IV)** = juvenile, localized, unpredictable
> **HIV‑related (VI)** = worst prognosis

| Type | Name | % | Age | Key features | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Classic adult | **80%** | 40–60 y | Cephalocaudal, orange plaques, islands of sparing, waxy PPK, erythroderma, ectropion | **Best** — clears in ~3 y |
| **II** | Atypical adult | 5% | ~20 y | Legs, eczematous, ichthyosiform, lamellar coarse PPK, sparse hair, **no cephalocaudal spread** | Chronic > 3 y |
| **III** | Classic juvenile | 10% | 5–10 y | Like Type I in a child; often post‑infectious | Good — 1–2 y |
| **IV** | Circumscribed juvenile | **25%** | < 12 y | Demarcated plaques of follicular papules on **elbows/knees**, PPK | Uncertain, relapsing |
| **V** | Atypical juvenile | 5% | Birth | Follicular hyperkeratosis + ichthyosiform scale + **scleroderma‑like palms/soles**; ***CARD14*** | Chronic |
| **VI** | HIV‑associated | — | Adult | Type I–like + **spicules, plugging**, HS, acne conglobata | **Poor**, ART‑resistant |

## 7. Complications — Why PRP Can Kill

Understanding complications means understanding that **skin is an organ with physiological functions**: barrier, thermoregulation, fluid balance, and immune defence. Strip those away and the body fails.

### 1. Erythroderma → high‑output cardiac failure

**The chain:** Generalized inflammation → massive cutaneous **vasodilatation** → a huge low‑resistance vascular shunt through the skin → **fall in systemic vascular resistance** → compensatory rise in **heart rate and stroke volume** to maintain perfusion → sustained **high cardiac output** → in an older patient or one with pre‑existing cardiac disease, **decompensation and high‑output cardiac failure**.

**Analogy:** Opening every tap in a house at once. The pump must work furiously to maintain pressure — and eventually it fails.

Erythroderma also causes: **hypothermia** (radiant heat loss through dilated vessels), **dehydration and electrolyte loss** (transepidermal water loss), **hypoalbuminaemia and negative nitrogen balance** (protein lost in scale — up to 20–30 g/day), **anaemia**, **thermoregulatory instability**, and **secondary infection/sepsis** from a breached barrier. These are the practical reasons erythrodermic PRP is admitted.

### 2. Ectropion → dry eyes → peripheral ulcerative keratitis

**The chain:** Tight, inflamed facial skin everts the lower lid → **incomplete lid closure (lagophthalmos)** and loss of the tear‑distributing lid mechanism → the cornea and conjunctiva are **exposed and desiccated** → **dry eye** → epithelial breakdown → **peripheral ulcerative keratitis**, and in the worst case corneal perforation and visual loss.

**Practical lesson:** Every PRP patient with facial involvement needs **eye examination and ophthalmology referral**. Lubricants, taping, and moisture chambers are cheap; corneal scarring is permanent.

## 8. Investigations

### The primary test: skin biopsy (histopathology)

**Practical tip:** Biopsy a **fully developed follicular papule or the edge of a plaque**, and consider **multiple biopsies**, because features are patchy — a single "wrong" spot may be non‑diagnostic. In erythroderma, repeat biopsies over time are often required.

**The findings, and what each one *means*:**

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Alternating perpendicular and horizontal orthokeratosis and parakeratosis — the "checkerboard" pattern** | **The pathognomonic feature.** Neighbouring columns of epidermis are keratinizing *asynchronously*. Orthokeratosis (anucleate, "normal" cornification) alternates with parakeratosis (retained nuclei = accelerated, incomplete cornification) in **both** vertical and horizontal planes |
| **Hyperkeratosis with follicular plugging** | The follicular ostium is blocked by a compact keratin plug — the histological basis of the clinical **acuminate follicular papule** and the nutmeg‑grater feel |
| **"Shoulder parakeratosis"** — parakeratosis on **either side of the follicular ostium** | Characteristic of PRP; the perifollicular epidermis "shoulders" the follicle with parakeratotic caps. In **psoriasis** parakeratosis is **confluent and diffuse**, not shoulder‑restricted |
| **Focal or confluent hypergranulosis** | The granular layer is **preserved or thickened** — opposite to psoriasis, where it is **lost/attenuated** |
| **Irregular psoriasiform acanthosis** | Epidermal thickening with **broad, short, irregular** rete ridges (psoriasis shows **regular, elongated, club‑shaped** ridges) |
| **Acantholysis** (may be present) | Focal loss of keratinocyte cohesion — an under‑recognized but well‑described PRP feature; can cause confusion with pemphigus or Grover's disease |
| **Sparse superficial perivascular lymphohistiocytic infiltrate — LACKING NEUTROPHILS** | Highly significant. PRP is a **lymphocyte/histiocyte** disease. Psoriasis is a **neutrophil** disease (Munro microabscesses in the stratum corneum, spongiform pustules of Kogoj in the spinous layer) |
| **In erythroderma: thinned epidermis with diminished granular layer** | Late/erythrodermic PRP loses its classic features and becomes non‑specific — one more reason to biopsy early and repeatedly |

### 🔍 PRP versus Psoriasis — The Comparison You Must Own

| Parameter | **PRP** | **Psoriasis** |
|---|---|---|
| **Colour** | **Orange‑red / salmon‑orange** | Salmon‑pink / bright red |
| **Scale** | Fine, **bran‑like**, powdery | Thick, **silvery‑white, micaceous** |
| **Primary lesion** | **Follicular** keratotic papule | Non‑follicular plaque |
| **Spread** | **Cephalocaudal** (classic types) | Extensors, scalp, sacrum; no set direction |
| **Erythroderma** | **Islands of sparing** | **Confluent**, no sparing |
| **Palms/soles** | **Waxy orange PPK** ("sandals") | Well‑demarcated plaques, fissures, pustules |
| **Nails** | **Thick, yellow‑brown, subungual hyperkeratosis**; **no pitting** | **Pitting**, oil‑drop sign, onycholysis |
| **Cornification** | **Checkerboard** ortho/parakeratosis; **shoulder parakeratosis** | **Confluent parakeratosis** |
| **Granular layer** | **Preserved / hypergranulosis** | **Lost** |
| **Rete ridges** | **Irregular**, broad, short | **Regular**, elongated, club‑shaped |
| **Neutrophils** | **Absent** | **Present** — Munro microabscesses, Kogoj pustules |
| **Capillaries** | Not characteristically altered | **Dilated, tortuous** in papillae; suprapapillary thinning |
| **Auspitz sign** | Absent | Present |
| **Phototherapy** | May **aggravate** | Usually **helps** |

### Supporting investigations (and the reasoning behind each)

- **HIV serology** — mandatory in adult‑onset PRP; identifies Type VI
- **Thyroid function (TSH, free T4)** — hypothyroidism is treatable and can drive keratinization defects
- **Complete blood count, renal and liver function, albumin, electrolytes** — baseline, and essential in erythroderma to quantify metabolic derangement
- **Fasting lipids and LFTs** — mandatory **before and during systemic retinoid therapy**
- **Pregnancy test / contraception counselling** — retinoids are **potent teratogens**
- **Age‑appropriate malignancy screening** — in abrupt, severe, refractory adult PRP (paraneoplastic possibility)
- ***CARD14* sequencing** — in familial and very‑early‑onset (Type V) disease; may guide biologic choice
- **Ophthalmology referral** — for ectropion and ocular surface assessment
- **Consider T‑cell clonality / repeat biopsy** — if erythroderma persists, to exclude **cutaneous T‑cell lymphoma / Sézary syndrome**, the most important erythroderma mimic

### Differential diagnosis worth rehearsing
**Psoriasis** (the big one) · **erythrodermic drug eruption** · **cutaneous T‑cell lymphoma / Sézary syndrome** · **atopic/seborrhoeic erythroderma** · **the ichthyoses** (especially for Types II and V) · **keratosis pilaris and lichen spinulosus** (for follicular papules) · **phrynoderma (vitamin A deficiency)** · **follicular eczema** · **subacute cutaneous lupus**.

## 9. Management — A Therapeutic Ladder with Reasons

Two principles frame everything:

1. **PRP has no licensed therapy and no randomized controlled trials** — the evidence base is case series and expert opinion. Therefore treatment is **individualized, staged, and by trial**.
2. **Many patients (especially Type I) will remit spontaneously within 3 years.** So for mild disease, *reassurance, explanation, and supportive care are genuine treatment* — and you must weigh the toxicity of a drug against a disease that may resolve on its own. That's not therapeutic nihilism; it's proportionality.

### A. Supportive and general measures (never skip these)

- **Education and prognosis counselling** — telling a Type I patient "this usually burns out in about 3 years" is enormously therapeutic
- **Erythroderma care**: warm ambient temperature, fluid and electrolyte replacement, **high‑protein nutritional support**, monitoring for **sepsis** and **cardiac failure**, wet wraps and bland emollients
- **Fissure care** for PPK: soaking, paring, occlusive ointments, cushioned footwear — this is what restores the ability to walk
- **Eye care** for ectropion: lubricants, ointment at night, moisture chambers, ophthalmology input
- **Avoid triggers**: sun protection (remember photo‑aggravation), avoid trauma (koebnerization), review and stop culprit drugs, treat intercurrent infection
- **Manage associations**: treat hypothyroidism, optimize ART in HIV

### B. Topical therapy — for limited disease and as adjunct to everything

| Agent | Mechanism / rationale |
|---|---|
| **Emollients with urea, salicylic acid, α‑hydroxy acids** | **Keratolysis + hydration.** Urea is humectant and breaks keratin hydrogen bonds; salicylic acid dissolves corneocyte desmosomal cement; AHAs reduce corneocyte cohesion. Together they **thin the hyperkeratotic shell**, relieve tightness and fissuring, and — importantly — **let other topicals penetrate** |
| **Topical corticosteroids** | Broad anti‑inflammatory: inhibit NF‑κB and cytokine transcription, reduce erythema and itch. Symptomatically helpful, rarely curative; watch for atrophy with long‑term use |
| **Topical retinoids** (tretinoin, tazarotene) | Bind RAR → **normalize keratinocyte differentiation and proliferation**, reduce follicular plugging. Directly attack the core defect. Can irritate |
| **Topical vitamin D analogues** (calcipotriol) | Bind VDR → **promote differentiation, inhibit proliferation**, mildly immunomodulatory. Often combined with a steroid |
| **Topical calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) | Inhibit calcineurin → block **NFAT** → reduce T‑cell IL‑2 and cytokine production. **Steroid‑sparing**, ideal for **face, eyelids, and flexures** — precisely where PRP causes ectropion |

**Note:** Topicals alone are usually adequate only for **Type IV (circumscribed)** disease. In extensive or erythrodermic disease they are adjuncts.

### C. Systemic therapy — the first‑to‑third line ladder

#### **First line — Systemic retinoids** ⭐
**Agents:** **Isotretinoin** (~0.5–1 mg/kg/day), **acitretin** (~0.5–0.75 mg/kg/day), **alitretinoin** (emerging evidence; a pan‑RAR/RXR agonist, not yet universally accepted as first‑line).

**Why they are first line:** PRP is fundamentally a **disorder of keratinization**. Retinoids bind nuclear **RAR/RXR** receptors and directly **reprogram the keratinocyte's transcriptional program**: normalizing terminal differentiation, reducing hyperproliferation and follicular plugging, and exerting anti‑inflammatory effects. They target the *mechanism*, not just the inflammation. Response is typically seen over **weeks to a few months**, and therapy is often continued for 6–9 months.

**Analogy:** If the keratinocyte is a factory producing warped bricks at frantic speed, retinoids rewrite the factory's instruction manual.

**Cautions you must state:** **teratogenicity** (contraception essential — **3 years after acitretin**, 1 month after isotretinoin), **hyperlipidaemia**, **hepatotoxicity**, mucocutaneous dryness, **myalgia**, **pseudotumour cerebri** (avoid concurrent tetracyclines), **skeletal changes** with long‑term use in children, and mood effects. Monitor **lipids and LFTs**.

#### **Second line — Methotrexate, ustekinumab, cyclosporine, fumaric acid esters**

| Agent | Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Methotrexate** (7.5–25 mg weekly + folic acid) | Antifolate → inhibits **dihydrofolate reductase** and, at low dose, **AICAR transformylase** → adenosine release → anti‑inflammatory; suppresses activated lymphocytes | Well‑established; can be **combined with a retinoid** (with careful hepatic monitoring — the combination increases hepatotoxicity risk) |
| **Ustekinumab** | Monoclonal antibody against the **p40 subunit shared by IL‑12 and IL‑23** → blocks Th1 and **Th17** differentiation | Mechanistically the most elegant option: it **cuts the CARD14→NF‑κB→IL‑23→Th17 feedback loop** at the IL‑23 node. Impressive case‑series responses, sometimes dramatic. Arguably first‑line in retinoid‑refractory or *CARD14*‑mutant disease |
| **Cyclosporine** (2.5–5 mg/kg/day) | Calcineurin inhibitor → blocks **NFAT** → suppresses T‑cell IL‑2 | **Rapid onset** — useful for acute erythroderma as a bridge. Limited by **nephrotoxicity and hypertension**; not for long‑term use |
| **Fumaric acid esters** (dimethyl fumarate) | Activates **Nrf2** antioxidant pathway, inhibits **NF‑κB**, shifts Th1/Th17 → Th2 | Used mainly in **European** practice; GI upset and flushing are common; monitor lymphocyte count |

#### **Third line — Anti‑TNF agents, anti‑IL‑17, IVIG, azathioprine**

| Agent | Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Anti‑TNF‑α** (infliximab, etanercept, adalimumab) | Neutralize TNF‑α, a key amplifier upstream of the Th17 loop | Multiple case reports of success, including in erythroderma; screen for **latent TB and hepatitis B** |
| **Anti‑IL‑17** (secukinumab, ixekizumab) | Block IL‑17A — the **terminal effector cytokine** of the feed‑forward loop | Growing, encouraging evidence; mechanistically very rational. (Anti‑IL‑23 agents such as **guselkumab** are also being reported.) Watch for **candidiasis** and **IBD** flare |
| **IVIG** | Immunomodulation via Fc‑receptor blockade, cytokine and autoantibody neutralization | Reserved for severe, refractory, or immunosuppression‑contraindicated cases |
| **Azathioprine** | Purine analogue → inhibits lymphocyte proliferation | Slow onset; check **TPMT** activity first |

### D. Phototherapy and photopheresis — handle with care ⚠️

- **UVB phototherapy (NB‑UVB)** — an adjunctive option
- **Acitretin + UVA1** — **specifically noted for Type VI (HIV‑related PRP)**, where the retinoid normalizes keratinization while UVA1 suppresses dermal T‑cell activity
- **Extracorporeal photochemotherapy (photopheresis)** — an alternative modality; leucocytes are exposed ex vivo to 8‑MOP + UVA and reinfused, inducing apoptosis of pathogenic T cells and tolerogenic dendritic cell reprogramming. Occasional dramatic responses in refractory erythroderma

> ### ⚠️ Clinical Pearl — The Phototherapy Paradox
> **Unlike psoriasis, PRP may WORSEN with phototherapy**, because **photo‑aggravation is a recognized trigger**. This is one of the most clinically important reasons not to mislabel PRP as psoriasis.
>
> **How to act on it:** If you use phototherapy, start with **conservative, low doses**, escalate slowly, and **monitor closely for flares**. Formal **phototesting (MED)** before treatment is prudent. And if a "psoriasis" patient flares after every light session, stop and reconsider the diagnosis — think PRP.

### E. A pragmatic approach by type

| Scenario | Sensible approach |
|---|---|
| **Type IV (circumscribed juvenile)** | Emollients/keratolytics ± topical steroid, vitamin D analogue or retinoid; systemic therapy only if disabling |
| **Type I / III (classic)** | Explain likely remission; **systemic retinoid** for moderate–severe disease; methotrexate or ustekinumab if refractory |
| **Erythrodermic PRP** | **Admit**; supportive care; **cyclosporine** for rapid control or retinoid ± methotrexate; ustekinumab/anti‑TNF if refractory; eye care |
| **Type II / V (atypical, chronic)** | Long‑term keratolytics and emollients; low‑dose retinoid maintenance; consider **biologics**, especially with proven ***CARD14*** mutation |
| **Type VI (HIV)** | **Optimize ART** (necessary but usually insufficient alone) + **acitretin**, ± **UVA1**; use immunosuppressants with great caution and infection surveillance |

## 10. Common Misunderstandings — Let Me Clear These Up

1. **"PRP is a variant of psoriasis."** ❌ No. They **share a pathway** (*CARD14* → NF‑κB → IL‑23/Th17) but are distinct diseases with different clinical and histological signatures. Shared genetics ≠ same disease — think of them as two different cars built on the same chassis.

2. **"The scale is silvery like psoriasis."** ❌ It's **fine and bran‑like**, and the plaques are **orange**. Colour and scale quality are diagnostic.

3. **"Islands of sparing means the disease is improving."** ❌ They are a **structural feature of active disease**, reflecting patchy/asynchronous keratinization — the clinical counterpart of the histological checkerboard. Not a sign of recovery.

4. **"Type I means the mildest disease."** ❌ Type I is the **most common** and has the **best prognosis** — but it is also the type that most often becomes **erythrodermic**. Good prognosis ≠ mild presentation.

5. **"Type IV is rare because it's number four."** ❌ At **~25%**, Type IV is the **second most common type overall** and the most common in children. The numbering reflects Griffiths' descriptive scheme, not frequency.

6. **"Phototherapy is good for all papulosquamous disease."** ❌ **PRP can be photo‑aggravated.** This is the single highest‑yield practical distinction from psoriasis.

7. **"Nail pitting supports PRP."** ❌ **Pitting is psoriasis.** PRP nails are **thickened, yellow‑brown with subungual hyperkeratosis**.

8. **"Absent neutrophils is a minor histological detail."** ❌ It is one of the **most discriminating features**. Psoriasis = neutrophils (Munro, Kogoj). PRP = lymphohistiocytic only.

9. **"Griffith's classification."** ❌ Correctly **Griffiths classification** — after **W.A.D. Griffiths** (1980), no possessive apostrophe in standard usage. Type VI was a later addition (**Miralles et al., 1995**).

10. **"HIV testing is optional."** ❌ In adult‑onset PRP — particularly with spicules, follicular plugging, HS, or acne conglobata — HIV testing is **essential**, because Type VI changes everything.

11. **"A negative biopsy rules out PRP."** ❌ Features are **patchy and evolve**; erythrodermic skin shows only a **thinned epidermis with diminished granular layer**. **Multiple and repeat biopsies** may be needed, and the diagnosis remains **clinicopathological**.

## 11. Summary Anchor — The Core Idea in One Picture

> Picture a **switch jammed "on" inside the keratinocyte**.
>
> A **gain‑of‑function *CARD14* mutation** (or an acquired trigger — UV, drug, infection, trauma) locks **NF‑κB** into permanent activation. The keratinocyte becomes an inflammation factory, releasing **IL‑23**, which recruits **Th17 cells**, which release **IL‑17**, which drives the keratinocyte to proliferate faster and differentiate badly — and to release still more IL‑23. **A microphone screeching into its own speaker.**
>
> Because this disorder of cornification is **follicle‑centred and asynchronous**, the result is a disease you can read on two scales:
> — Under the microscope, patches of normal and abnormal cornification alternate in both planes → the **checkerboard**, with **shoulder parakeratosis**, **preserved granular layer**, and **no neutrophils**.
> — At the bedside, the same patchiness produces **islands of sparing** in a sea of **orange‑red, bran‑scaled** skin, studded with **nutmeg‑grater follicular papules**, marching **head to toe**, ending in **waxy "PRP sandals"**, **thick yellow‑brown nails**, and a face pulled into **ectropion**.
>
> **Griffiths sorts this into six types along one axis — genetic versus acquired:**
> **Acquired = classic = self‑limiting (I, III).** **Genetic = atypical = chronic (II, V).** **Circumscribed = IV.** **HIV = VI = worst.**
>
> And treatment simply follows the mechanism:
> **Soften the shell** (emollients, urea, salicylic acid) → **rewrite the keratinocyte's instructions** (retinoids, first line) → **cut the feedback cable** (methotrexate, ustekinumab, cyclosporine; then anti‑TNF, anti‑IL‑17, IVIG, azathioprine) → **support the failing organ** (erythroderma care, eye care) — while remembering two things above all: **Type I often burns out within 3 years, and light can make PRP worse.**

### 🎯 If You Remember Only Five Things

1. **Orange‑red plaques + bran‑like scale + islands of sparing + nutmeg‑grater follicular papules + waxy PPK = PRP.**
2. **Checkerboard ortho/parakeratosis, shoulder parakeratosis, preserved granular layer, NO neutrophils** = PRP histology; **Munro/Kogoj** = psoriasis.
3. **Griffiths I–VI**: I classic adult (80%, best prognosis, ~3 y), II atypical adult (chronic), III classic juvenile (1–2 y), IV circumscribed juvenile (25%, elbows/knees), V atypical juvenile (birth, ***CARD14***, scleroderma‑like palms), VI HIV (spicules, HS, ART‑resistant, poor).
4. **Systemic retinoids are first line**; ustekinumab/methotrexate/cyclosporine second; anti‑TNF, anti‑IL‑17, IVIG, azathioprine third.
5. **Test for HIV and thyroid disease, examine the eyes, and be cautious with phototherapy** — PRP is photo‑aggravated.
