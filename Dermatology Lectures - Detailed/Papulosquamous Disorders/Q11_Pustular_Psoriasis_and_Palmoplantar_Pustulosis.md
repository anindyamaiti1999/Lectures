# 🎓 Pustular Psoriasis — A Mini-Lecture

**Chapter:** Disorders of Keratinization | **Source:** pp. 166–168 | **Refs:** Rook's, Bolognia, Andrews'

---

## PART 1 — The Big Picture: Why This Topic Deserves Its Own Lecture

Let me start by setting the scene, because if you understand *one* idea today, this is the one.

When most people hear "psoriasis," they picture a well-demarcated, salmon-red plaque with thick silvery scale on an elbow or knee. That is **plaque psoriasis**, and it is fundamentally a disease of the **adaptive immune system** — dendritic cells present something to T cells, the T cells become Th17 cells, they pour out IL-17 and IL-22 under the direction of IL-23, and the keratinocytes respond by proliferating madly. Scale is the visible signature of *keratinocyte hyperproliferation*.

**Pustular psoriasis is a different animal.** Here the dominant player is the **innate immune system** — specifically, a runaway **IL-36 → neutrophil** amplification loop. And when neutrophils are the main actor, the visible signature is not scale but **pus**. So the morphology changes from "thick scaly plaque" to "sterile pinpoint pustule."

> **🔑 The core intuition:** *Plaque psoriasis is a T-cell disease that makes scale. Pustular psoriasis is a neutrophil disease that makes pus.* Same organ, different immunological engine — which is exactly why they respond to different drugs.

This distinction is not academic trivia. It is the reason a drug like **spesolimab** (an anti-IL-36-receptor antibody) can clear a generalized pustular flare in days but does nothing special for ordinary plaque disease. **Pathophysiology dictates pharmacology.** Keep that thread in your hand as we walk through everything else.

One more framing point before we dive in: pustular psoriasis is **rare**, but generalized pustular psoriasis is one of the few genuine **dermatological emergencies** — a disease that can kill a previously stable patient within days through fluid loss, sepsis, and multi-organ failure. Dermatology is often thought of as a "cold" specialty; this is one of the places where you may be the person standing between a patient and death. That is why we treat it with the seriousness of a burn.

## PART 2 — Definitions: Let's Be Precise About Words

**Pustular psoriasis** is a rare variant of psoriasis characterised by **sterile, pinpoint pustules on variably erythematous skin**.

Let's unpack each word, because each one is doing real work:

| Term | What it actually means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Pustule** | A visible, circumscribed elevation of skin containing pus (neutrophils) | This is the *clinical* unit of the disease |
| **Sterile** | No organisms grow on culture; Gram stain shows neutrophils but **no bacteria** | This is the diagnostic linchpin — it tells you the pus is *immunological*, not *infectious* |
| **Pinpoint** | 1–2 mm, not follicular, not centred on hair follicles | Distinguishes it from folliculitis, where pustules sit on hairs |
| **Variably erythematous** | The background skin ranges from near-normal to fiery red | The redder and more tender the background, the more acute and dangerous the flare |

**Why "sterile" is the whole ballgame:** Imagine two patients, both with sheets of pustules and a fever of 40 °C. One has staphylococcal pustulosis; the other has von Zumbusch GPP. In the first, antibiotics are life-saving and steroids are harmful. In the second, immunosuppression is life-saving and antibiotics alone let the patient deteriorate. **A Gram stain and culture is therefore not a formality — it is the fork in the road.**

*Analogy:* Think of pus as smoke. Smoke usually means fire (infection). But sometimes the smoke alarm itself is faulty and fills the room with smoke with no fire at all. Pustular psoriasis is a **faulty alarm** — the neutrophils have been recruited by a misfiring cytokine signal, not by a microbe. If you keep hunting for the fire, you'll miss that the alarm needs fixing.

## PART 3 — Classification: The Scaffold for Your Answer

Whenever an examiner asks you to "discuss" a condition, the first thing out of your pen should be a **classification**, because it proves you have a mental map. The standard first cut is by **extent**: generalized versus localized.

### 🅰️ Generalized forms
1. **Acute generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP) — von Zumbusch type**
2. **Subacute annular GPP** (annular pustular psoriasis)
3. **Exanthematic GPP**
4. **Localized GPP** (an odd-sounding but real category — see note below)
5. **Impetigo herpetiformis** (pustular psoriasis of pregnancy)

### 🅱️ Localized forms
1. **Palmoplantar pustulosis (PPP)**
2. **Acrodermatitis continua of Hallopeau (ACH)**

**Two clarifications students trip over:**

- **"Localized GPP"** sounds like a contradiction. It refers to the situation where the *generalized-type* pustular reaction — the von Zumbusch mechanism, with lakes of pus on inflamed skin — appears **confined to pre-existing plaques or to a limited region**, without generalization. Think of it as the same machinery running at lower volume. It matters because these patients can progress to full GPP.
- **"Exanthematic GPP"** is a widespread crop of small sterile pustules, characteristically **triggered by an infection (classically streptococcal) or a drug**, which appears abruptly and then resolves without the relentless waves of von Zumbusch disease. Its clinical overlap with **AGEP (acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis)** is genuinely difficult, sometimes impossible, to resolve — the histology is similar, and the distinction often rests on personal/family history of psoriasis, the presence of psoriatic nail or joint disease, and the tempo of resolution. The **ERASPEN** (European Rare and Severe Psoriasis Expert Network) consensus retains it as a GPP subtype, which is why your source lists it.

*Analogy for the classification:* Think of the IL-36/neutrophil engine as a fire. **Von Zumbusch** is a house fire — everything burning at once, life-threatening. **Annular GPP** is a slow grass fire with a creeping, ring-shaped burning edge. **Exanthematic GPP** is a flash of sparks after a specific spark-source (infection/drug). **PPP** is a persistent smouldering ember confined to the palms and soles. **ACH** is a smouldering ember confined to the fingertips — small, but destructive enough to eat the nail and even the bone beneath it.

## PART 4 — Generalized Pustular Psoriasis (GPP): The Main Event

### 4.1 Definition and Behaviour

GPP is a **rare inflammatory skin disorder characterised by primarily sterile, macroscopic pustules on non-acral skin.**

Notice two deliberate words:
- **Macroscopic** — you can see these pustules with the naked eye, unlike the *microscopic* Munro microabscesses of ordinary plaque psoriasis (every plaque psoriasis biopsy has micro-collections of neutrophils; that does not make it pustular psoriasis).
- **Non-acral** — the trunk, flexures, and proximal limbs, as opposed to the palms and soles (acral), which is PPP territory. This one word separates the two ends of the spectrum.

**Natural history:** GPP characteristically behaves as **acute flares on a chronic remitting–relapsing background**. It may arise *de novo* in someone who has never had psoriasis, or it may erupt out of long-standing plaque psoriasis, or evolve from ACH or PPP. That heterogeneity in origin mirrors a heterogeneity in genetics — which we'll see shortly.

### 4.2 Epidemiology

| Parameter | Value | Teaching point |
|---|---|---|
| **Sex ratio** | **Women : Men = 2 : 1** | Female predominance — partly explained by the pregnancy-associated variant |
| **Typical age of onset** | **40–59 years** | Middle age is the peak |
| **Other age groups** | Juvenile and infantile cases occur | Early-onset disease is the group most enriched for *IL36RN* mutations, often **without** any plaque psoriasis |

That last row is worth a highlighter. **The younger the patient and the less plaque psoriasis they have, the more likely you are dealing with a monogenic autoinflammatory disease rather than "psoriasis that turned pustular."** That single heuristic will guide your genetic testing and your choice of biologic.

### 4.3 Pathophysiology — The IL-36 Loop, Step by Step

This is the heart of the lecture. Let's build it slowly.

**Step 0 — The normal design.** The IL-36 family (IL-36α, IL-36β, IL-36γ) are IL-1-family cytokines made by keratinocytes. They are the skin's **"alarm cytokines"**: when the epidermis is stressed or breached, IL-36 is released, binds the **IL-36 receptor** on keratinocytes and dendritic cells, and calls in neutrophils and amplifies inflammation. This is useful — it's how skin defends itself.

But an alarm that cannot be switched off is a catastrophe. So nature built a brake: **IL-36Ra**, the IL-36 *receptor antagonist*, encoded by the gene **IL36RN**. IL-36Ra binds the same receptor but transmits **no signal** — it simply occupies the seat so IL-36 cannot sit in it.

> *Analogy:* The IL-36 receptor is a car's **accelerator pedal**. IL-36 is a **foot pressing it**. IL-36Ra is the **foot-brake**. In health, accelerator and brake are balanced, and the car (inflammation) moves only when needed.

**Step 1 — The mutation.** In many GPP patients, **loss-of-function mutations in *IL36RN*** mean the brake is missing or broken. This condition even has its own name in the autoinflammatory literature: **DITRA — Deficiency of the Interleukin-36 Receptor Antagonist.** Inheritance in these families often follows a **Mendelian (typically autosomal recessive)** pattern, which is why family history and consanguinity matter.

**Step 2 — Unopposed signalling.** With no brake, **any** IL-36 released by stressed keratinocytes produces a full-throttle response. → **Unopposed IL-36 signalling.**

**Step 3 — Innate immune activation.** IL-36 signalling drives keratinocytes and dendritic cells to release **TNF-α, IL-1, IL-8/CXCL8 and other chemokines**, plus antimicrobial peptides.

**Step 4 — Neutrophil recruitment.** Those chemokines pull **neutrophils** out of the papillary dermal capillaries and drive them *upward* through the epidermis. Clinically, that upward migration is the pustule. Histologically, it is the Kogoj pustule (we'll get there).

**Step 5 — The autoinflammatory loop closes.** Arriving neutrophils release **proteases (cathepsin G, elastase, proteinase-3)** which **cleave and activate more IL-36** — and IL-36 precursors require exactly such proteolytic cleavage to become potent. More active IL-36 → more chemokines → more neutrophils → more proteases → more active IL-36.

> **This is a positive feedback loop with no negative regulator.** That is the definition of an **autoinflammatory** disease, and it explains three clinical facts at once: (1) why flares escalate explosively over hours to days, (2) why the disease has a fever and a raging acute-phase response even though nothing is infected, and (3) why blocking the IL-36 receptor pharmacologically (spesolimab) shuts the whole thing down so dramatically — you are reinstalling the missing brake.

**Other genes in the story:**

| Gene | Protein / pathway | Clinical association |
|---|---|---|
| ***IL36RN*** | IL-36 receptor antagonist (the brake) | **Commonest**; especially **early-onset GPP without plaque psoriasis**; DITRA |
| ***AP1S3*** | Adaptor protein complex 1, σ3 subunit — vesicular trafficking, impairs autophagy and TLR-3 signalling → excess IL-1/IL-36 | GPP, ACH, PPP; often in European patients |
| ***CARD14*** | Caspase recruitment domain 14 — a keratinocyte scaffold that activates **NF-κB** | Gain-of-function → **CAMPS** (CARD14-mediated psoriasis); GPP, pityriasis rubra pilaris-like disease, and familial plaque psoriasis (*PSORS2*) |

Notice the unifying theme: **every one of these genes, when mutated, ends up over-activating innate/NF-κB-driven inflammation in the keratinocyte.** Different doors, same room.

### 4.4 Environmental Triggers — and the Mechanism Behind Each

Genetics loads the gun; the environment pulls the trigger. Memorise this list, but more importantly, understand *why* each item is on it.

| Trigger | Mechanism / comment |
|---|---|
| **Infection** (esp. streptococcal, also viral) | Pathogen-associated molecular patterns activate innate immunity → IL-36 release. Classic precipitant of exanthematic GPP |
| **Withdrawal of systemic corticosteroids** ⚠️ | **The classic iatrogenic precipitant.** Steroids suppress the loop; sudden withdrawal releases rebound inflammation. *Analogy:* sitting on the lid of a boiling pot, then jumping off |
| **Irritant topical therapy** — **coal tar, dithranol (anthralin)** | Paradoxical: traditional *anti*-psoriatic agents. They are **keratinocyte irritants**; irritation → keratinocyte stress → IL-36 release → pustulation in a primed host. **This is why you must avoid tar and dithranol in pustular disease** |
| **Hypocalcaemia** (and hypoparathyroidism) | Calcium is a master regulator of keratinocyte differentiation; low calcium destabilises the differentiation programme. Historically linked with impetigo herpetiformis. Also arises *as a consequence* of GPP — a vicious circle |
| **Pregnancy** | The basis of **impetigo herpetiformis**; hormonal shifts (high progesterone, relative late-pregnancy hypocalcaemia/hypoparathyroidism) |
| **Stress** | Neuro-immune activation (substance P, NGF) lowering the threshold for flare |
| **Biologics** — **ustekinumab, infliximab, adalimumab** | **Paradoxical pustular psoriasis.** With TNF blockade, the leading explanation is unopposed **type I interferon** production by plasmacytoid dendritic cells, which drives a neutrophil-rich pustular phenotype. An exam favourite because it is counter-intuitive: *the drug that treats psoriasis can cause pustular psoriasis* |

> **⚠️ Practical corollary:** Never treat a psoriatic patient with a tapering course of oral prednisolone "just to settle things." That habit is the single most preventable cause of von Zumbusch GPP. And when a patient on a TNF inhibitor develops pustules, think paradoxical reaction before you think infection.

### 4.5 Long-Term Complications of Chronic GPP

- **Secondary (AA) amyloidosis** — years of an unrelenting acute-phase response means years of elevated **serum amyloid A**, which deposits in tissues (kidney, gut, liver). Presents as proteinuria/nephrotic syndrome. This is the same mechanism as amyloidosis in chronic rheumatoid arthritis or familial Mediterranean fever, and it is a powerful argument for **controlling inflammation durably**, not just treating flares.
- **Telogen effluvium** — a severe systemic insult (high fever, catabolic state, hypoalbuminaemia) pushes many anagen follicles synchronously into telogen; diffuse shedding follows **2–3 months** after the flare. Reassure the patient: it is **non-scarring and self-limiting**.

## PART 5 — Clinical Subtypes of GPP

### 5.1 🔴 Von Zumbusch Type (Acute GPP) — The Emergency

This is the **most severe form**, defined by **repeated waves of erythema and pustulation**. It is both **clinically and genetically heterogeneous** — some patients are *IL36RN*-mutant with no prior psoriasis; others have had plaque psoriasis for twenty years; still others evolve from **ACH** or **PPP**. Three roads, one destination.

#### The clinical sequence — learn this as a story, not a list

```
① Burning/tenderness of the skin (often the earliest symptom — before anything is visible)
        ↓
② High fever + severe malaise (systemic inflammation precedes/accompanies the rash)
        ↓
③ Pre-existing plaques become FIERY RED, tender, oedematous ("angry" psoriasis)
        ↓
④ Crops of PINPOINT STERILE PUSTULES erupt on this red skin,
   coalescing into "LAKES OF PUS"
        ↓
⑤ Pustulation spreads onto previously UNINVOLVED skin,
   favouring FLEXURES and GENITAL AREA
        ↓
⑥ GENERALIZED ERYTHRODERMA with sheets of superficial desquamation
        ↓
⑦ Wave subsides over days → skin peels → and then the next wave begins
```

Two details to hold on to:

- **"Lakes of pus"** — when adjacent pinpoint pustules coalesce beneath the thin, detachable stratum corneum, they form broad, flat, sometimes annular or serpiginous collections that can be wiped away. This is a near-pathognomonic clinical description and a beautiful viva phrase.
- **The relapsing wave pattern.** Von Zumbusch is not one event; it is a series. Just as the skin begins to clear, a new burning sensation and fever herald the next crop. Warn the patient and the ward team, or they will think treatment has failed.

#### Japanese Diagnostic Criteria (the formal framework)

Use these to give your answer structure:

1. **Systemic symptoms** — fever, fatigue
2. **Multiple sterile pustules merging to form lakes of pus** on erythematous skin
3. **Kogoj's spongiform pustule** on histopathology
4. **Repeated recurrences** of the above

*(In the full Japanese severity system these features are supplemented by the characteristic laboratory abnormalities; for exam purposes, the four above are the quotable core.)*

#### Nail and mucosal involvement

- **Nails:** pustules form **under the nail plate (subungual)**; the accumulating pus lifts the plate off its bed. Result: **thickened, dystrophic nails that may detach entirely (onycholysis → anonychia)**. Mechanistically identical to the skin lesion — it just happens to be occurring under a rigid keratin roof, which is why it is so painful and so destructive.
- **Mucosae:** the buccal mucosa and tongue may be involved, classically producing **geographic tongue (benign migratory glossitis)** — migratory, map-like red patches with white serpiginous borders, seen in roughly 10% of GPP patients (and also associated with plaque psoriasis). Histologically it is, in essence, "psoriasis of the tongue," complete with neutrophilic microabscesses.

#### Systemic effects — why this patient belongs in hospital

Now let's reason through each one, because if you understand the mechanism you will never forget the list.

| Systemic effect | Mechanism (cause → effect → outcome) |
|---|---|
| **Hypovolaemia** | Erythroderma + loss of the stratum corneum barrier → massive **transepidermal water loss** and cutaneous vasodilatation → intravascular volume depletion → tachycardia, hypotension |
| **Hypoalbuminaemia** | Two hits: **protein exudation** through the denuded surface, plus **reduced hepatic albumin synthesis** as the liver switches to acute-phase protein production. → Oedema, and altered pharmacokinetics of albumin-bound drugs |
| **Acute kidney injury (AKI)** | Hypovolaemia → renal hypoperfusion → **pre-renal azotaemia**; may become acute tubular necrosis if unrecognised |
| **Deranged LFTs / cholestatic jaundice** | Neutrophilic cholangitis — the same sterile neutrophilic process occurring in bile ducts. Reversible with disease control |
| **Aseptic pneumonitis → ARDS** | Neutrophils infiltrate the lung interstitium in the absence of infection → hypoxia, diffuse infiltrates on imaging. A recognised **cause of death** in GPP |
| **Hypocalcaemia** | Follows hypoalbuminaemia (reduced protein-bound calcium) and true losses; also a *trigger* — so it both causes and results from the disease |
| **High-output cardiac failure** | Erythroderma = an enormous cutaneous shunt. The heart must dramatically increase output to perfuse dilated skin vessels; in an elderly or cardiac-compromised patient this precipitates decompensation |
| **Sepsis** | The barrier is gone. Although the pustules are sterile, the **denuded skin is an open door** to *Staphylococcus aureus* and Gram-negatives. **Secondary sepsis is the commonest cause of death** |

> *Analogy:* **Think of von Zumbusch GPP as a burn without the burn.** The skin has lost its function as a waterproof, protein-retaining, thermoregulating, microbe-excluding wall. Every complication above follows automatically from that single fact. Manage the patient the way you'd manage a major burn, and you will not go far wrong.

**Complications list to quote:** hypocalcaemia, erythroderma, renal failure, high-output cardiac failure, secondary infection/sepsis, ARDS, and — in the pregnant patient — placental insufficiency, stillbirth and neonatal death.

### 5.2 🤰 Impetigo Herpetiformis (Pustular Psoriasis of Pregnancy)

First, dismantle the name, because it is doubly misleading — a classic exam trap.

> **⚠️ Common misunderstanding:** Impetigo herpetiformis has **nothing to do with impetigo** (it is not staphylococcal/streptococcal) and **nothing to do with herpes** (no HSV). It is a historical name. "Impetigo" was once used loosely for any pustular eruption, and "herpetiformis" simply means "grouped, like herpes." **It is generalized pustular psoriasis occurring in pregnancy** — full stop.

**Key features:**
- Occurs in **pregnancy, characteristically the last trimester** (though earlier onset is described), and typically **resolves after delivery** — but may recur in subsequent pregnancies, often earlier and more severely, and can be precipitated by the oral contraceptive pill.
- **Morphology and evolution:** begins in the **inguinal and other flexural areas** (axillae, submammary folds, umbilicus) with erythematous patches studded at the margin by pustules → **centrifugal spread** onto the trunk and limbs, with central crusting and fresh pustules at the advancing edge → **severe constitutional symptoms** (fever, malaise, delirium, diarrhoea, arthralgia).
- **Laboratory:** leucocytosis, raised ESR/CRP, hypoalbuminaemia, and characteristically **hypocalcaemia** (sometimes with hypoparathyroidism and low vitamin D).

**Why it is feared — the maternal–fetal axis:** the mother is systemically inflamed, volume-depleted, hypoalbuminaemic and often febrile. That physiology compromises uteroplacental perfusion. → **Placental insufficiency** → **intrauterine growth restriction, stillbirth, and neonatal death**, as well as maternal **cardiac and renal failure**. This is why obstetric input is mandatory from day one, with fetal monitoring, and why **early delivery or termination of pregnancy** must be considered if maternal life is threatened. Delivery is, in effect, the definitive treatment.

### 5.3 ⭕ Subacute Annular Generalized Pustular Psoriasis

The gentle cousin — and the **paediatric** one.

- **Age:** most common in **infancy and childhood**, sometimes congenital or within the first year of life. Rare overall.
- **Morphology:** **persistent annular (ring-shaped) lesions** — and here is the detail that gives it away: **pustules sit on the advancing (expanding) edge, leaving a trailing scale and central clearing behind.**
- **Systemic symptoms:** typically **absent or mild** — no high fever, no multi-organ involvement.
- **Course:** may remain **localized to the flexures for long periods**; **good prognosis**.

*Why the ring?* Picture a wave of neutrophil recruitment spreading outward through the epidermis like a ripple on a pond. The active inflammatory front is where the pustules are; behind the front, inflammation has already burnt out and the skin is left with the residue of the wave — the **trailing scale**. Once you see the lesion as a *moving front*, the morphology becomes obvious rather than something to memorise.

> **⚠️ Don't confuse this with tinea corporis** (also an annular lesion with an active scaly edge) **or with erythema annulare centrifugum.** Your discriminators: pustules *on the rim*, absence of fungal hyphae on KOH, and a biopsy showing Kogoj pustules.

---

### 5.4 ⚡ Exanthematic GPP

A brief but important entity: **abrupt onset of numerous small sterile pustules over the trunk and limbs**, typically **following an infection (classically streptococcal) or a drug**, with **less severe systemic upset** than von Zumbusch, and **resolution without the repeated waves.** Its major differential is **AGEP**, which shares the abrupt drug-triggered onset, the pin-head pustules, and the flexural start. Clues favouring exanthematic GPP over AGEP: personal/family history of psoriasis, psoriatic nails or arthritis, a longer time-course, and histology with more psoriasiform acanthosis. In practice, the two overlap so much that some authorities view AGEP as sitting on the same IL-36 spectrum — and indeed *IL36RN* variants have been found in some AGEP patients.

## PART 6 — Investigations in GPP (and the Reasoning Behind Each)

Never list investigations without saying *why*. Here is the logic:

| Investigation | What you are looking for | Why it changes management |
|---|---|---|
| **Gram stain + culture of pustule contents** | **Sterile pus — neutrophils with no organisms** | The single most important test. Confirms the pus is immunological, and excludes staphylococcal pustulosis/impetigo |
| **CBC** | **Leucocytosis with neutrophilia**, sometimes lymphopenia and anaemia of inflammation | Reflects severity; a rising count with new symptoms may also flag secondary sepsis |
| **CRP and ESR** | Markedly raised | Objective severity markers; useful for tracking response |
| **LFT** | Transaminitis, raised ALP/bilirubin — **cholestatic jaundice** | Signals hepatic involvement; also mandatory before methotrexate/acitretin |
| **RFT + electrolytes** | Raised urea/creatinine (AKI), **hypocalcaemia**, hypokalaemia | Directs fluid and electrolyte replacement; guides cyclosporine/methotrexate safety |
| **Serum albumin** | Hypoalbuminaemia | Marker of severity and protein loss; guides nutritional support |
| **Blood cultures** | Secondary bacteraemia | Because sepsis is the leading cause of death |
| **Chest X-ray** | Infiltrates of **aseptic pneumonitis / ARDS** | Distinguishes sterile neutrophilic pneumonitis from pneumonia; hypoxia = ICU |
| **Skin biopsy** | **Kogoj spongiform pustule** (see next section) | Confirms the diagnosis; part of the Japanese criteria |
| **KOH mount / fungal culture** | Excludes dermatophyte and candidal pustulosis | Especially in flexural and annular presentations |
| **Genetic testing** (*IL36RN*, *AP1S3*, *CARD14*) | Monogenic autoinflammatory disease | Most useful in **early-onset GPP without plaque psoriasis**; supports IL-36-directed therapy and family counselling |
| **Pregnancy test / obstetric assessment** | Impetigo herpetiformis | Completely changes the drug choices (no retinoids!) |

## PART 7 — Histopathology: Kogoj versus Munro (Get This Exactly Right)

This is the single most commonly muddled pair in the whole chapter, so let's slow right down.

### The features you will see

1. **Parakeratosis** — retained nuclei in the stratum corneum, because keratinocytes are moving upward so fast they never complete terminal differentiation (they don't have time to shed their nuclei).
2. **Acanthosis with elongation of the rete ridges** — regular downward extension of the epidermal ridges, the psoriasiform architecture.
3. **Spongiform pustule of Kogoj** — the defining lesion (below).
4. **Munro microabscess** — the sequel (below).
5. **Upper dermis:** infiltration by **lymphocytes and neutrophils**, with dilated, tortuous papillary capillaries.

### The vertical journey of the neutrophil — one continuous story

```
Papillary dermal capillary
        │  (chemokines: IL-8/CXCL8, driven by unopposed IL-36)
        ▼
Neutrophils exit the vessel and migrate UP into the epidermis
        │
        ▼
STRATUM SPINOSUM → they accumulate in the widened (spongiotic)
                    intercellular spaces between keratinocytes
                 = ★ SPONGIFORM PUSTULE OF KOGOJ ★
        │  (the keratinocyte network is stretched and degenerates
        │   into a thin sponge-like meshwork around the neutrophils)
        ▼
They continue to migrate upward; as they approach/reach the
cornified layer they die and become PYKNOTIC (shrunken, dense nuclei)
        │
        ▼
STRATUM CORNEUM → aggregate of pyknotic neutrophils
                 = ★ MUNRO MICROABSCESS ★
        │
        ▼
Coalescence of many such collections beneath a thin horny layer
= the clinically visible MACROSCOPIC PUSTULE ("lake of pus")
```

> **🔑 Mnemonic:** **"S for Spinosum = Spongiform Kogoj."** Then **Munro goes up and out** to the corneum. Alphabetically and anatomically: **K** before **M**, spinosum before corneum.

### ⚠️ An important correction to the source text

Your source reads: *"Spongiform pustule of Kogoj — a **macropustule** forming through migration of neutrophils..."* and *"...they become pyknotic and assume the appearance of a large **Munro abscess**."*

**Please read this carefully, because the wording is misleading:**

- The **Kogoj pustule is a microscopic histological structure**, visible only on the slide. It is called a "spongiform pustule" because the neutrophils sit within a spongy meshwork of degenerating keratinocytes — *not* because it is a macroscopic clinical pustule.
- It is described as comparatively "macro" **only relative to the tiny Munro microabscess**, and only in the sense that it is a larger, better-formed collection than a few scattered neutrophils. The clinically visible pustule arises when **many** of these microscopic collections coalesce beneath the thin, parakeratotic stratum corneum.
- The correct term for the corneal collection is **Munro microabscess** (not "large Munro abscess").

**Bottom line to write in your exam:** ***Kogoj = spongiform pustule in the stratum spinosum; Munro = microabscess in the stratum corneum.*** Both are hallmarks of psoriasis, but in **pustular** psoriasis they are large, numerous and confluent, whereas in plaque psoriasis they are small and focal. **It is a difference of degree, not of kind** — which is a lovely illustration of why the two diseases live on one spectrum.

### 🔬 A crucial histological contrast: GPP versus PPP

Bank this comparison now, because it is a high-yield discriminator:

| | **GPP** | **PPP** |
|---|---|---|
| **Site of pustule formation** | Within the **spongiotic epidermis** (Kogoj mechanism), arising from capillary-derived neutrophils | Around and within the **acrosyringium** — the intraepidermal **eccrine sweat duct** — as vesicles that evolve into pustules |
| **Spongiosis** | Present (it defines the Kogoj lesion) | Characteristically **absent/minimal** |
| **Pustule contents** | Predominantly **neutrophils** | **Mononuclear cells** *and* neutrophils |
| **Dermal infiltrate** | Lymphocytes and neutrophils | **T-lymphocytes, eosinophils and mast cells** |

*Why the sweat duct?* The acrosyringium is the weak point in the thick acral epidermis and a site of dense antimicrobial peptide expression and IL-36γ production — it is the natural focus for neutrophil accumulation where the horny layer is otherwise impenetrably thick. This anatomical fact is one of the arguments used by those who consider PPP a distinct entity rather than simply psoriasis on the palms.

## PART 8 — Management of GPP

Organise your answer in three tiers: **general/supportive → topical → systemic.** And say out loud, at the start, that **acute GPP is a dermatological emergency requiring in-patient care.**

### 8.1 General Measures — "Treat It Like a Burn"

| Measure | Reasoning |
|---|---|
| **In-patient admission** (ICU/HDU if erythrodermic, hypoxic, or haemodynamically unstable) | Multi-organ risk, need for hourly monitoring |
| **Maintain a warm ambient temperature** | Erythroderma abolishes thermoregulation: vasodilated skin loses heat rapidly, and shivering adds a catabolic burden. Warm the room, not the patient's skin with heaters |
| **Strict input/output charting; fluid and electrolyte correction** | Transepidermal water loss is enormous; correct hypovolaemia, hypokalaemia, and especially **hypocalcaemia** — remembering that hypocalcaemia is both a cause and consequence |
| **Nutritional support — high-protein diet ± supplementation** | Massive protein loss through the skin plus a hypercatabolic state; hypoalbuminaemia impairs healing |
| **Monitor for and treat secondary infection with antibiotics** | Culture skin, blood and urine. Treat **proven or strongly suspected** infection — but do not reflexively give antibiotics for the sterile pustules themselves |
| **Analgesia** | The skin is genuinely painful; untreated pain adds to the stress-driven flare |
| **Bed rest, bland emollient wet dressings, non-adherent dressings** | Comfort and barrier restoration |
| **Obstetric co-management; consider early delivery or termination** in the pregnant patient if maternal life is threatened | Delivery removes the driver and may be definitive |
| **Identify and remove the trigger** | Was a steroid stopped? Was tar applied? Is there a streptococcal throat? Was a TNF inhibitor started? **Removing the trigger is treatment** |

### 8.2 Topical Therapy

- **Bland creams and lotions / emollients** — restore the barrier, reduce water loss, soothe.
- **Mild to mid-potency topical corticosteroids** — reduce local inflammation without the systemic absorption risk of superpotent agents over a huge surface area.
- **⛔ AVOID coal tar and dithranol (anthralin).** Say this explicitly in an exam. These are irritants; irritation stresses keratinocytes; stressed keratinocytes release IL-36; in a patient without a functioning IL-36 brake, you have just poured petrol on the fire. **The same drugs that help plaque psoriasis can worsen pustular psoriasis.** Similarly, be cautious with **phototherapy during an acute pustular flare** (it is an option in the stable/clearing phase, not in the acute storm).

### 8.3 Systemic Therapy

| Agent | Dose / role | Key reasoning and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Acitretin (retinoid)** — **FIRST LINE** | **1 mg/kg/day** for severe disease; **0.5–0.75 mg/kg/day** for maintenance | Normalises keratinocyte differentiation and is **directly anti-neutrophilic** — the ideal mechanism for a neutrophilic dermatosis. Rapid onset (days). ⛔ **Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy** — teratogenic, and because of its conversion to etretinate, **pregnancy must be avoided for 3 years (and alcohol avoided) after stopping**. Monitor lipids and LFTs |
| **Cyclosporine** | 2.5–5 mg/kg/day | **Achieves rapid control** — the fastest of the conventional agents. Calcineurin inhibition suppresses T-cell-derived cytokines that feed the loop. **Suitable/usable in pregnancy** (see note below) when retinoids are barred. Monitor BP and renal function |
| **Systemic corticosteroids** | **Prednisolone 30–40 mg/day, then taper** | **Drug of choice in impetigo herpetiformis.** Otherwise **use with caution** — steroids are themselves a classic cause of rebound GPP on withdrawal. A paradox worth stating explicitly in an exam (see discussion below) |
| **Methotrexate** | 7.5–25 mg/week with folic acid | Useful for maintenance and in those with concomitant psoriatic arthritis; slower onset (4–8 weeks). Avoid in pregnancy; beware hypoalbuminaemia (increases free drug), renal impairment, and hepatotoxicity |
| **Biologics** | Infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol (anti-TNF); ustekinumab (anti-IL-12/23); secukinumab (anti-IL-17A); **spesolimab (anti-IL-36 receptor)** | **Anti-TNF agents (especially infliximab)** act fastest among the older biologics and are favoured in severe acute flares. **Certolizumab** is the anti-TNF of choice in pregnancy (minimal placental transfer, as it lacks an Fc portion). **Spesolimab** is the mechanism-specific answer — see below |

### 8.4 Three Therapeutic Points Worth Dwelling On

**① Spesolimab — mechanism-based therapy, and a beautiful story.**
Spesolimab (Spevigo®) is a monoclonal antibody against the **IL-36 receptor**, FDA-approved in 2022 for **flares of GPP**. Think back to our accelerator/brake analogy: in DITRA, the brake (IL-36Ra) is missing. Spesolimab does not replace the brake — it **blocks the accelerator pedal itself** by occupying the receptor, so IL-36 has nothing to signal through. In the pivotal trial (**Bachelez et al., NEJM** — EFFISAYIL), a **single intravenous dose** produced pustule clearance in a large proportion of patients **within one week**. This is the payoff for understanding pathophysiology: identify the broken regulator, then drug the pathway it regulates. Note that it works even in patients *without* demonstrable *IL36RN* mutations, because the IL-36 loop is the common final pathway regardless of which upstream gene is at fault.

**② Corticosteroids in impetigo herpetiformis — resolving the apparent contradiction.**
Students rightly ask: *"You told me steroid withdrawal causes GPP. Why are steroids the drug of choice in pustular psoriasis of pregnancy?"* Excellent question. The answer has three parts:
- **Retinoids are absolutely contraindicated** in pregnancy, and cyclosporine and methotrexate each carry their own concerns — so the pharmacological options are narrow.
- **Rapid control is imperative**, because the risks are not merely cosmetic: placental insufficiency, stillbirth and neonatal death are on the table. Steroids act fast.
- **The condition is self-limiting after delivery.** This is the key. The rebound risk of steroid withdrawal arises when you taper while the underlying disease driver is still running. In impetigo herpetiformis, **delivery removes the driver**, so a post-partum taper is far safer than a taper in a chronic von Zumbusch patient.
> So the rule is not "steroids are forbidden in pustular psoriasis." The rule is: **"Steroids are dangerous when you will have to withdraw them while the disease is still active."** Understand the principle and the apparent contradiction dissolves.

**③ Cyclosporine in pregnancy.**
Your source says cyclosporine "achieves rapid control; suitable in pregnancy." To be precise: cyclosporine is **FDA pregnancy category C**. It **does cross the placenta**, but extensive experience — largely from transplant recipients — shows **no consistent major teratogenic signal**, though there are associations with prematurity and low birth weight. It is therefore an **accepted option in severe GPP/impetigo herpetiformis when the benefit outweighs the risk**, particularly when steroids are being tapered or are insufficient, and when retinoids are barred. Use it with obstetric co-management, blood pressure and renal monitoring.

## PART 9 — Palmoplantar Pustulosis (PPP): The Stubborn Localized Form

### 9.1 Names and Definition

Also called **chronic palmoplantar pustular psoriasis**, **pustulosis palmaris et plantaris**, and **persistent palmoplantar pustulosis**.

**Definition:** a **chronic condition characterised by multiple sterile pustules on the palms and/or soles, often accompanied by erythema and scaling, and notoriously highly resistant to treatment.**

Note the two-word clinical essence: **chronic** and **resistant**. Unlike von Zumbusch, PPP will not kill your patient — but it will frustrate you both for years, and it can be genuinely disabling.

### 9.2 Is PPP Even Psoriasis? (The Honest Answer)

This is worth mentioning in a good exam answer, because it shows sophistication. There is a real, ongoing debate:

**Arguments that PPP *is* psoriasis:** association with the **PSORS1** locus (6p21.3) and **HLA-C\*06:02**, co-occurrence with plaque psoriasis in a substantial minority, and shared IL-17 pathway biology.

**Arguments that PPP is a *distinct* entity:** a different epidemiology (older, strongly female, overwhelmingly **smokers**), a different histology (**acrosyringeal** origin, no spongiosis, mononuclear cells in the pustule, eosinophils and mast cells in the dermis), a different treatment response profile, and — importantly — it **lacks the genetic associations typical of GPP**; classic *IL36RN* biallelic loss-of-function is not the PPP story.

The pragmatic modern position: PPP sits within the **broader pustular psoriasis spectrum**, sharing the IL-36/IL-17 neutrophilic axis, but with its own distinctive drivers — above all **smoking** and the **eccrine sweat apparatus**.

### 9.3 Epidemiology and Associations

- May occur **in isolation** or **with plaque psoriasis** elsewhere.
- **Age 30–50 years; Women > Men** — a strikingly female-predominant disease.
- **Lacks the Mendelian genetic associations of GPP.**

### 9.4 Etiopathogenesis — and Why Smoking Sits at the Centre

**Genetic background (susceptibility, not Mendelian causation):**
- **PSORS1** and **HLA-C\*06:02** — linking PPP to the psoriasis susceptibility spectrum
- ***IL36RN*** variants — reported, but far less central than in GPP
- ***CARD14*** variants — NF-κB over-activation in keratinocytes
- ***ATG16L1*** — an **autophagy** gene (*Autophagy-related 16-like 1*), best known for its Crohn's disease association. Defective autophagy means damaged organelles and protein aggregates are not cleared → inflammasome activation → IL-1/IL-36 family release. This elegantly links PPP to the **gut inflammation** phenotype
- **IL-19 family polymorphisms** — the IL-19 subfamily (IL-19, IL-20, IL-24) are IL-10-family cytokines that act **on keratinocytes** to drive psoriasiform proliferation and antimicrobial peptide production

**Triggers:**
- **Smoking** ⚠️ — the strongest and most clinically actionable association (>80% of patients in many series are current or ex-smokers)
- **Medications** — **TNF-α inhibitors and IL-17 inhibitors** (again, paradoxical pustulosis)
- **Rheumatoid arthritis** and **Crohn's disease** — shared inflammatory and autophagy biology
- **Infections** (including focal dental/tonsillar foci — the basis of the Japanese practice of tonsillectomy for refractory PPP)
- **Altered skin microbiome**

**The cytokine signature of lesional PPP:** antimicrobial peptides plus **IL-18, IL-17A, IL-22, IL-36γ and IL-36Ra** — i.e. an **IL-17 pathway** activated alongside an **IL-36 axis**.

**⭐ The smoking mechanism — connect these dots, because it is the most satisfying part of the PPP story:**

```
Nicotine binds acetylcholine receptors densely expressed on the
ACROSYRINGIUM (intraepidermal eccrine sweat duct) of palms and soles
        ↓
Duct epithelial stress/dysfunction + cigarette smoke as an
        aryl-hydrocarbon-receptor ligand
        ↓
Up-regulation of IL-36γ and antimicrobial peptides at the duct
        ↓
Neutrophil chemotaxis INTO and AROUND the sweat duct
        ↓
Vesicle at the acrosyringium → evolves into a sterile PUSTULE
        ↓
Chronic, relapsing, treatment-resistant palmoplantar pustulosis
```

> **Therefore: smoking cessation is a therapeutic intervention, not a lifestyle suggestion.** It should be your *first* prescription in PPP, discussed before any drug. Be honest with the patient, though — improvement after quitting is often slow and incomplete, because the disease acquires a degree of self-sustaining momentum. But without cessation, everything else works less well.

### 9.5 Clinical Features

**Symptoms:** **itching, burning, pain, and painful fissuring.** The pain is the point — thick acral skin cannot expand to accommodate a pustule or a fissure, so every step and every grip hurts.

**Signs:** crops of sterile, deep-seated pustules on erythematous, scaly, often hyperkeratotic skin, either **diffuse** or **within well-defined plaques**, on palms, soles, or both. Pustules evolve through a characteristic colour sequence — **yellow (fresh) → dark brown/mahogany (older, dried)** — so that lesions of different ages coexist. This "multiple generations" appearance is a useful clinical clue.

**⭐ Distribution — a high-yield pearl:**
- **Hands:** **thenar and hypothenar eminences > central palm > distal palm**
- **Feet:** **instep (medial arch) > sides of the foot > back of the heel**
- Uncommonly: **digital and subungual pustulation** (overlapping with ACH)

*Why these sites?* These are the regions with the **highest density of eccrine sweat glands** relative to trauma exposure. Once you know the pustule arises at the acrosyringium, the distribution stops being arbitrary and becomes predictable. **Mechanism explains map.**

**Functional impact:** pain may **impair walking or hand function** — so ask about work, driving, and activities of daily living. PPP has a quality-of-life impact comparable to severe plaque psoriasis despite affecting a tiny body surface area, which is exactly why BSA-based severity scores under-serve these patients when you are arguing for biologic funding.

**Associated conditions to screen for:** psoriatic arthritis, **SAPHO syndrome** (Synovitis, Acne, Pustulosis, Hyperostosis, Osteitis), sternoclavicular joint pain, and **thyroid dysfunction** (autoimmune thyroid disease is over-represented).

### 9.6 Investigations in PPP

1. **Dermoscopy** — vesicles and vesiculopustules, scaling, and erythema; older lesions show **yellow-to-brown globules** on a background of dotted/globular vessels. A quick, non-invasive way to confirm you are looking at pustules rather than eczematous vesicles.
2. **Histopathology (HPE):**
 - **Vesicles form around the intraepidermal eccrine sweat ducts and evolve into pustules, characteristically WITHOUT spongiosis**
 - The pustules contain **mononuclear cells** as well as neutrophils
 - The dermal infiltrate contains **T-lymphocytes, eosinophils and mast cells**
3. **KOH mount** — **mandatory**, to exclude dermatophyte infection. Vesiculobullous tinea is the great mimic, and treating it with potent steroids makes it dramatically worse (tinea incognito).
4. **Bacterial swab/culture** — confirm sterility; exclude secondary infection.
5. **Patch testing** — where allergic contact dermatitis is plausible (occupational exposure, footwear, rubber, chromate).
6. **Smoking history** — as important as any laboratory test.

### 9.7 Differential Diagnoses — With the Discriminating Features

Do not just list these. For each, give the feature that separates it.

| Differential | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|
| **Palmoplantar (plaque) psoriasis** | Hyperkeratotic, fissured plaques **without pustules**; look for nail pitting and plaques elsewhere |
| **Tinea — vesiculobullous type** ⚠️ | **Severe itching with nocturnal aggravation; asymmetrical/unilateral; similar lesions elsewhere on the body; positive family history; KOH/culture positive.** Asymmetry is the giveaway — PPP is characteristically symmetrical |
| **Palmoplantar (dyshidrotic) eczema / pompholyx** | Intensely itchy, **clear tapioca-like vesicles** on the sides of fingers; spongiosis on histology; personal/family atopy |
| **Allergic contact dermatitis** | Distribution follows the contactant; positive **patch test**; improves on avoidance |
| **Epidermolysis bullosa acquisita (EBA)** | Trauma-induced blisters with **scarring and milia**; DIF shows linear IgG at the BMZ with a **u-serrated** pattern; salt-split skin shows dermal-side binding |
| **Keratolysis exfoliativa** | **Non-inflammatory**, asymptomatic collarettes of peeling on palms — no pustules, no erythema |
| **Scabies** | Intense **nocturnal itch**, burrows in web spaces, affected contacts, mites/eggs on dermoscopy |
| **Dyshidrosiform bullous pemphigoid** ⚠️ | **Elderly** patient; **tense vesicles/bullae** on palms and soles mimicking pompholyx/PPP; confirm with **DIF (linear IgG and C3 at the BMZ)** and anti-BP180/BP230 serology. Easy to miss if you assume every palmar blister is eczema |
| **Drug-induced pustulosis (AGEP, pustular drug eruption)** | Clear temporal drug relationship; abrupt onset; more widespread; resolves on withdrawal |
| **Pemphigoid gestationis** ⚠️ | **Pregnant patient** with urticarial plaques and vesicles, classically **starting periumbilically**, which may involve the palms. Autoimmune, anti-**BP180 (NC16A)** antibodies; DIF shows linear C3 at the BMZ. **Critical differential in a pregnant woman with palmar vesicles** |
| **Acrodermatitis continua of Hallopeau** | Pustules centred on the **distal digit and nail bed**, with nail destruction — more distal than classic PPP |
| **Infected/pustular conditions** — bacterial folliculitis, candidal intertrigo, herpetic whitlow | Pustules are **follicular** (folliculitis), satellite lesions and KOH-positive pseudohyphae (candida), grouped painful vesicles with positive Tzanck/PCR (herpes) |

### 9.8 Treatment of PPP — Managing a Resistant Disease

Begin with the honest framing: **PPP is highly treatment-resistant**, remissions are often partial, and the realistic goal is control of pain and function rather than permanent cure. Set expectations early — it builds trust and prevents the patient bouncing between clinicians.

**🚭 Step 0 — Smoking cessation.** Always. First. With referral to a cessation service.

**Topical:**
- **Superpotent topical corticosteroids** (e.g. clobetasol propionate) — the mainstay. Acral skin is **extremely thick**, so ordinary potencies simply cannot get through.
- **Hydrocolloid gel occlusion** — a lovely practical tip. Occlusion **hydrates the stratum corneum and increases corticosteroid penetration several-fold**, so that a **mid-potency** steroid under hydrocolloid can match a superpotent steroid applied openly — with less risk of local atrophy. *Analogy:* the stratum corneum is a locked door; occlusion doesn't force it, it **soaks it until it swells open.**
- **Vitamin D analogues** (calcipotriol, often combined with a steroid) — promote normal keratinocyte differentiation and are steroid-sparing for maintenance.
- Adjuncts: keratolytics (salicylic acid, urea) for hyperkeratosis and fissuring; potassium permanganate soaks for weeping lesions; emollients.

**Phototherapy:**
- **Topical (bath/soak) or oral PUVA** — psoralen plus UVA is more effective than narrowband UVB here, because **UVA penetrates the thick acral epidermis more deeply**. It induces T-cell apoptosis and is locally immunosuppressive.
- **Excimer laser (308 nm)** — targeted monochromatic UVB delivered at high fluence to lesional skin only, sparing surrounding normal skin.
- **Retinoid + PUVA (Re-PUVA)** — acitretin thins the hyperkeratosis, allowing better light penetration; the combination is more effective than either alone.

**Systemic conventional agents:**
- **Acitretin** — among the more reliable options; anti-neutrophilic and normalises differentiation
- **Cyclosporine** — effective, but relapse on withdrawal is common
- **Methotrexate** — modest evidence in PPP specifically, but useful with joint disease
- **Leflunomide** — pyrimidine-synthesis inhibitor; an option, especially with associated arthritis
- **Fumaric acid esters (dimethyl fumarate)** — used mainly in Europe; acts via **Nrf2 activation** (antioxidant response) and lymphocyte apoptosis; evidence in PPP is limited but real *(Mrowietz et al., Br J Dermatol 2017)*
- **Tofacitinib** — a **JAK inhibitor**, blocking signalling downstream of many of the cytokine receptors involved (IL-23/Th17 axis); encouraging reports in refractory PPP
- **Apremilast** — a **PDE4 inhibitor** that raises intracellular cAMP, damping TNF-α, IL-17 and IL-23 production; oral, well-tolerated, with modest efficacy

**Biologics:**
- **TNF-α inhibitors** — effective in some, but remember they can also *cause* paradoxical PPP; a genuine double-edged sword
- **Ustekinumab** (anti-IL-12/23 p40)
- **Secukinumab** (anti-IL-17A) — targets the demonstrated IL-17A over-expression in lesional skin
- **Guselkumab** (anti-IL-23 p19) — notable because it has dedicated PPP evidence from the **GESTURE trial**, and is approved for PPP in Japan

*(And keep spesolimab in mind conceptually — as an IL-36-receptor blocker it is the mechanism-matched agent for the IL-36γ component of PPP, and evidence in localized pustular disease is evolving.)*

## PART 10 — Acrodermatitis Continua of Hallopeau (ACH): Don't Forget the Second Localized Form

Your source lists ACH in the classification but does not expand it. Since you classified it, you must be able to describe it — examiners notice exactly this kind of gap. Here is the essential material.

- **What it is:** a chronic, relapsing, **sterile pustular eruption confined to the distal digits**, typically beginning at the **tip of a single finger or thumb, in the nail bed and nail fold**, often **following trauma**.
- **Evolution:** recurrent crops of pustules on a glazed, erythematous distal digit → the pus lifts and destroys the nail apparatus → **onychodystrophy → anonychia (complete nail loss)** → atrophy of the skin of the fingertip → in long-standing cases, **osteolysis/resorption of the distal phalanx** with shortening and tapering of the digit. Then it spreads proximally and to adjacent digits.
- **Why it matters disproportionately to its size:** it is **permanently destructive** (unlike PPP) and it can **generalize into von Zumbusch GPP**. A tiny lesion with big consequences.
- **Genetics:** ***IL36RN*** and ***AP1S3*** mutations reported — placing ACH firmly within the pustular psoriasis/IL-36 spectrum.
- **Differentials:** herpetic whitlow, bacterial paronychia, onychomycosis, subungual squamous cell carcinoma (in a chronic single-digit lesion, **always** consider malignancy and biopsy), and Reiter's/reactive arthritis-associated dactylitis.
- **Treatment:** notoriously refractory. Superpotent topical steroids (often under occlusion), topical calcipotriol, topical calcineurin inhibitors; then **acitretin, methotrexate, cyclosporine**; then biologics (**anti-TNF, ustekinumab, secukinumab, spesolimab**), and **apremilast** in milder disease. **Treat early and aggressively**, because the aim is to prevent irreversible nail and bone destruction.

## PART 11 — Common Misunderstandings (Let's Clear These Up Now)

1. **"Pustules mean infection."** ❌ In pustular psoriasis the pustules are **sterile**. Gram stain and culture prove it. Antibiotics are for *secondary* infection only.
2. **"Kogoj and Munro are the same thing."** ❌ **Kogoj = spongiform pustule in the stratum spinosum** (within widened intercellular spaces); **Munro = microabscess of pyknotic neutrophils in the stratum corneum.** They are consecutive stages of one upward journey.
3. **"Kogoj is a macroscopic pustule."** ❌ It is a **microscopic** histological structure. The visible clinical pustule results from **coalescence of many** such collections under a thin parakeratotic horny layer. (This is precisely where the source wording is misleading.)
4. **"Impetigo herpetiformis is caused by bacteria or herpes."** ❌ Neither. It is **GPP of pregnancy** — a purely historical misnomer.
5. **"All psoriasis treatments help all psoriasis."** ❌ **Coal tar and dithranol can precipitate or worsen pustular psoriasis**, and **TNF/IL-17 inhibitors can paradoxically induce it**. Always match the drug to the *mechanism*.
6. **"Steroids are simply forbidden in psoriasis."** ❌ Too crude. The real principle: **steroids are dangerous when they must be withdrawn while disease activity persists.** In impetigo herpetiformis, delivery ends the disease, so a postpartum taper is safe and steroids are the drug of choice.
7. **"PPP and palmoplantar plaque psoriasis are the same."** ❌ PPP is defined by **sterile pustules of multiple generations arising at the acrosyringium**; palmoplantar psoriasis is hyperkeratotic plaques without pustules. Different histology, different smoking association, different prognosis.
8. **"PPP is trivial because it covers so little skin."** ❌ BSA is a terrible severity measure for acral disease. PPP causes **pain, fissuring, and loss of hand and foot function**, with quality-of-life scores rivalling extensive plaque disease.
9. **"GPP is just severe plaque psoriasis."** ❌ Genetically and immunologically distinct — **innate, IL-36-driven autoinflammation** versus **adaptive, Th17/IL-23-driven inflammation**. That is why anti-IL-36 therapy is specific to GPP, and why a fifth of GPP patients have never had a plaque in their lives.
10. **"A young child with annular pustules must be very sick."** ❌ **Subacute annular GPP** of childhood typically **lacks systemic symptoms** and carries a **good prognosis** — a very different conversation from von Zumbusch.
11. **"Don't biopsy, the diagnosis is clinical."** ❌ In pustular psoriasis, histology (Kogoj) is part of the **formal diagnostic criteria**, and is essential to exclude AGEP, subcorneal pustular dermatosis, and IgA pemphigus.
