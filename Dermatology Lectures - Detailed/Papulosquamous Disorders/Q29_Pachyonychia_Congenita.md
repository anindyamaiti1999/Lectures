# Pachyonychia Congenita — A Mini-Lecture

## 1. Setting the Scene: Why This Disease Deserves Your Attention

Let me start with a small confession about dermatological naming: **pachyonychia congenita is one of the worst-named diseases in the specialty.** "Pachyonychia" means "thick nails" (Greek *pachys* = thick, *onych* = nail), and "congenita" means present from birth. So the name tells you: *thick nails from birth*. Perfectly descriptive — and almost completely misleading about what actually ruins these patients' lives.

Because if you interview adults with pachyonychia congenita (PC) — and the International Pachyonychia Congenita Research Registry (IPCRR) has done exactly this on hundreds of patients — the nails are not what they complain about. **They complain about their feet.** They complain that every step feels like walking on hot gravel or on a stone lodged in the sole. Many use crutches, wheelchairs, or canes not because of any neurological or joint disease, but because the pain of weight-bearing on their soles is intolerable.

So here is the single most important framing sentence of this entire lecture:

> **PC is named for the nails, defined by a triad, but *disabled by the soles*.**

Keep that in your pocket. It's the high-yield point, the exam answer, and the clinical truth all at once.

**Where does PC sit in the bigger map?** PC belongs to the **disorders of keratinization** — a family of genetic diseases in which the skin's structural or barrier machinery is faulty, producing scaling, thickening, or fragility. Within that family, PC belongs to a more specific sub-tribe: the **keratin disorders**, diseases caused by mutations in the genes encoding keratin intermediate filament proteins. Its cousins in that sub-tribe include epidermolysis bullosa simplex (KRT5/KRT14), epidermolytic ichthyosis (KRT1/KRT10), and Meesmann corneal dystrophy (KRT3/KRT12). Understanding PC therefore teaches you a general principle: **whichever tissue expresses the mutated keratin is the tissue that fails.** Everything about PC's clinical picture — nails, soles, mouth, cysts, teeth — falls out of that one principle.

---

## 2. The Formal Definition

**Pachyonychia congenita** is a **heterogeneous group of autosomal dominant genodermatoses** caused by mutations in keratin genes, classically defined by a **triad**:

1. **Painful focal palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK)** — thick, callus-like, exquisitely tender plaques on pressure points, especially the soles
2. **Nail dystrophy (pachyonychia)** — hypertrophic, thickened, dystrophic nails
3. **Oral leukokeratosis** — white thickened plaques on the tongue and buccal mucosa

"Heterogeneous group" is doing important work in that sentence. PC is not one disease; it is **five related diseases** that happen to look similar because they hit five closely related proteins working in the same structural network. That's why modern classification is genetic, not clinical — more on that shortly.

---

## 3. First Principles: What Keratins Actually Do (and Why Mutating Them Hurts)

Before we can make sense of a single clinical sign, we need to understand keratins properly. Skip this and PC becomes a list to memorize; understand it and PC becomes obvious.

### Keratins are the skeleton *inside* the skin cell

Every keratinocyte contains a three-dimensional internal scaffold of **keratin intermediate filaments**. These filaments run from the nucleus outward and anchor into two kinds of junction:

- **Desmosomes** — the "spot welds" that attach one keratinocyte to its neighbour
- **Hemidesmosomes** — the "rivets" that attach the basal cells to the basement membrane

Think of the epidermis as **a brick wall where each brick has internal steel rebar, and the rebar of every brick is bolted to the rebar of the bricks around it.** The result is a continuous, tissue-wide tension-bearing network. When you press your heel into the ground, that mechanical load is not borne by cell membranes (which are flimsy soap bubbles) — it is borne by this keratin rebar network, transmitted cell to cell.

### Keratins work in obligate pairs

Keratins are not monomers. A keratin filament is built from a **heterodimer of one type I (acidic) keratin and one type II (basic) keratin**. In the relevant pairings for PC:

- **K6 (type II) pairs with K16 (type I)**
- **K6 (type II) also pairs with K17 (type I)**

This pairing rule explains an otherwise puzzling fact: **mutations in either partner produce essentially the same disease.** If you break the bolt or you break the girder, the joint fails either way. That is why KRT6A and KRT16 mutations give near-identical phenotypes.

### These are the "stress" keratins

Here's the elegant part. K6, K16, and K17 are **not** the workhorse keratins of ordinary interfollicular skin (that job belongs to K5/K14 basally and K1/K10 suprabasally). Instead, K6/K16/K17 are **inducible, stress-response, "high-turnover, high-mechanical-load" keratins.** They are switched on where epithelium is being hammered, sheared, abraded, or forced to proliferate rapidly:

- **Nail bed**, around the nail progenitor cells
- **Suprabasal layers of palmoplantar skin** — the highest-load skin on the body
- **Oral mucosal epithelium** — constantly abraded by food, teeth, and tongue
- **Hair follicles** (outer root sheath and companion layer)
- **Wound edges** — K6/K16 are upregulated in any healing wound, which is why they're sometimes called "wound-healing keratins"

**K17** has an additional, distinct home: the **pilosebaceous unit** — the follicle–sebaceous gland complex — and the developing tooth germ.

Now look back at the clinical triad. **The triad is simply a map of where K6/K16 live.** Nail bed → nail dystrophy. Palmoplantar suprabasal skin → PPK. Oral epithelium → leukokeratosis. There is nothing to memorize; the anatomy *is* the expression pattern.

### Why dominant? The "one rotten girder" principle

PC is **autosomal dominant**, and the mutations are **dominant-negative (a functional gain-of-dysfunction)**, not simple loss-of-function. Most PC mutations are missense changes in the **helix boundary motifs** — the highly conserved ends of the central α-helical rod domain where filaments join end-to-end and side-to-side.

Why does that matter? Because a mutant keratin monomer is still *made* and still *incorporated* into filaments — it just poisons them. Imagine a bricklayer building a wall where **one in every two bricks is crumbly.** You cannot simply skip them; they get built into the structure and become the failure points throughout. That is why a single mutant allele is enough to cause disease, and why the affected allele must be **silenced, not supplemented** — a fact that will become crucial when we discuss siRNA therapy at the end.

## 4. Classification, Part 1: The Historical (Eponymous) System — and a Trap

For most of the twentieth century, PC was split clinically into two types.

| Feature | **Type I: Jadassohn–Lewandowsky** | **Type II: Jackson–Lawlor** |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary keratins** | KRT6A, KRT16 | KRT6B, KRT17 |
| **Nail involvement** | Severe — all 20 nails | Less severe — fewer nails |
| **Plantar keratoderma** | Present | Present (often painful) |
| **Oral leukokeratosis** | Prominent | Less prominent |
| **Natal teeth** | Absent | **Present** |
| **Cysts** | Follicular keratoses | **Steatocystomas, epidermal cysts, vellus hair cysts** |
| **Hair** | Generally normal | May show pili torti |

**How to remember which eponym is which:** *Jadassohn–Lewandowsky* was described **first (1906)**, so it is **Type 1** — and it is the "**mouth and nails**" type. *Jackson–Lawlor* came **later (1951)** = **Type 2**, and it is the "**teeth and cysts**" type. A useful mnemonic: **Type 2 has the two extras — teeth and cysts.**

> ### ⚠️ Critical Source Error — the Eponyms Are Inverted
>
> Your source text (p. 185) labels **"Type I: Jackson–Lawlor"** and **"Type II: Jadassohn–Lewandowsky."** **This is reversed.** The established classification in Rook's, Bolognia, and the primary literature (Smith et al., *J Invest Dermatol*, 2005) is:
>
> - **PC-1 = Jadassohn–Lewandowsky** (KRT6A / KRT16)
> - **PC-2 = Jackson–Lawlor** (KRT6B / KRT17)
>
> The table above is **corrected**. This is a high-impact error — if you reproduce the source version in an exam, you will lose the mark. Note also that the source misspells the eponym as **"Jadahsson"**; the correct spelling is **Jadassohn**, after Josef Jadassohn (1863–1936).

### And here's the deeper lesson

The fact that a modern textbook could invert these eponyms is not merely embarrassing — it is *the very reason the classification was abandoned.* The clinical overlap between "Type 1" and "Type 2" turned out to be so extensive that even expert dermatologists could not reliably assign patients, and once genetic testing arrived, the eponyms were found to cut across the genotypes untidily. So the field moved on.

> **Modern position:** The PC-1 / PC-2 nomenclature is now considered **obsolete**, replaced by **molecular classification** (McLean et al., *Br J Dermatol*, 2011). Learn the eponyms because they are still examined and still appear in older literature — but know that clinically they are historical.

## 5. Classification, Part 2: The Modern Molecular System

The current system simply names the gene: **PC-K6a**, **PC-K6b**, **PC-K6c**, **PC-K16**, **PC-K17** (equivalently written PC-KRT6A, etc.).

| Subtype | Gene | Protein | Key distinguishing features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PC-K6a** | *KRT6A* | Keratin 6a | **Most common.** Severe nail dystrophy (all nails); prominent oral leukokeratosis; painful PPK; laryngeal involvement (hoarseness, neonatal stridor); **ear pain and feeding difficulty**; natal teeth can occur |
| **PC-K6b** | *KRT6B* | Keratin 6b | Resembles K6a but **milder nail disease**; cysts prominent (overlaps historical PC-2) |
| **PC-K6c** | *KRT6C* | Keratin 6c | **Mildest**; often focal PPK with **little or no nail change** — may present as "isolated focal PPK" |
| **PC-K16** | *KRT16* | Keratin 16 | Nail dystrophy of **only a few nails**; painful plantar keratoderma; **splinter haemorrhages of fingernails**; overall less severe |
| **PC-K17** | *KRT17* | Keratin 17 | **Natal teeth**; **steatocystomas and pilosebaceous cysts**; cysts increase at puberty |

**Why this system is better:** it carries prognostic and therapeutic information. Telling a family "this is PC-K6c" predicts a mild course with minimal nail disease; "PC-K6a" predicts severe, multi-system involvement with possible neonatal airway concerns. And in the age of allele-specific therapy, you *must* know the exact mutation to design a drug against it. **Genotype is now the diagnosis.**

**A note on genomic geography:** *KRT16* and *KRT17* both sit in the type I keratin cluster on **chromosome 17q21.2**; the *KRT6* genes (6A, 6B, 6C) sit in the type II cluster on **12q13**. This clustering reflects their origin by gene duplication — which is also why K6a, K6b, and K6c are so similar in function that mutations in any of them produce overlapping disease.

## 6. Pathophysiology: The Causal Chain, Step by Step

Let's walk the mechanism as an unbroken cause → effect chain, because this is where most students skip steps.

```
Dominant missense mutation in KRT6A / 6B / 6C / KRT16 / KRT17
(usually in the helix boundary motif)
                    ↓
Mutant keratin is still expressed and still incorporated into filaments
                    ↓
Defective keratin intermediate filament assembly
→ filaments aggregate, kink, or fail to bundle properly
                    ↓
Compromised intracellular cytoskeleton in the tissues expressing that keratin
                    ↓
Cells are mechanically FRAGILE — they cannot tolerate shear/pressure
                    ↓
    ┌───────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────┐
    ↓                           ↓                           ↓
PALMS/SOLES               NAIL BED                   ORAL MUCOSA
Trauma → cell injury      Disordered nail-bed         Disordered, fragile
& blistering beneath      keratinisation              epithelium → reactive
the cornified layer                                   hyperkeratosis
    ↓                           ↓                           ↓
Reactive/compensatory      Abnormal nail plate         Oral leukokeratosis
HYPERKERATOSIS             formation + subungual       (white plaques)
    ↓                      hyperkeratosis
Thick, hard plaque acts        ↓
as a rigid foreign body   PACHYONYCHIA
    ↓
Increased focal pressure → more sub-keratotic blistering
    ↓
VICIOUS CYCLE → PAIN
```

Two mechanistic subtleties are worth pausing on.

**(a) Hyperkeratosis is the *response*, not the primary defect.** The skin is not "programmed" to be thick. It becomes thick because fragile, injured suprabasal keratinocytes trigger a proliferative, stress-keratin-upregulating repair programme — the same programme that makes an ordinary callus form on a labourer's hand. In PC, this programme is running permanently and out of control, because the tissue can never achieve mechanical competence. **PC keratoderma is a chronic, failed wound-healing response.**

**(b) There is a self-amplifying loop.** The thick plaque is *harder and less compliant* than normal skin. When you stand, a rigid plaque does not deform to spread load — it concentrates it, like standing with a coin taped to your sole. That concentrated load causes more injury beneath the plaque, which causes more hyperkeratosis. **This is why simple mechanical debridement (paring) actually works so well:** you are breaking the loop by removing the rigid load-concentrator.

**(c) K17's separate domain explains PC-2's "extras."** Because K17 is expressed in the **pilosebaceous unit** and in **developing tooth germ**, a K17 mutation adds features no other subtype prominently shows: **steatocystomas, epidermal cysts, vellus hair cysts** (all pilosebaceous-derived), and **natal teeth** (abnormal timing/keratinisation of the dental epithelium). This is the single best illustration of the principle "*expression pattern determines phenotype.*"

## 7. Clinical Features — In Detail, With Reasons

### 7.1 Painful focal palmoplantar keratoderma — the defining disability

This is the feature to lead with in any answer.

- **Morphology:** *Focal*, not diffuse — discrete, well-demarcated, yellowish, callus-like plaques over **pressure points** (metatarsal heads, heels, and on the hands at grip sites), often with an **erythematous halo**. Distinguish this in your mind from *diffuse* PPK (e.g. Unna–Thost/Vörner) which carpets the whole palm/sole.
- **Timing of onset:** appears in the **first years of life — specifically when the child begins weight-bearing and walking.** Notice the logic: no load, no lesion. This is one of the most beautiful demonstrations in dermatology that these lesions are **mechanically driven**. Some infants show lesions on the *knees* first — from crawling.
- **The blister beneath the callus:** this is the key to the pain. **Blisters develop *beneath* the keratoderma.** You often cannot see them, because they are buried under a centimetre of hard keratin. The patient feels a burning, throbbing, "stone-in-the-shoe" pain; the clinician looks at a callus and sees nothing dramatic. Hence the classic teaching: **the pain is grossly out of proportion to the clinical appearance.** This mismatch is a frequent cause of patients being disbelieved, dismissed, or labelled as exaggerating — a genuine clinical harm you should be alert to.
- **Progression:** pain typically becomes a **major problem after about 10 years of age**, as body weight increases and activity demands rise.
- **Seasonal pattern:** blistering and pain **worsen in summer.** Why? Heat plus **hyperhidrosis** macerates and softens the stratum corneum, weakening the interface and promoting blister formation and painful fissuring — and occlusive footwear compounds it.
- **Hyperhidrosis with fetid odour:** the palmoplantar skin sweats excessively, and the thick, macerated keratin is a superb substrate for bacterial (and fungal) overgrowth, generating the characteristic foul smell. This is not incidental — it is socially distressing and it is *treatable*, which makes it a high-value therapeutic target (see Botox, below).

> **Clinical picture to hold in your head:** a teenager who walks on the outer edges of the feet or on the heels, avoids sport, carries a scalpel blade or pumice stone in their bag, and describes their soles as "walking on pebbles."

### 7.2 Nail dystrophy (pachyonychia)

The predominant pattern is **hypertrophic nail dystrophy**, and your source usefully divides it into **two morphological phenotypes** — worth learning because they look quite different at the bedside:

1. **Nails that grow to full length and slant upwards.** This is caused by prominent **distal subungual hyperkeratosis (SUHK)** — a wedge of keratin heaping up under the distal nail bed which acts like a ramp, levering the nail plate upward. The result is the classic "**pincer-like**" or "**omega/claw**" profile with the free edge tilted off the digit.
2. **Nails whose plate terminates prematurely,** leaving a **gently sloping distal region of hyperkeratosis** — i.e. the plate stops short and what continues distally is a slope of hard keratin rather than nail.

Other features: nails are **thickened, hard, discoloured (yellow-brown), transversely over-curved,** and extremely difficult to cut. **PC-K6a** tends to involve **all 20 nails severely**; **PC-K16** may involve **only a few nails**, and adds **splinter haemorrhages of the fingernails** (from fragility of the nail-bed capillary–epithelial interface). **PC-K6c** may spare nails almost entirely.

- **Paronychia** — recurrent painful periungual inflammation — is a recognised associated finding. Mechanistically, the distorted, over-curved plate traumatises the lateral folds and the abnormal epithelium is a poor barrier, permitting recurrent bacterial and candidal infection.

> **Common misunderstanding:** students see thick yellow nails and reflexly diagnose **onychomycosis**. In PC the nails are thick **from birth or infancy**, affect **multiple/all nails symmetrically**, and come with PPK and oral changes. Do a KOH/culture if you must (secondary fungal colonisation *is* common), but don't let a positive culture fool you into thinking you've found the whole answer.

### 7.3 Oral and mucosal involvement

- **Oral leukokeratosis:** thick white plaques on the **tongue (especially dorsal/lateral) and buccal mucosa.** They are **not** premalignant — this is an important reassurance point and a key distinction from true oral leukoplakia.
- **Infant feeding difficulty:** babies have **difficulty sucking** — the thickened, fragile mucosa is painful and mechanically inefficient.
- **Hoarseness of voice:** from **laryngeal leukokeratosis** (K6a/K16 are expressed in laryngeal epithelium too).
- **Respiratory distress (the source's "RDS"):** in **severe neonatal cases**, laryngeal leukokeratosis can narrow the airway, producing **stridor and respiratory compromise**.

> **Terminology clarification:** "RDS" in this context does **not** mean the surfactant-deficiency respiratory distress syndrome of the premature neonate. It denotes **airway obstruction from laryngeal involvement** — a mechanical, not a surfactant, problem. Conflating the two is a classic error. Clinically this matters enormously: a hoarse, stridulous neonate with PC needs **ENT/airway assessment**, not surfactant.

### 7.4 Follicular hyperkeratosis

Spiny, keratotic follicular papules appear at sites of **friction and pressure** — **elbows, knees, buttocks, and trunk** (and waistband/bra-line areas). Again, note the mechanical logic: K6/K16/K17 are expressed in follicular epithelium, so friction-loaded follicles fail and plug. In infants, knee and elbow lesions may be the earliest sign, appearing with crawling.

### 7.5 Cystic lesions — the K17 signature

- **Steatocystomas** (sebaceous-duct-derived cysts, classically multiple on trunk, axillae, and thighs)
- **Epidermal (epidermoid) cysts**
- **Milia**
- **Vellus hair cysts**

All of these are **pilosebaceous-derived**, which is exactly where K17 lives — hence their concentration in **PC-K17** (historical PC-2 / Jackson–Lawlor). Crucially, they **increase markedly at puberty**, because androgen-driven sebaceous activation loads the pilosebaceous unit precisely when its keratin scaffold is defective. In some adults the cyst burden, not the feet, becomes the dominant complaint.

> **Link worth knowing:** *KRT17* mutations also cause **steatocystoma multiplex** as an isolated trait — so steatocystoma multiplex and PC-K17 are **allelic disorders**, two phenotypic expressions of the same gene. This is a favourite viva connection.

### 7.6 Natal / neonatal teeth

Teeth present **at birth (natal)** or erupting **within the first month (neonatal)**. Classically the hallmark of **PC-2 / KRT17**, but — and your source is right to flag this — they are **also reported with KRT6A**. They are typically poorly formed, mobile, and frequently shed or extracted (they pose an aspiration and maternal-nipple-trauma risk during feeding).

### 7.7 Other reported features

- **Ear pain and difficulty feeding** — specifically reported in **PC-KRT6A**, plausibly reflecting keratin expression in the external auditory canal epithelium and oral/pharyngeal mucosa.
- **Pili torti / hair abnormalities** — described in the historical PC-2 group.
- **Hoarseness, angular cheilitis, and corneal/ocular changes** (leukokeratosis, cataract in rare reports) round out the older literature.

### 7.8 Putting it on a timeline

Because PC is mechanically driven, its natural history tracks the child's motor milestones — a lovely teaching point:

| Age | What appears | Mechanical reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Birth / neonatal** | Natal teeth; oral leukokeratosis; feeding difficulty; hoarseness/stridor; occasionally early nail change | Present before any loading — developmental/mucosal |
| **Crawling (6–12 mo)** | Knee and elbow keratoses; nail thickening becomes evident | Knees and elbows are the first loaded surfaces |
| **Walking (1–3 y)** | **Focal plantar keratoderma begins** | Weight-bearing starts |
| **Childhood (>10 y)** | **Plantar pain becomes disabling**; follicular keratoses | Rising body weight and activity |
| **Puberty** | **Cysts proliferate** (K17) | Androgen-driven sebaceous activation |
| **Adulthood** | Established PPK, all nails dystrophic, mobility aids, hyperhidrosis/odour, cyst burden | Cumulative |

## 8. Differential Diagnosis — and How to Reason Through It

The source lists two principal differentials. Let me give you the *reasoning*, because a list without discriminators is useless at the bedside.

| Condition | Gene | How it resembles PC | **How to tell it apart** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Howel–Evans syndrome** (tylosis with oesophageal cancer) | ***RHBDF2*** | **Focal, pressure-point PPK** — morphologically very similar; also painful | **No nail dystrophy.** And crucially: massive risk of **oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma** — this is a cancer-surveillance diagnosis, so missing it is dangerous |
| **Clouston syndrome** (hidrotic ectodermal dysplasia) | ***GJB6*** (connexin 30) | **Nail dystrophy + PPK** — the two loudest PC features | **Sparse hair/alopecia** is the giveaway; **no oral leukokeratosis**; **no painful blistering**; nails thin/dystrophic rather than classically hypertrophic; a connexin, not a keratin, disease |

Worth also holding in mind (broadening beyond the source):

- **Epidermolysis bullosa simplex, localised (Weber–Cockayne)** — painful palmoplantar blistering with summer worsening; but **no thick nails, no oral leukokeratosis**, and histology shows **true epidermolysis** (see next section)
- **Focal PPK (Wachters/Brünauer–Fuhs–Siemens type)** — pressure-point keratoderma without the triad
- **Onychomycosis / psoriatic nails** — the commonest *practical* mimic of the nail component, but not congenital and not accompanied by the triad
- **Oral candidiasis / leukoplakia / white sponge naevus** — mimics of the oral component; white sponge naevus (KRT4/KRT13!) is another keratin disease, and a nice conceptual neighbour

## 9. Histopathology — and the Single Most Important Negative Finding

Skin biopsy is **not** how PC is diagnosed (genetic testing is), but the histology is examinable and conceptually instructive.

| Finding | Description |
|---|---|
| **Gross hyperkeratosis** | Massive thickening of the stratum corneum — the compensatory response |
| **Acanthosis** | Epidermal hyperplasia — the proliferative response to chronic injury |
| **Patchy hypergranulosis** | Irregular thickening of the granular layer |
| **⚠️ NO epidermolysis** | **No intraepidermal cytolysis / clefting** |

> ### Why "no epidermolysis" is the pearl
>
> Compare PC with **epidermolysis bullosa simplex (EBS)** and **epidermolytic ichthyosis**. Those are *also* dominant keratin diseases, *also* feature painful palmoplantar blistering, and *also* worsen with heat and friction. So how does histology separate them?
>
> - In **EBS** (KRT5/KRT14) and **epidermolytic ichthyosis** (KRT1/KRT10), the mutated keratins are the **primary structural keratins of basal or suprabasal epidermis**. Their failure causes the keratinocytes themselves to **rupture — cytolysis** — producing a visible **intraepidermal split**: *epidermolysis*.
> - In **PC**, the mutated keratins are the **inducible stress keratins**. The tissue becomes fragile and blisters form, but the plane of failure is **sub-keratotic** — a split *beneath the massively thickened cornified layer*, not a cytolytic cavity *within* the living epidermis.
>
> **Therefore: in PC, pain arises from sub-keratotic blistering; in EBS, from intraepidermal cytolysis.** The absence of epidermolysis on H&E is the histological discriminator. (This also explains why the blisters in PC are so often invisible clinically — they are buried.)

**Definitive diagnosis** is by **molecular genetic testing** (sequencing *KRT6A*, *KRT6B*, *KRT6C*, *KRT16*, *KRT17*), which both confirms the diagnosis and assigns the modern subtype. Note that many cases are **de novo** — a negative family history does not exclude an autosomal dominant diagnosis, and this trips up trainees constantly.

## 10. Treatment — Organised by Mechanism

There is **no cure**. Management is best understood as three tiers: *reduce the mechanical load*, *reduce the keratin load*, and *target the molecular defect*.

### Tier 1 — Mechanical / physical (the backbone of real-world care)

| Modality | Details | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Paring or filing of callosities** | Scalpel blade, pumice, foot files, often self-administered daily or weekly | **Primary palliative measure.** Removes the rigid load-concentrating plaque, breaking the hyperkeratosis → pressure → injury vicious cycle. Patients often report more relief from this than from any drug |
| **Comfortable footwear with customised insoles** | Orthotic, cushioned, pressure-redistributing insoles; wide soft shoes | Spreads load away from focal pressure points, reducing the shear that initiates sub-keratotic blistering. Genuinely disease-modifying for quality of life |
| **Supportive/adjunctive** | Blister drainage, moisture-wicking socks, antiseptics for odour/infection, analgesia (including neuropathic agents in refractory pain), physiotherapy/mobility aids, psychological support | Addresses the downstream burden — infection, odour, pain sensitisation, social withdrawal |

### Tier 2 — Pharmacological / symptomatic

| Modality | Details | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Topical keratolytics** | **Urea 10–40%**, ± salicylic acid, lactic acid, propylene glycol | Urea is **hygroscopic and keratolytic** — it hydrates and disrupts the hydrogen bonding of the cornified layer, softening it so it can be pared off more easily and painlessly. Adjunct to Tier 1, not a substitute |
| **Botulinum toxin for hyperhidrosis** | Intradermal plantar injection | Blocks **acetylcholine release at sympathetic cholinergic nerve terminals** supplying eccrine glands → less sweating → less maceration, less softening of the keratin interface, less blistering, **less pain and less odour.** Many patients report substantial pain reduction — an unexpectedly high-value intervention. Limitations: injections into the sole are painful (requires nerve block/ice), effect lasts ~3–6 months |
| **Systemic retinoids (acitretin/isotretinoin)** | Used in other PPKs | **⚠️ Historically disappointing in PC and often *worsen pain*.** They thin the callus, which sounds desirable, but the thinned skin then transmits pressure directly to fragile tissue. A superb illustration of "not all keratoderma should be de-keratinised" — the thick layer is partly *protective* |

### Tier 3 — Molecularly targeted (the frontier)

| Modality | Details |
|---|---|
| **Oral rapamycin (sirolimus)** | An **mTOR inhibitor**. mTOR signalling drives keratinocyte proliferation and keratin gene expression; inhibiting it **reduces keratin expression** and has produced **reduction of plantar callus** in small case series. **Caveat:** systemic rapamycin is significantly immunosuppressive with mucositis, dyslipidaemia, and cytopenias — a heavy price for a non-lethal disease. **Topical sirolimus** formulations are under investigation with a more favourable risk profile |
| **siRNA (small interfering RNA) therapy** | **Mutation-specific gene silencing** of the mutant keratin allele |

> ### Why PC is the poster child for siRNA therapy
>
> This is one of the most instructive stories in translational dermatology, so let me spell out the logic.
>
> 1. PC is **autosomal dominant** with **dominant-negative (gain-of-dysfunction)** mutations. The mutant protein actively poisons the filament network.
> 2. Therefore the therapeutic goal is **not** to add normal protein — the patient already has a perfectly good wild-type allele — but to **remove the mutant transcript.**
> 3. **siRNA** does exactly that: a short double-stranded RNA designed to match the mutant mRNA sequence loads into RISC and directs its degradation. Because a single nucleotide distinguishes mutant from wild type, an exquisitely designed siRNA can achieve **allele-specific silencing**, destroying the bad transcript while sparing the good one.
> 4. If the mutant allele is silenced, the remaining wild-type allele can build **normal filaments** — and the phenotype should correct.
> 5. **Proof of concept:** the **Phase Ib trial of TD101**, an siRNA targeting the **KRT6A N171K** mutation, delivered by intralesional injection, produced **regression of the plantar callus at the injection site** while the placebo-injected site did not improve (Leachman et al., *Mol Ther*, 2010). This was among the first demonstrations of allele-specific siRNA therapy in humans.
> 6. **The remaining obstacles are delivery, not biology:** injecting siRNA into the sole is extremely painful, effects are local and transient, and each mutation may need its own drug. Current research therefore focuses on **topical/microneedle delivery** and on **mutation-agnostic** approaches.

**A note on genetic counselling** — easy to forget, always worth a mark. PC is **autosomal dominant**: 50% transmission risk from an affected parent, with **near-complete penetrance but variable severity**. Many patients are **de novo** mutations, in which case sibling risk is low but the patient's own offspring risk is 50%. Prenatal and preimplantation genetic diagnosis are technically feasible once the family mutation is known. Enrolment in the **IPCRR** registry offers patients access to expertise and trials.

## 11. Common Misunderstandings — Let's Clear Them Up

1. **"PC is a nail disease."** No. PC is *named* for the nails but *disabled by the soles*. Lead every answer with painful plantar keratoderma.
2. **"The eponyms: Type I is Jackson–Lawlor."** No — **Type I is Jadassohn–Lewandowsky** (first described, 1906; mouth-and-nails; K6a/K16); **Type II is Jackson–Lawlor** (1951; teeth-and-cysts; K6b/K17). Your source has these **inverted**.
3. **"Thick yellow nails from childhood = fungus."** Congenital onset, all/most nails, plus PPK and oral white plaques = think PC. Secondary fungal colonisation can coexist and mislead you.
4. **"Oral leukokeratosis is premalignant."** It is **not**. Unlike true leukoplakia, PC's oral plaques carry **no malignant potential** — but the *differential* (Howel–Evans) carries a massive **oesophageal** cancer risk, so don't relax about the differential.
5. **"PPK is present at birth because it's 'congenita'."** No — the plantar keratoderma appears **when weight-bearing begins.** The gene is congenital; the lesion is mechanical.
6. **"The pain is exaggerated / psychogenic."** The pain is real, arises from **blisters hidden beneath the callus**, and is classically **out of proportion to appearance.** Believing the patient is part of the treatment.
7. **"Retinoids are the answer for any keratoderma."** In PC they frequently **worsen** pain by removing protective hyperkeratosis. Mechanism should guide therapy, not reflex.
8. **"No family history means it can't be autosomal dominant."** Many PC cases are **de novo**.
9. **"PC histology looks like EBS."** It does not — **PC shows no epidermolysis.** That negative is the discriminator.
10. **"Gene therapy means giving a normal gene."** For a **dominant-negative** disease, adding normal protein doesn't help; you must **silence the mutant allele** — hence siRNA, not gene addition.

## 13. The Core Idea in One Picture

If you remember nothing else, remember this single causal sentence and you can reconstruct the whole disease:

> **A dominant mutation in one of the "stress keratins" (K6a/6b/6c, K16, K17) makes the cytoskeleton fail *wherever that keratin is expressed* — nail bed, palmoplantar skin, oral and laryngeal mucosa, hair follicles, and (for K17) the pilosebaceous unit and tooth germ. Those tissues become mechanically fragile, so every load injures them; the skin answers with relentless hyperkeratosis, and the resulting rigid callus concentrates pressure, blisters underneath, and hurts far more than it looks. Therefore: thick nails, white mouth, and — above all — feet you cannot stand on.**

And the therapeutic corollary follows just as cleanly:

> **Because the problem is a mechanically fragile tissue under load, take the load off (paring, insoles, botulinum toxin for sweat). Because the problem is ultimately one poisonous allele, silence that allele (siRNA). Everything in between is palliation.**

That's pachyonychia congenita — misnamed, under-recognised, mechanically elegant, and, of all the genodermatoses, perhaps the one closest to a genuinely targeted cure.
