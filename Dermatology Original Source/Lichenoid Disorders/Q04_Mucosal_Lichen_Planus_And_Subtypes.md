# Q4. Write a brief note on lichen planus of mucous membrane and its subtypes.

**Chapter**: Lichen Planus (Book Chapter 45 / Chapter 53) | **Source Pages**: 792–793 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Introduction

**Mucous membrane** lesions occur in **30–50%** of all cases of lichen planus and can present **without any associated skin lesions** (isolated mucosal LP occurs in approximately **15%** of cases).

---

### Affected Areas

- **Buccal mucosa and tongue** (most common)
- **Perianal**
- **Genital**
- **Larynx**
- **Esophagus**

---

### Oral Lichen Planus

**Oral lichen planus** occurs in **15%** of all LP cases.

- **Characterized by white streaks** on buccal mucosa, which often form a **lace-like pattern** (*Wickham's striae*)
- These streaks can be found on the **inner surface of cheeks, gum margins**, or on the **lips**
- On **tongue**, lesions typically present as **fixed white plaques**, often slightly **depressed below surrounding mucosa**

#### Associated Conditions

- **DM** (Diabetes Mellitus)
- **Candidiasis**
- **Oral lichen sclerosus** with LP overlap

---

### Subtypes of Oral LP

| Subtype | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Reticular** | White streaks (Wickham's striae) in lace-like pattern; most common subtype; usually asymptomatic |
| **Atrophic** | Erythematous, thinned mucosa; painful; often associated with erosive type |
| **Hypertrophic** | Plaque-like thickened white lesions; may mimic leukoplakia |
| **Erosive** | Most debilitating; also known as **ulceration**; painful eroded areas surrounded by radiating white striae |
| **Bullous** | Rare; fluid-filled vesicles/bullae that rupture leaving erosions |
| **Papular** | Small white papules; early/initial presentation |

> **Clinical Pearl**: The **erosive subtype** is the most debilitating and is associated with the highest risk of malignant transformation.

---

### Complications

- Lesions can extend into the **larynx** and **esophagus**, causing **strictures** and **dysphagia**
- **Squamous cell carcinoma** — malignant transformation risk
  - In **darkly pigmented individuals**, striking pigmentation is present
  - Ulcerative lesions, though uncommon, **may undergo malignant transformation**
- May be associated with **burning sensation, dysphagia, irritation**

---

### Differential Diagnoses

| Differential | Distinguishing Features |
|---|---|
| **Leukoplakia** | Homogeneous white patch; no Wickham's striae; higher dysplasia risk |
| **Gingivitis** | Inflammatory; bleeding; no reticular pattern |
| **Smoker's patches** | Tobacco-related; palatal predilection; disappears on cessation |
| **Chronic candidiasis** | Pseudomembranes removable with scraping; KOH positive |
| **White sponge nevus** | Autosomal dominant; bilateral; onset in childhood; diffuse white mucosa |
| **Lichenoid drug eruption** | Adjacent to dental amalgam; resolves on removal of trigger; eosinophils on histology |

---

### Genital LP

#### Males

- **Young men**
- Found on **penile shaft, glans penis, prepuce**, or **scrotum**
- **Subtle reticulate papules** to **severe erosive disease**

#### Females (Vulval LP)

- Leading to **scarring** and **loss of vulvar architecture**
- **Dyspareunia** and **pruritus**
- Lesions complicate **vulval lichen sclerosus** and **oral lichenoid** diagnosis
  - Coexisting vulval lichen sclerosus and oral lichenoid lesions complicate diagnosis
  - **Pelisse's vulvovaginal-gingival syndrome**: Classic triad of erosive LP affecting the **vulva**, **vagina**, and **gingiva**

---

### Reddish Dysplastic Variant

- **Increased cell proliferation** compared with reticular form
- **Moderate-to-severe epithelial dysplasia** — **increased risk of malignancy**

> **⚠ Exam Alert**: The reddish (erythroplakia-like) variant of oral LP carries the highest malignant transformation potential (**1–2% overall** for erosive/atrophic oral LP per Rook's and Bolognia). Monitor with serial biopsies.

---

### Histopathology

- **Thinned-out epithelium** with **parakeratosis** (unlike cutaneous LP which shows orthokeratosis)
- **Epithelium may resemble epidermis** (from skin — keratinization)
- **Plasma cells** may be prominent in **band-like infiltrate**
- **Colloid bodies** are fewer compared to cutaneous LP

---

### Management

#### Lifestyle Modifications

- **Educate** about chronic course and treatment
- **Direct remission** expectations
- **Avoid alcohol and tobacco**
- **Avoid spicy or acidic foods/drinks**
- **Good oral hygiene**
- **Professional dental care**

#### Topical Therapy

- **Topical calcineurin inhibitors**: Tacrolimus 0.1% and pimecrolimus 1%
- **Topical steroids/intralesional triamcinolone acetonide**
- *Oral retinoids*: Retinoic acid 0.05%, isotretinoin gel, and tazarotene gel

#### Systemic Therapy — Widespread Lesions

- **Soluble tablet prednisolone** (5 mg in 15 mL H₂O) — **swish and spit TID** for 6 weeks
- **Acitretin** for 8 weeks
- **Systemic corticosteroids**
- **Methotrexate**, **apremilast**
- **Cyclosporine**

#### Anogenital Management

- **Foreskin retraction/removal, surgery** in uncircumscribed lesions
- **Vaginal dilators** to prevent synechiae formation
- **Surgery** required after complete resolution of active lesions if adhesion occurs

## Corrections & Verification Notes

### 🚨 Factual & Medical Errors

- **Malignant transformation rate not explicitly stated in source** → Per Rook's Textbook of Dermatology and Bolognia, the malignant transformation rate of oral LP (particularly erosive and atrophic subtypes) is **approximately 1–2%** over 5–10 years of follow-up. The WHO classifies oral LP as a **potentially malignant disorder**. This has been added for completeness. (Ref: Rook's — Lichen Planus, Oral Manifestations; Bolognia — Oral LP Malignant Potential)

- **Classification of topical agents** — Source text on page 792 states *"Topical calcineurin inhibitors: Tacrolimus 0.1% and pimecrolimus 1%"* as a distinct management category. This is correct. However, earlier in the treatment section, the separation between topical steroids and calcineurin inhibitors could be clearer. These are distinct drug classes and should not be conflated.

### 🔍 Typographical & OCR Errors

- **"mucus membrane"** (Question text) → **"mucous membrane"**
  "Mucus" is a noun (the substance); "mucous" is the adjective describing the membrane. Standard medical terminology uses **"mucous membrane"** (synonym: mucosa). (Ref: Stedman's Medical Dictionary)

- **"Write a brief note of"** → **"Write a brief note on"**
  Grammatically, the correct preposition is "on," not "of." (Ref: Standard English usage)

- **"Oral Lichen sclerosis"** (Page 792) → **"Oral lichen sclerosus"**
  The correct spelling of the dermatological condition is **lichen sclerosus** (previously lichen sclerosus et atrophicus). "Sclerosis" refers to hardening of tissue (as in systemic sclerosis), which is a different entity. (Ref: Rook's — Lichen Sclerosus; ISSVD nomenclature)

- **"Methotrexate apremilast"** (Page 793) → **"Methotrexate, apremilast"**
  Missing comma/separator between two distinct systemic agents. (Ref: Page layout inspection)

- **"Consisting vulval lichen sclerosus and oral lichenoid lesions complicate diagnosis"** (Page 792–793) → **"Coexisting vulval lichen sclerosus and oral lichenoid lesions complicate diagnosis"**
  "Consisting" is a grammatical error; the intended word is **"coexisting"** — referring to the diagnostic challenge when vulval lichen sclerosus and oral LP occur together. (Ref: Contextual correction)

### 📝 Grammar & Formatting

- **Pelisse's vulvovaginal-gingival syndrome** — The source does not explicitly name this syndrome but describes the triad of erosive vulval, vaginal, and gingival LP. Per Bolognia and Rook's, this triad is formally recognized as **Pelisse's vulvovaginal-gingival syndrome** (described by Pelisse et al., 1982). This has been added for exam completeness. (Ref: Bolognia — Mucosal LP; Rook's — Vulvovaginal-Gingival Syndrome)

- **Subtype table** — The source lists subtypes as bullet points (Reticular, Atrophic, Hypertrophic, Erosive, Bullous, Papular). These have been formatted as a comparison table with key distinguishing features for exam readability.

- **"direct remission"** — This phrase from the source likely means **"direct patients toward realistic remission expectations"** rather than implying direct/immediate remission. Clarified in context.
