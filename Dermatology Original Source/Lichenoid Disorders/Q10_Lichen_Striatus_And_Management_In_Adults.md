# Q10. Write a brief note on lichen striatus and its management in adults.

**Chapter**: Lichen Planus (Book Chapter 45 / Chapter 53) | **Source Pages**: 796–797 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Definition

**Lichen striatus** is a **benign, self-limiting, linear inflammatory dermatosis** that follows the **lines of Blaschko**. It is classified as a **linear lichenoid dermatosis** and is characterized by the rapid eruption of small, flat-topped, lichenoid papules coalescing into continuous or interrupted linear bands.

---

### Synonyms

| Synonym | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| **Lichen striatus** | Preferred current term |
| **Linear lichenoid dermatosis** | Descriptive clinical synonym emphasizing the Blaschkoid pattern |
| **Lichen striatus albus** | Variant descriptor when the resolving phase leaves prominent **postinflammatory hypopigmentation** |
| **Blaschkoid acquired inflammatory skin eruption (BLAISE)** | Proposed umbrella term encompassing Blaschko-linear inflammatory dermatoses (Ref: Bolognia) |

---

### Epidemiology

- **Age**: Predominantly affects **children aged 5–15 years** (median onset 2–3 years)
- **Sex**: **Females > Males** (approximately 2:1 ratio)
- **Adults**: **Less common** but recognized; tends to be **more symptomatic** (intensely pruritic) and **more persistent** than the childhood form
- **Atopic background**: Strong association — approximately **50% of patients** have an atopic diathesis (atopic dermatitis, asthma, allergic rhinitis)

---

### Etiology & Triggers

Lichen striatus is thought to arise from a **somatic mosaic clone of altered keratinocytes** (reflecting genetic mosaicism along Blaschko's lines) that becomes clinically manifest when triggered by an **environmental stimulus**:

| Trigger Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Viral infections** | Varicella (VZV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) |
| **Vaccinations** | BCG, Hepatitis B, MMR |
| **Trauma** | Local injury or friction (Koebner-like phenomenon) |
| **Pregnancy** | Hormonal/immunological shifts |
| **Genetic predisposition** | Atopy (strongest risk factor) |

> **Pathogenesis Pearl**: The prevailing model is that lichen striatus represents an **immune response (CD8+ T cell-mediated) directed against a mosaic clone of epidermal keratinocytes** distributed along Blaschko's lines. This explains the strict unilateral, linear Blaschkoid distribution — the T-cell attack targets only the genetically distinct mosaic strip. (Ref: Bolognia Dermatology, Rook's Textbook)

---

### Clinical Features

#### Morphology

- **Papules**: Small (**1–3 mm**), **pink to dull-red or brownish**, flat-topped lichenoid papules
- **Arrangement**: Coalesce into **continuous or interrupted linear scaly bands** along the lines of Blaschko
- **Width**: Typically **2 mm–2 cm** wide plaques or linear bands
- **Distribution**: **Unilateral** — predominantly on **extremities** (legs > arms); may also involve arm, leg, neck, trunk, and nails
- Can **cover an entire limb** in extensive cases

#### Onset & Course

- **Sudden onset** — appears rapidly over days to weeks
- **Asymptomatic** in children (usually)
- **Pruritus** is **severe in adults** — an important clinical distinction from the childhood form

#### Nail Changes

- **Unilateral solitary nail dystrophy** — when the linear band crosses the nail matrix
- Features include: **splitting, longitudinal ridging, onycholysis, trachyonychia**

> **Exam Pearl**: **Unilateral nail dystrophy** involving a single digit, particularly in a child, should prompt consideration of lichen striatus even in the absence of cutaneous lesions — the nail may be the only manifestation.

#### Clinical Variants

| Variant | Description |
|---|---|
| **Parallel** | Multiple parallel linear bands |
| **Zosteriform** | Dermatomal distribution mimicking herpes zoster |
| **Bilateral extensive** | Rare; bilateral involvement (less common, denoted "l/c" in source) |

---

### Histopathology

The histopathology of lichen striatus is distinctive and includes features of **both lichenoid and spongiotic dermatitis**:

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Infiltrate pattern** | **Superficial and deep perivascular, interstitial, and perieccrine** lymphohistiocytic infiltrate |
| **Infiltrate composition** | Predominantly **CD8+ T cells > CD4+ T cells** |
| **Interface dermatitis** | **Basal vacuolar degeneration** with **focal liquefactive necrosis** of basal keratinocytes |
| **Spongiosis** | **Intercellular edema** and stretching of tonofilament-desmosome complexes |
| **Dyskeratosis** | **Dyskeratotic keratinocytes** (scattered apoptotic bodies) |
| **Acanthosis** | Irregular acanthosis with focal parakeratosis and hyperkeratosis |
| **Dermal changes** | **Focal liquefactive degeneration of basal layer** with **dermal edema** |
| **Para- and hyperkeratosis** | Alternating foci of parakeratosis and hyperkeratosis |

> **🔑 Key Histopathological Hallmark — Perieccrine Infiltrate**: The **perieccrine lymphohistiocytic infiltrate** (surrounding eccrine sweat glands) is the **crucial histopathological sign** that distinguishes lichen striatus from **linear lichen planus** (which lacks perieccrine involvement and shows a purely band-like superficial infiltrate). This is a **high-yield exam distinction**.

> **Histology Pearl**: The triad of **(1) perieccrine infiltrate**, **(2) interface dermatitis with spongiosis**, and **(3) superficial + deep perivascular infiltrate** is pathognomonic for lichen striatus and reliably differentiates it from other Blaschko-linear dermatoses. (Ref: Bolognia Dermatology, Rook's Textbook)

---

### Differential Diagnoses

| Differential | Key Distinguishing Feature |
|---|---|
| **Linear lichen planus** | Purely band-like superficial lichenoid infiltrate; **no perieccrine involvement**; wedge-shaped hypergranulosis; Wickham's striae clinically; chronic and persistent |
| **ILVEN (Inflammatory Linear Verrucous Epidermal Nevus)** | Present since birth/early childhood; persistent and treatment-resistant; alternating ortho- and parakeratosis with no spongiosis; does not self-resolve |
| **Linear psoriasis** | Silvery micaceous scale; Auspitz sign; Munro microabscesses and spongiform pustules of Kogoj on histology; chronic course |
| **Linear LP** | Violaceous polygonal papules with Wickham's striae; wedge hypergranulosis; saw-tooth rete; does not spontaneously resolve |
| **Linear porokeratosis** | Cornoid lamella on histology (column of parakeratotic cells); thread-like raised border; persistent |
| **Linear vitiligo (hypopigmented)** | Pure depigmentation without papules or scaling; no inflammatory infiltrate on histology |
| **Nevoid hypomelanosis (hypopigmented)** | Hypopigmented whorls/streaks along Blaschko's lines; congenital; non-inflammatory; no papules |

---

### Course & Prognosis

- **Variable course** — may resolve **within 1 year** in most cases
- **Children**: Spontaneous resolution in **6–12 months**
- **Adults**: More prolonged course, up to **1–2 years**; may be more symptomatic and treatment-resistant
- **Prognosis**: **Good** — benign and self-limiting
- **Residual change**: Temporary **postinflammatory hypopigmentation** (*lichen striatus albus*) is common and may persist for months after the inflammatory phase resolves

---

### Management

#### Investigations

- **Biopsy** — confirmatory; demonstrates the pathognomonic perieccrine infiltrate with interface dermatitis and spongiosis

#### Treatment in Adults

Since lichen striatus is self-limiting, treatment is primarily **symptomatic** and directed at controlling **pruritus** (which is more prominent in adults):

| Therapy | Details |
|---|---|
| **Reassurance** | First-line: Explain the benign, self-limiting nature of the condition |
| **Topical corticosteroids (TCS)** | **Potent TCS** (e.g., clobetasol propionate, betamethasone dipropionate) for symptomatic adults to control itch and inflammation |
| **Topical calcineurin inhibitors** | **Tacrolimus 0.1%** or **pimecrolimus 1%** — preferred for **facial involvement** or when concerned about steroid-induced atrophy/hypopigmentation; particularly useful for the hypopigmented (albus) phase |
| **Photodynamic therapy (PDT)** | For **refractory adult cases** that do not respond to topical therapy |
| **Nail involvement** | **Potent TCS under occlusion** applied over the proximal nail fold; **intralesional triamcinolone** (2.5–5 mg/mL) for persistent nail dystrophy |

> **Management Pearl (Adults vs. Children)**: While children rarely require treatment beyond reassurance, **adults often need active therapy** because of (1) **significant pruritus**, (2) **longer disease duration** (up to 2 years), and (3) **greater cosmetic concern** — particularly with persistent postinflammatory hypopigmentation. **Topical calcineurin inhibitors** are preferred over prolonged potent TCS in adults to minimize cutaneous atrophy. (Ref: Rook's Textbook, Bolognia)
