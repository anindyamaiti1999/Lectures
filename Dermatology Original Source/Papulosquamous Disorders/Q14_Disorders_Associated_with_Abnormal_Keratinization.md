# Q14. Mention disorders associated with abnormal keratinization.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 171–172 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Definition

- **Keratinization**, also known as **cornification**, is a process of cytodifferentiation that keratinocytes undergo when transitioning from their postgerminative state (**basal layer**) to fully differentiated, hardened **corneocytes** of the **stratum corneum**
- Disorders of keratinization encompass a broad group of conditions where this terminal differentiation program is disrupted — either through **genetic mutations** (inherited) or **acquired pathological processes**

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **"Epidermolytc Ichthyosis" (p. 171, Inherited section)** → **Correction:** "Epidermolytc" is a typographical error — the correct spelling is **"Epidermolytic ichthyosis"** (formerly known as epidermolytic hyperkeratosis or bullous congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma). This is caused by mutations in **KRT1** or **KRT10**. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"SK/AK/KP indented under pustular psoriasis" (p. 171, Acquired section)** → **Correction:** In the source text, **seborrheic keratosis**, **actinic keratosis**, and **keratosis pilaris** are visually indented under "Pustular psoriasis" with sub-bullet formatting (■ markers), which incorrectly implies they are subtypes of pustular psoriasis. These are **separate, independent entities** in the acquired disorders of keratinization and should be listed at the same hierarchical level as the psoriasis group. SK is a benign epidermal neoplasm; AK is a premalignant UV-induced lesion; KP is a follicular keratinization disorder. None are related to pustular psoriasis. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; Andrews')

- **"DLE: Lichenoid disorders" (p. 171, right column heading)** → **Clarification:** The source groups **DLE (discoid lupus erythematosus)** under the heading "Lichenoid disorders." While DLE can exhibit **lichenoid histopathology** (interface dermatitis with band-like lymphocytic infiltrate at the DEJ), it is **not** a primary lichenoid disease. DLE is an autoimmune lupus-specific skin disease classified under **chronic cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CCLE)**. Its inclusion here is justified on the basis of shared histopathological features (lichenoid tissue reaction pattern), but students should understand that DLE belongs nosologically to the **LE spectrum**, not to the lichen planus family. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia — Chapter on "Lichenoid tissue reaction/interface dermatitis")

- **"Conradi–Hünermann–Happle syndrome" (p. 171, Ichthyoses subsection)** → **Verification:** This is an **X-linked dominant** form of chondrodysplasia punctata (CDPX2) caused by mutations in **EBP** (emopamil-binding protein) involved in cholesterol biosynthesis. Features include asymmetric ichthyosiform erythroderma with Blaschko-linear distribution, chondrodysplasia punctata, cataracts, and skeletal asymmetry. Its inclusion under ichthyoses is appropriate as it presents with ichthyosiform skin changes. (Ref: Bolognia; Rook's)

- **"Naxos syndrome" (p. 171, PPK subsection)** → **Verification:** Naxos syndrome is an **autosomal recessive** cardiocutaneous syndrome caused by mutations in **JUP** (plakoglobin). Triad: **diffuse non-epidermolytic PPK + woolly hair + arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC)**. First described in families from Naxos island, Greece. Its inclusion under PPKs is appropriate as PPK is a cardinal feature. (Ref: Bolognia; Rook's)

- **"Olmsted syndrome" (p. 171, PPK subsection)** → **Verification:** Olmsted syndrome is caused by **gain-of-function mutations in TRPV3** (transient receptor potential vanilloid 3). Features: **mutilating bilateral PPK** with periorificial keratotic plaques, alopecia, and onychodystrophy. It is one of the most severe inherited PPKs. (Ref: Bolognia; Rook's)

- **"Loricrin keratoderma" (p. 171, PPK subsection)** → **Verification:** Caused by mutations in the **loricrin gene** (1q21, within the epidermal differentiation complex). Loricrin constitutes ~70–85% of the protein mass of the cornified cell envelope. Mutations cause a characteristic **honeycomb-type transgradient PPK** with associated mild ichthyosis. Previously included under the Vohwinkel syndrome umbrella (loricrin variant), which should be distinguished from the classic Vohwinkel syndrome caused by **GJB2** (connexin 26) mutations. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Perforating dermatitis" (p. 172, Perforating disorders subsection)** → **Clarification:** The source lists three perforating disorders: perforating folliculitis, perforating collagenosis, and perforating dermatitis. In standard classification (*Mehregan's classification*), the four classic perforating dermatoses are: (1) **Elastosis perforans serpiginosa (EPS)**, (2) **Reactive perforating collagenosis (RPC)**, (3) **Kyrle's disease**, and (4) **Perforating folliculitis (PF)**. "Perforating dermatitis" is not a standard Mehregan category — it likely refers to **acquired perforating dermatosis (APD)**, the umbrella term used for perforating disorders in the setting of CKD/dialysis and diabetes mellitus. (Ref: Bolognia; Andrews')

- **"Chediak–Higashi syndrome" (p. 172, Genodermatoses section)** → **Note:** Chediak–Higashi syndrome (CHS) is caused by mutations in **LYST/CHS1** and is primarily a disorder of **lysosomal trafficking** (giant granules in leukocytes, melanocytes, Schwann cells, and platelet-dense granules). While it produces pigmentary dilution (silvery hair, partial oculocutaneous albinism), it is **not** conventionally classified as a disorder of keratinization. Its inclusion in this list is likely due to the associated skin changes (infections, silvery scales) rather than a primary keratinization defect. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Keratoderma: Hypohidrosis syndrome" (p. 172, Genodermatoses section)** → **Clarification:** This likely refers to a **keratoderma with hypohidrosis** phenotype. The entity is not widely recognized as a single named syndrome in major dermatology references. It may refer to conditions such as **ectodermal dysplasia with keratoderma** or overlap phenotypes involving both PPK and sweat gland dysfunction. The italicization of *Keratoderma* in the source suggests it may be used as a descriptive prefix rather than a specific syndrome name. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Chediak higashi" capitalization (p. 172)** → **Correction:** The source writes "Chediak higashi syndrome" without proper capitalization of the second eponym. The correct spelling is **"Chediak–Higashi syndrome"** (both surnames capitalized, linked by an en-dash). Named after **Moisés Chediak** (Cuban physician) and **Otokata Higashi** (Japanese pediatrician). (Ref: Standard medical nomenclature)

## Integrated Margin Notes

- **Page 171 (Q13 → Q14 transition, mid-page):** Q14 begins immediately after the Q13 comparative table on the same page. The transition marks the **structural pivot** of Chapter 10 — from the **psoriasis/PsA disease block** (Q1–Q13) to the broader **keratinization disorders block** (Q14 onwards). Q14 functions as a **master classification framework** — an overview catalog of all keratinization disorders that will be individually elaborated in subsequent questions (Q15–Q38). Students should use Q14 as an organizational roadmap for the remainder of the chapter.

- **Page 171 (left column, Definition and Inherited disorders):** The left column presents the definition of keratinization/cornification followed by the inherited classification tree. The classification proceeds systematically: **Ichthyoses** → **PPKs** → **Acantholytic disorders** → **Acquired disorders**. This mirrors the organizational logic of the 2009 Ichthyosis Consensus Classification and standard dermatology textbook chapter structures. Note that the ichthyoses listed here are the major entities — **ichthyosis vulgaris** (most common, **FLG mutations**, AD) and **X-linked ichthyosis** (second most common, **STS deficiency**, XLR) are the two most commonly tested on examinations.

- **Page 171 (right column, Acantholytic disorders and Acquired disorders):** The right column begins with the acantholytic triad (**Darier, Hailey–Hailey, Grover**) — all three share **acantholytic histopathology** but have distinct pathogeneses: Darier (**ATP2A2/SERCA2**, ER calcium pump) and Hailey–Hailey (**ATP2C1/SPCA1**, Golgi calcium pump) are inherited calcium-pump disorders, while Grover (transient acantholytic dermatosis) is acquired. The acquired disorders section then pivots to psoriasis variants — this reinforces that psoriasis, the single largest topic in this chapter (Q1–Q13), is fundamentally a **disorder of keratinization** with markedly accelerated epidermal turnover.

- **Page 171 (right column, Lichenoid disorders heading):** The grouping of **DLE with lichenoid disorders** (lichen planus family) reflects a **histopathological classification** approach rather than a clinical/etiological one. All entities listed under this heading share the **lichenoid tissue reaction pattern** — a band-like lymphocytic infiltrate at the dermoepidermal junction causing basal cell damage (interface dermatitis). For examination purposes, students should be able to enumerate the lichenoid reaction pattern diseases: **LP, LPP, lichen nitidus, lichen striatus, lichenoid drug eruption, LP-LE overlap, GVHD, erythema dyschromicum perstans (ashy dermatosis)**.

- **Page 172 (left column, Perforating disorders and Others):** The perforating disorders are listed as three entities, whereas the classic **Mehregan classification** includes four: EPS, RPC, Kyrle's disease, and perforating folliculitis. The "Others" category functions as a catch-all for conditions that involve **abnormal keratinocyte biology** but don't fit neatly into the ichthyosis/PPK/acantholytic/psoriasis/lichenoid categories. Notable inclusions: **Bowen's disease** (full-thickness epidermal dysplasia = abnormal keratinization in a neoplastic context), **porokeratosis** (clonal expansion with the pathognomonic **coronoid lamella** = column of parakeratotic cells), and **acanthosis nigricans** (papillomatosis + hyperkeratosis driven by IGF-1/FGFR3 signaling).

- **Page 172 (right column, Genodermatoses):** The final category — "Genodermatoses" — appears to list genetic syndromes where keratinization abnormalities are a component feature rather than the defining feature. **Muir–Torre** (MSH2/MLH1, sebaceous neoplasms + visceral malignancies), **Gorlin syndrome** (PTCH1, basal cell carcinomas + skeletal anomalies), and **Chediak–Higashi** (LYST, lysosomal trafficking defect) are primarily classified under other disease categories (cancer predisposition syndromes, immunodeficiencies) but are included here because of their cutaneous keratinization-related manifestations.

- **Page 172 (overall structure note):** Q14 occupies approximately one and a half pages (bottom half of p. 171 + top third of p. 172). It is a **classification-only question** — no pathogenesis, clinical features, or management details are provided. Each entity listed in Q14 will be discussed in detail in its own dedicated question later in the chapter. This is a commonly tested **"enumerate" or "classify" question** in dermatology examinations, where students must be able to provide a comprehensive organized list under the inherited vs. acquired framework.
