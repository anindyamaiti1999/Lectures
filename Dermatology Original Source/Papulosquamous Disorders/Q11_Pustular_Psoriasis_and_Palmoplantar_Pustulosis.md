# Q11. Discuss the classification, clinical features, and management of pustular psoriasis.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 166–168 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Introduction

Pustular psoriasis is a rare variant of psoriasis characterized by **sterile pinpoint pustules** on variably erythematous skin.

---

### Classification

#### Generalized:
- **Acute GPP** (von Zumbusch)
- **Subacute annular GPP**
- **Exanthematic GPP**
- **Localized GPP**
- **Impetigo herpetiformis**

#### Localized:
- **Palmoplantar pustulosis** (PPP)
- **Acrodermatitis continua of Hallopeau**

---

### Generalized Pustular Psoriasis (GPP)

- A rare inflammatory skin disorder characterized by the presence of primarily **sterile, macroscopic** pustules on non-acral skin
- Often presents with **acute flares** with a **chronic remitting-relapsing pattern** — may coexist with plaque psoriasis

#### Epidemiology

- **W:M = 2:1**
- **Onset** usually **40–59 years**; juvenile/infantile cases can occur

#### Pathophysiology

- Often shows **mendelian inheritance**
- **IL36RN** mutations common especially in cases of **early onset** without plaque psoriasis; others — **AP1S3** and **CARD14** which affect inflammatory pathways
- Mechanism — mutation of IL36RN → **unopposed IL-36 signaling** → innate immunity: **TNF-alpha** and **neutrophilic recruitment** → autoinflammatory loop
- **Environmental triggers** — infection, topical Rx (coal tar, dithranol), systemic Rx (sudden stoppage of systemic corticosteroids), hypocalcemia, stress, **pregnancy**, biologics — ustekinumab, infliximab, adalimumab

#### Complications (Long-Term)

- Amyloidosis
- Telogen effluvium

---

### Clinical Subtypes of GPP

#### A. Von Zumbusch (Acute GPP)

- **Most severe form** with repeated waves of erythema and pustulation
- Clinically and genetically **heterogeneous**
- Can occur as sole phenotype or follow chronic plaque psoriasis, acrodermatitis continua of Hallopeau, or palmoplantar pustulosis

##### Japanese Diagnostic Criteria

- Systemic symptoms — fever, fatigue
- Multiple sterile pustules **merging to form lakes of pus**
- Kogoj's spongiform pustules on skin biopsy
- Repeated recurrences

##### Clinical Sequence

```
Burning sensation → High fever and severe malaise
→ Pre-existing lesions become fiery red
→ Pinpoint pustules → Progress to unaffected skin
→ Flexures and genital area → Generalized erythroderma
```

##### Nail and Mucosa

- Thickened nails / detach due to subungual pus
- Buccal and tongue — **geographic tongue**

##### Systemic Effects

- Hypovolemia
- Hypoalbuminemia
- AKI (acute kidney injury)
- Elevated liver enzymes: cholestatic jaundice
- Aseptic pneumonitis (ARDS)

##### Complications

- Hypocalcemia
- Erythroderma
- Renal failure
- Cardiac/renal failure
- Placental insufficiency
- Still birth
- Neonatal death

---

#### B. Impetigo Herpetiformis

- Seen in **pregnancy**, usually in **last trimester**
- Begins in inguinal/flexural areas → **centrifugal spread** → severe constitutional symptoms

##### Complications

- Cardiac/renal failure
- Placental insufficiency
- Still birth
- Neonatal death

---

#### C. Subacute Annular Generalized Pustular Psoriasis

- Most common in **infancy and childhood**; congenital/first year of life
- **Persistent annular lesions** with pustules on advancing edge leaving a **trailing scale**
- Rare
- Typically lacks systemic symptoms
- May be localized to flexures for long periods — **good prognosis**

---

### Investigations (GPP)

- **Gram stain** — sterile pus without any organisms
- **Laboratory investigations:**
  - CBC: leukocytosis
  - LFT, CRP, ESR, RFT
- **Chest X-ray** — shows signs of pneumonitis

---

### Skin Biopsy (HPE) — GPP

- Epidermis shows **parakeratosis** and **acanthosis**
- **Spongiform pustule of Kogoj** — a macropustule forming through migration of neutrophils from papillary dermal capillaries to upper layer of epidermis (**stratum spinosum**)
- **Granular layer:** As neutrophils of the spongiform pustule move up to the cornified layer, they become **pyknotic** and assume the appearance of a large **Munro abscess**
- Elongation of rete ridges
- Upper dermis shows infiltration of **lymphocytes and neutrophils**

---

### Treatment — GPP

#### General Measures (Supportive Care)

- **In-patient care**
- Maintain ambient temperature
- Monitor input/output
- Treat infections with antibiotics
- **Early delivery/termination** in pregnant women if threat to maternal life

#### Topical

- **Bland creams/lotions:** Avoid tar and dithranol
- Mild to mid-potent steroid creams

#### Systemic

- **Corticosteroids** — DOC in **impetigo herpetiformis**; Prednisolone 30–40 mg/day and tapered
- **Retinoids — first line**
  - Acitretin 1 mg/kg/day for severe and 0.5–0.75 mg/kg/day for maintenance
  - Contraindicated in pregnancy
- **Cyclosporine** — achieves rapid control; suitable in pregnancy
- **Methotrexate**
- **Biologics** — Infliximab, Adalimumab, Certolizumab, Ustekinumab, Secukinumab, and **Spesolimab**

---

### Palmoplantar Pustulosis (PPP)

Also known as:
- Chronic palmoplantar pustular psoriasis
- Pustulosis palmaris et plantaris
- Persistent palmoplantar pustulosis

#### Definition

- Chronic condition characterized by **multiple sterile pustules** on the palms and/or soles, often accompanied by erythema and scaling, and **highly resistant to treatment (Rx)**

#### Epidemiology & Associations

- Can occur in isolation or associated with plaque psoriasis
- Lacks genetic associations (unlike GPP)
- **30–50 years; W > M**

#### Etiopathogenesis

- **PSORS1** and **HLA-C: 06:02**
- **IL36RN mutations**
- **CARD 14 mutations**
- Genetic association with autophagy gene **ATG16L1** and **IL-19 family polymorphism**
- **Triggers** — medications (TNF-alpha inhibitor, IL-17 inhibitor), RA, Crohn's, **smoking**, infections, altered microbiome of skin
- **Lesional PPP expresses:** AMPs and cytokines (IL-18, IL-17A, IL-22; IL-36 gamma and IL-36RA) → IL-17 pathway and **IL-36 cytokine induced by smoking**

#### Clinical Features

- Itching/burning/pain and fissuring
- Diffuse or within plaques — pustular eruptions on palms/soles/both
- **Thenar hypothenar > central palm > distal palm**; instep > sides > back of heel
- Rarely digital, subungual pustulation
- Pain may affect walking or hand function

#### Investigations

1. **Dermoscopy** — vesicles/vesiculopustules, scaling, erythema
2. **HPE:**
   - Vesicles around epidermal sweat ducts evolve into pustules without spongiosis
   - Pustules contain **mononuclear cells** and neutrophils
   - Dermal infiltrate contains **T-lymphocytes, eosinophils, and mast cells**
3. **KOH** (to exclude fungal infection)

#### Differential Diagnoses

- Palmoplantar psoriasis
- **Tinea (vesiculobullous)** — associated with severe itching, nocturnal aggravation; similar lesions elsewhere on body, family history, asymmetrical
- Palmoplantar eczema
- Contact dermatitis
- EBA (epidermolysis bullosa acquisita)
- Keratolysis exfoliativa
- Scabies
- **Dyshidrosiform BP** (bullous pemphigoid)
- Drug-induced
- **Pemphigoid gestationis**

#### Treatment — PPP

##### Topical:
- **Superpotent TCS** (topical corticosteroids)
- **Hydrocolloid gel occlusion** — enhances efficacy of mid-potent TCS
- Vitamin D analogs

##### Phototherapy:
- Topical/oral **PUVA**
- **Excimer laser**

##### Systemic:
- Acitretin
- Cyclosporine
- Methotrexate, Leflunomide, Acitretin, Fumaric acid esters, Tofacitinib, Apremilast

##### Biologics:
- TNF-alpha inhibitors, Ustekinumab, Secukinumab, and Guselkumab

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **"early ousel"** → **"early onset"** — OCR/autocorrect error on p. 167. The intended word is "onset" (referring to early-onset GPP associated with IL36RN mutations). (Ref: Grammar/context)

- **"apis3", "card14", "Il36rn"** → **"AP1S3, CARD14, IL36RN"** — Inconsistent capitalization of gene names in the source. Standard HUGO nomenclature requires gene symbols to be fully capitalized and italicized for human genes: *IL36RN*, *AP1S3*, *CARD14*. (Ref: HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee)

- **"Kogoj is a macropustule"** — **Clarification needed:** The spongiform pustule of Kogoj is a **microscopic** histological finding located in the **stratum spinosum** (upper epidermis). It forms when neutrophils migrate from papillary dermal capillaries into the epidermis and accumulate within the spongiotic intercellular spaces. The source text states "Kogoj is a macropustule forming through migration of neutrophils" — the term "macropustule" here is used in the context of a histological structure that is relatively large compared to individual neutrophilic aggregates, but it is **not a clinically visible macroscopic pustule**. The Munro microabscess is the corresponding collection in the **stratum corneum**, formed when pyknotic neutrophils from the Kogoj pustule migrate upward. Students should be careful to distinguish: **Kogoj = spongiform pustule in stratum spinosum; Munro = microabscess in stratum corneum.** (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"ass. With severe itching"** → **"associated with severe itching"** — Abbreviated/OCR error in the differential diagnosis section for tinea (vesiculobullous) on p. 168. (Ref: Grammar)

- **"nonacral"** → **"non-acral"** — Standard medical hyphenation for compound modifier. GPP pustules appear on non-acral skin (distinguishing from PPP which is acral/palmoplantar). (Ref: Formatting)

- **"ILR36RN"** → **"IL36RN"** — Typo on p. 168 (PPP etiopathogenesis section). The correct gene symbol is *IL36RN* (Interleukin-36 receptor antagonist gene, encoding IL-36Ra). The extra "R" is a typographical insertion error. (Ref: OMIM #614204)

- **"CARD 14"** → **"CARD14"** — The source inserts a space in the gene name on p. 168. The correct HUGO symbol has no space: *CARD14* (Caspase Recruitment Domain Family Member 14). (Ref: HUGO)

- **"ATG16LI"** → **"ATG16L1"** — OCR/font confusion on p. 168. The gene is *ATG16L1* (Autophagy-Related 16-Like 1), ending in the numeral "1" not the letter "I." ATG16L1 polymorphisms are associated with Crohn's disease and have been reported in PPP susceptibility studies. (Ref: Bolognia; GWAS literature)

- **"11-19 family polymorphism"** → **"IL-19 family polymorphism"** — OCR error on p. 168; "11" misread for "IL" (interleukin). The IL-19 subfamily (IL-19, IL-20, IL-24) are IL-10 family cytokines implicated in psoriasiform inflammation. (Ref: Rook's)

- **Spesolimab inclusion — verification:** Spesolimab (Spevigo®) is an anti-IL-36 receptor monoclonal antibody approved (FDA 2022) for flares of GPP. Its inclusion in the GPP systemic treatment section is current and accurate. Spesolimab blocks the IL-36 signaling axis, which is the central pathogenic pathway in GPP (particularly in patients with *IL36RN* loss-of-function mutations). (Ref: Bachelez H et al., *NEJM* 2019; FDA label)

- **Cyclosporine safe in pregnancy — verification:** The source states cyclosporine "achieves rapid control; suitable in pregnancy." Cyclosporine is FDA Category C but has been used in severe GPP/impetigo herpetiformis in pregnancy when the benefit outweighs the risk. It does cross the placenta but is not associated with major teratogenicity. This is an accepted clinical practice, particularly when systemic corticosteroids are being tapered or when retinoids (absolutely contraindicated) cannot be used. (Ref: Bolognia; Rook's)

- **Corticosteroids as DOC for impetigo herpetiformis — verification:** Systemic corticosteroids (prednisolone 30–40 mg/day) are historically considered first-line for impetigo herpetiformis (pustular psoriasis of pregnancy) because retinoids are teratogenic and cyclosporine carries pregnancy risks. However, the use of systemic corticosteroids in pustular psoriasis is controversial — they can cause rebound flares upon tapering. In impetigo herpetiformis specifically, their use is justified because: (1) the condition is self-limiting after delivery; (2) rapid control is needed to prevent maternal/fetal complications; (3) the steroid is tapered postpartum. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Dyshidrosiform BP"** — **Verification:** Dyshidrosiform bullous pemphigoid is a rare variant of BP presenting with tense vesicles/bullae on the palms and soles mimicking dyshidrotic eczema or PPP. It is a valid differential for palmoplantar vesiculopustular disease, particularly in elderly patients. Diagnosis is confirmed by DIF (linear IgG/C3 at BMZ). Its inclusion in the DD list is accurate. (Ref: Bolognia)

- **"Pemphigoid gestationis"** — **Verification:** Pemphigoid gestationis (formerly herpes gestationis) can present with vesicles and urticarial plaques on the palms, making it a differential for PPP in pregnant women. It is an autoimmune blistering disease with anti-BP180 antibodies. Valid DD inclusion. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **Fumaric acid esters — verification:** Fumaric acid esters (dimethyl fumarate, FAE) are used primarily in European countries for psoriasis. Their inclusion in PPP systemic therapy is supported by limited evidence. Dimethyl fumarate has immunomodulatory properties (Nrf2 pathway activation, lymphocyte apoptosis). (Ref: Mrowietz U et al., *Br J Dermatol* 2017)

- **"Exanthematic GPP"** — **Verification:** Exanthematic GPP is a subtype characterized by a widespread eruption of small sterile pustules, often triggered by infections (particularly streptococcal) or drugs. It overlaps clinically with acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP), and distinguishing the two can be difficult. The source lists it as a GPP subtype, which is consistent with current classifications. (Ref: Bolognia; European Rare And Severe Psoriasis Expert Network — ERASPEN)

## Integrated Margin Notes

- **Page 166 (bottom right, Q11 opening):** The question introduces pustular psoriasis as a "rare variant" — this framing is important for examinations. GPP is distinct from plaque psoriasis both genetically (IL36RN-driven autoinflammatory pathway vs. Th17/IL-23 adaptive immunity in plaque psoriasis) and clinically. The classification into generalized vs. localized is the standard initial framework for structuring an answer.

- **Page 167 (top left, epidemiology & pathophysiology, skewed text):** The epidemiology data (W:M = 2:1; onset 40–59 years) and the genetic triad (IL36RN, AP1S3, CARD14) are high-yield examination points. The key mechanistic concept is that **IL36RN** encodes the IL-36 receptor antagonist (IL-36Ra); loss-of-function mutations lead to **unopposed IL-36 signaling** → innate immune activation → TNF-α and neutrophil recruitment → autoinflammatory pustular disease. This is mechanistically distinct from the adaptive Th17/IL-23 axis of plaque psoriasis and explains why anti-IL-36 therapy (spesolimab) is specifically effective in GPP.

- **Page 167 (left column, environmental triggers):** The trigger list is comprehensive and examination-relevant. Key triggers to emphasize: (1) **sudden withdrawal of systemic corticosteroids** — a classic precipitant of GPP flares; (2) **pregnancy** — the basis of impetigo herpetiformis; (3) **biologics** (paradoxical pustular psoriasis with TNF-alpha inhibitors). The mention of coal tar and dithranol as topical triggers is noteworthy — these are traditional psoriasis treatments that can paradoxically trigger pustular transformation.

- **Page 167 (left column, Von Zumbusch clinical sequence):** The clinical sequence (burning → fever → erythema → pinpoint pustules → flexural spread → erythroderma) is a classic examination viva description. The "lakes of pus" formed by merging pustules is a pathognomonic clinical description. The Japanese diagnostic criteria (systemic symptoms + sterile merging pustules + Kogoj on biopsy + recurrences) are important for structured exam answers, providing a formal diagnostic framework.

- **Page 167 (left column, nail and mucosal involvement):** Geographic tongue and nail dystrophy/detachment from subungual pus collections are often-tested clinical features. Geographic tongue occurs in ~10% of GPP patients and is also associated with plaque psoriasis.

- **Page 167 (left column, systemic effects):** The list of systemic effects (hypovolemia, hypoalbuminemia, AKI, cholestatic jaundice, ARDS) reflects the **life-threatening nature** of acute GPP. This is a medical emergency requiring ICU-level care. Hypoalbuminemia results from massive protein loss through denuded skin; AKI from hypovolemia and prerenal azotemia; cholestatic jaundice from hepatic involvement; ARDS from aseptic pneumonitis (neutrophilic lung infiltration without infection).

- **Page 167 (right column, impetigo herpetiformis):** This is pustular psoriasis specifically occurring in pregnancy (usually third trimester). Key examination points: (1) begins in flexures → centrifugal spread; (2) complications include placental insufficiency, stillbirth, neonatal death; (3) early delivery/termination may be needed; (4) systemic corticosteroids are DOC (retinoids contraindicated); (5) cyclosporine is an alternative.

- **Page 167 (right column, subacute annular GPP):** This is the **childhood-predominant** form — important to mention in pediatric dermatology contexts. The annular morphology with pustules on the advancing edge and a trailing scale is distinctive. Unlike von Zumbusch, it typically **lacks systemic symptoms** and has a **good prognosis**.

- **Page 167 (right column, HPE):** The histological description establishes the crucial distinction: **Kogoj spongiform pustule** (stratum spinosum) vs. **Munro microabscess** (stratum corneum). The Kogoj pustule is formed by neutrophils migrating upward through the epidermis; when they reach the cornified layer and become pyknotic, they form the Munro collection. Both are hallmarks of pustular psoriasis. The elongation of rete ridges and dermal lymphocytic/neutrophilic infiltrate are additional features.

- **Page 168 (left column, treatment of GPP):** The treatment ladder is clinically important: (1) **supportive care** first (inpatient, fluid/electrolyte management); (2) avoid tar and dithranol topically (can worsen); (3) **retinoids (acitretin) first-line systemic** — but contraindicated in pregnancy; (4) **cyclosporine** for rapid control/pregnancy; (5) **biologics** including the newer spesolimab (anti-IL-36R). The dosing of acitretin (1 mg/kg/day acute, 0.5–0.75 mg/kg/day maintenance) is a frequently tested point.

- **Page 168 (left column, PPP section):** PPP is presented as a distinct entity from GPP — this reflects the ongoing debate about whether PPP is truly a form of psoriasis or a separate condition. Key distinctions: (1) PPP **lacks genetic associations** typical of GPP (no IL36RN); (2) **strong smoking association** (IL-36 cytokine pathway induced by smoking); (3) **HLA-C*06:02** and **PSORS1** association. The PSORS1 locus (6p21.3) links PPP to psoriasis susceptibility, supporting its classification within the psoriasis spectrum.

- **Page 168 (left column, PPP clinical features):** The distribution pattern (thenar/hypothenar > central palm > distal palm; instep > sides > back of heel) is a high-yield clinical pearl for examinations, helping distinguish PPP from other palmoplantar dermatoses.

- **Page 168 (right column, PPP investigations):** The dermoscopic and HPE features of PPP differ from GPP: PPP pustules arise from **eccrine sweat ducts** (vesicles around epidermal sweat ducts → pustules without spongiosis), whereas GPP pustules form via the Kogoj mechanism. The mononuclear cell content in PPP pustules (vs. predominantly neutrophils in GPP) is another distinguishing feature.

- **Page 168 (right column, PPP DDs):** The extensive differential list is clinically practical. **Tinea (vesiculobullous)** — distinguished by itching, nocturnal aggravation, asymmetry, and positive KOH. **Dyshidrosiform BP** — important in elderly patients with palmoplantar blisters; requires DIF. **Pemphigoid gestationis** — critical DD in pregnant women with palmoplantar vesicles.

- **Page 168 (right column, PPP treatment):** The treatment ladder (superpotent TCS → hydrocolloid occlusion → vitamin D analogs → PUVA → systemic retinoids/cyclosporine/biologics) reflects the therapy-resistant nature of PPP. The hydrocolloid gel occlusion technique to enhance mid-potent TCS efficacy is a practical clinical tip. The inclusion of **guselkumab** (anti-IL-23p19) among biologics is noteworthy — guselkumab has shown efficacy in PPP in the GESTURE trial.
