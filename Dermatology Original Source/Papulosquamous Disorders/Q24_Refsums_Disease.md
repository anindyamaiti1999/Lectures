# Q24. Write a short note on Refsum's disease.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 181–182 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Definition

- **Refsum's disease** (also known as **heredopathia atactica polyneuritiformis**)
- An **ultra-rare, autosomal recessive (AR)** neurocutaneous **lipid storage disorder**
- Characterized by the clinical tetrad of:
  - **Deteriorating vision** (retinitis pigmentosa)
  - **Peripheral neuropathy**
  - **Cerebellar ataxia**
  - **Mild ichthyosis**

### Pathophysiology

#### Genetic Basis:

Two genes are implicated, both disrupting the **α-oxidation of phytanic acid**:

| Gene | Protein | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| ***PHYH*** (chromosome 10p13) | **Phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase** | Direct enzyme deficiency — cannot perform α-oxidation of phytanic acid |
| ***PEX7*** (chromosome 6q23) | **PEX7 peroxin** | Encodes a peroxisomal import receptor (PTS2) that transports phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase **into peroxisomes** — without PEX7, the enzyme cannot reach its site of action |

#### Pathogenesis Flowchart:

> **Mutations in *PHYH* gene**
> ↓
> Encodes the enzyme **phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase**
> ↓
> Responsible for **α-oxidation of phytanic acid**
> ↓
> **Impaired breakdown of phytanic acid**
>
> *— OR (alternative pathway) —*
>
> **Mutations in *PEX7* gene**
> ↓
> Encodes protein that **transports phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase into peroxisomes**
> ↓
> **Further disrupts the process** (enzyme cannot access peroxisomal site)
> ↓
> **Accumulation of phytanic acid in tissues**
> ↓
> **Altering structure and function of cell membranes**
> ↓
> **Causes Refsum's disease**

> **High-Yield Point**: Phytanic acid is a **branched-chain fatty acid** derived entirely from **dietary sources** (dairy, ruminant meat, certain fish). It cannot be synthesized endogenously. Because the body cannot β-oxidize branched-chain fatty acids directly, phytanic acid requires **α-oxidation** (removal of one carbon to produce pristanic acid) before it can enter the β-oxidation pathway. When α-oxidation is defective, phytanic acid accumulates in plasma, nerves, retina, myocardium, and skin — explaining the multi-system clinical features. This is why **dietary restriction** is the cornerstone of treatment. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

### Clinical Features

- **Childhood onset**
- **Ichthyosis vulgaris–like lesions** — small white scales on the extremities and trunk, larger adherent scales on the legs
- **Hyperlinearity and hyperkeratotic palms and soles**
- **Progressive retinitis pigmentosa (RP)** — visual field defect (night blindness → progressive peripheral visual field loss → eventual blindness)
- **Anosmia**, impaired taste
- **Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL)** with tinnitus
- **Mixed sensorimotor polyneuropathy (Type IV)** with hypertrophied peripheral nerves and increased CSF proteins
- **Cerebellar ataxia**

> **Clinical Pearl**: The combination of **ichthyosis + retinitis pigmentosa + deafness + neuropathy** should immediately raise suspicion for Refsum's disease. The ichthyosis is characteristically mild compared to the neurological and ophthalmological features, which dominate the clinical picture. The peripheral neuropathy is a **hypertrophic demyelinating type** (analogous to Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease), and CSF protein is elevated without pleocytosis (albuminocytologic dissociation). (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

### Histopathological Examination (HPE)

- **Basal cell vacuolation**
- **Orthohyperkeratosis**
- **Special stains with Oil Red O** — numerous **fat globules** within the **basal layer and other keratinocytes** (lipid storage)
- **Ultrastructural studies** — **detached or complete absence of corneocyte lipid envelope**

### Investigations

1. **Histopathology** — as above (Oil Red O positive)
2. **Plasma phytanic acid levels** — elevated, **>200 μmol/L** [Normal (N) <3 μmol/L]
3. **CSF analysis** — elevated protein levels (albuminocytologic dissociation)
4. **Ophthalmological examination** — retinitis pigmentosa, visual field testing

### Treatment

1. **Exclusion of chlorophyll in diet** to **less than 5 mg/day** — phytanic acid is derived from the phytol side-chain of chlorophyll, so dietary restriction of green vegetables, dairy fat, and ruminant animal fat is essential
2. **Avoid rapid weight loss** — fasting or rapid catabolism mobilizes phytanic acid from adipose tissue stores, causing dangerous acute elevations in plasma phytanic acid levels that can precipitate cardiac arrhythmias and neuropathy flares
3. **Lipid apheresis** (plasmapheresis) — for acute crises or dangerously elevated levels; mechanically removes phytanic acid from the circulation
4. **Emollients** — for ichthyosis management

> **Exam Tip**: Refsum's disease is one of the **syndromic ichthyoses** in the 2009 ichthyosis consensus classification. The key exam associations are: **(1)** the only ichthyosis treated primarily by **dietary restriction**; **(2)** phytanic acid is exclusively dietary — no endogenous synthesis; **(3)** avoid fasting (mobilizes stored phytanic acid); **(4)** the triad of **ichthyosis + RP + neuropathy** = Refsum; **(5)** differential includes Sjögren-Larsson syndrome (ichthyosis + spastic diplegia + macular dystrophy — *ALDH3A2* gene) and Rud's syndrome (ichthyosis + epilepsy + hypogonadism). (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; Andrews')

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **">200 mmol/L" (p. 182, Investigation #2)** → **">200 μmol/L"** — The source text states plasma phytanic acid levels ">200 mmol/L" with a normal value of <3. The correct unit is **μmol/L** (micromoles per liter), **not mmol/L** (millimoles per liter). A plasma phytanic acid level of 200 mmol/L would be physiologically impossible and immediately fatal — this would represent a concentration of approximately 60 g/L, which is incompatible with life. Normal plasma phytanic acid is <3 μmol/L; diagnostic levels in Refsum's disease are typically >200 μmol/L (i.e., >0.2 mmol/L). This is a critical unit error that could be tested in examinations. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Exclusion of chlorophyll in diet to less than 5 mg/day" (p. 182, Treatment #1)** → **Clarification:** The source states dietary restriction of chlorophyll to <5 mg/day. More precisely, the dietary target is restriction of **phytanic acid** (and its precursor **phytol**) to <10 mg/day in most references. Chlorophyll contains phytol as a side chain, which is converted to phytanic acid by gut bacteria and intestinal enzymes. The source's phrasing of "chlorophyll" restriction is a simplified way of expressing this — in practice, the dietary restriction encompasses all sources of phytanic acid, not just chlorophyll. The main dietary sources are **dairy products, ruminant animal fats** (beef, lamb), and **certain fish** (herring, tuna, cod) — not just green vegetables. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"heredopathia atactica polyneuritiformis" (p. 181, Definition)** → **Verification:** This is the correct classical eponymous name for Refsum's disease, coined by Sigvald Refsum in 1946. The term describes the three cardinal neurological features: hereditary (heredopathia), ataxic (atactica), polyneuritic (polyneuritiformis). Confirmed accurate. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Phytanoyl CoA hydroxylase" (p. 181, Pathophysiology)** → **Verification:** The source correctly identifies the enzyme as phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase (also known as phytanoyl-CoA 2-hydroxylase, EC 1.14.11.18). This iron- and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase catalyzes the first step of phytanic acid α-oxidation within peroxisomes. Confirmed accurate. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Type IV" polyneuropathy (p. 181, Clinical features)** → **Clarification:** The source describes the neuropathy as "Mixed sensorimotor polyneuropathy (Type IV)." In the context of hereditary motor and sensory neuropathies (HMSN), Refsum's disease is classified as **HMSN Type IV** (also called hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy type IV). This is a **hypertrophic demyelinating polyneuropathy** with onion-bulb formation on nerve biopsy. The "Type IV" designation specifically refers to the Refsum variant of HMSN. Confirmed accurate. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Oil Red O" staining (p. 182, HPE)** → **Verification:** Oil Red O is a fat-soluble diazo dye used on frozen sections (not paraffin-embedded) to demonstrate neutral lipids and triglycerides. In Refsum's disease, the accumulated phytanic acid (a branched-chain fatty acid) is stored as lipid droplets within keratinocytes and other cells, which stain positively with Oil Red O. This is an important diagnostic histopathological finding. Confirmed accurate. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Corneocyte lipid envelope" absence (p. 182, HPE — ultrastructural)** → **Verification:** The corneocyte lipid envelope (CLE) is a covalently bound lipid monolayer on the outer surface of the cornified envelope. In Refsum's disease, the accumulated phytanic acid disrupts normal lipid metabolism, leading to detachment or complete absence of the CLE on electron microscopy. This contributes to the barrier dysfunction and ichthyosis. Confirmed accurate. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Lipid apheresis" (p. 182, Treatment #3)** → **Verification:** The source lists "lipid apheresis" as a treatment. This is correctly stated — plasmapheresis or specific lipid apheresis (lipoprotein apheresis) can be used to acutely lower plasma phytanic acid levels in crisis situations. It is typically reserved for patients with dangerously high levels (>800–1000 μmol/L) or acute neurological deterioration. Confirmed accurate. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)
