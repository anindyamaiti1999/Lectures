# Q5. Discuss the management of stable psoriasis and add a note on phototherapy in psoriasis.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 160–161 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Management

#### Lifestyle and Supportive Care
- Regular moisturization
- Avoid known triggers, e.g., stress, smoking
- Patient education
- Adherence to therapy

#### 1. Topical Therapies

- **Corticosteroids** — medium/high/low potency: reduce inflammation, pruritic and in combination with salicylic acid (SA) reduce scaling.
- **Vitamin D analogues** — maintain remission and persistent plaque buildup reducing keratinocyte proliferation.
- **Topical calcineurin inhibitors** — effective in sensitive areas, i.e., face and genitals.
- **Topical tar** — can reduce scaling and inflammation.

#### 2. Phototherapy
- **NB-UVB** — maintain clearance and prevent flares.
- **PUVA** — rare but effective in some cases.

#### 3. Systemic Therapy
- Acitretin
- Methotrexate

#### 4. Biologics

#### 5. Excimer Laser (308 nm)
Targets specific plaques with UVB light, average ~6 treatments delivered biweekly effective.

#### 6. Photodynamic Therapy (PDT)
Useful in chronic stable psoriasis, with photosensitizers like aminolaevulinic acids and light sources such as lasers. The principle is application of a photosensitizing agent such as porphyrins to skin which absorbs light energy and produces toxic oxygen species that damage the hyperproliferative cells.

### Phototherapy in Psoriasis

Sunlight benefits psoriasis by using UV light to induce apoptosis of activated cells, although 5% can be photosensitive.

#### Common UVL Sources
- UVB — broadband and narrowband
- UVA
- UVA1

#### In Psoriasis, Phototherapy Given As:
- UVB phototherapy
- NB-UVB
- Photodynamic therapy
- Photochemotherapy
- Excimer laser

---

### UVB Phototherapy

Narrow-band UVB (311 ± 2 nm) has been found superior to conventional broadband UVB (300–320 nm).

The most effective wavelength range is between 311 and 313 nm, typically used in NB-UVB.

#### Mechanism of Action (MOA)
UVB induces apoptosis in T cells leading to a shift from a proinflammatory immune response to an anti-inflammatory one.

This depletion of epidermal T cells contributes to the clearing of psoriatic lesions.

#### Treatment Protocol
- UVB therapy involves gradually increasing **suberythemogenic** doses (below levels that cause redness) over 20–30 sessions.
- 80% of MED is used as starting dose, with 20% increments if no erythema is seen.
- Poor results can occur if low doses are used, and phototoxicity can arise from high doses or rapid increases.

#### Effectiveness
- 90% show clearance after 20–30 sessions.
- Requires more frequent treatment for maintenance.
- Maintenance involves weekly/biweekly sessions.
- To enhance penetration, psoriatic plaques are often treated with petrolatum before UVB exposure.

#### Examine Laser in UVB Therapy
- The 308 nm excimer laser emits a monochromatic coherent UVB beam.
- It delivers supra-erythemogenic doses to psoriatic plaques while sparing healthy skin.
- Indications include stable and recalcitrant psoriasis on elbows and knees.
- Side effects have led to recommendations for medium doses (about 3 MED).

---

### Photochemotherapy (PUVA)

PUVA — a combination of UVA and psoralens.

- Psoralens: UVA radiation at 320–400 nm.
- Psoralens such as 8-methoxy psoralen and 5-methoxy psoralen intercalate with DNA, forming bifunctional photo adducts that inhibit DNA synthesis and induce apoptosis in cutaneous lymphocytes.

#### MOA
- PUVA interferes with DNA synthesis, reducing cellular proliferation.
- It also includes apoptosis of lymphocytes, leading to localized immune suppression in psoriatic lesions.
- Psoralens also produce free radicals, which damage cell membranes and activate eicosanoid mediators, further reducing psoriasis activity.

#### Indications
Moderate-to-severe psoriasis; ≥50 years.

#### Contraindications
- Photosensitive disorder
- Pregnancy
- Renal/hepatic/cardiovascular diseases
- History of skin cancer
- Regular eye protection and shielding of genitalia required to prevent long-term damage.

#### Side Effects
- **Acute:** Nausea, vomiting, headaches, erythema, pruritis and blistering.
- **Long-term:** Cataracts, hypertrichosis, squamous cell carcinoma.

#### Advances in PUVA
- New formulations such as liquid topical methoxsalen for better absorption and efficacy.
- **Psoralen baths** followed by PUVA offers localized treatment with fewer systemic side effects.

#### Combination
- *With topical treatment:*
  - Coal tar
  - Urea
  - Dithranol
  - Salicylic acid
- *With systemic treatment:*
  - Acitretin
  - Methotrexate
  - Etretinate (REPUVA with retinoids)

#### Additional Side Effects (Right Column, Page 161)
- Squamous cell carcinoma
- Ocular damage and cataracts

---

### Photodynamic Therapy (PDT)

#### MOA
Uses photosensitizing agents (e.g., porphyrins) combined with light to target hyperproliferative cells in psoriasis.

The light activates porphyrins leading to the formation of toxic oxygen species that selectively damage psoriatic cells.

#### Photosensitizers and Light Sources
- **1st generation: Hematoporphyrin** — cause prolonged photosensitivity.
- **2nd generation: Verteporphyrin** — reduced photosensitivity and used for psoriasis.

**Light sources:** Laser, LED arrays.

**In psoriasis:** Notable improvement with 5-aminolaevulinic acid.

#### Side Effects
- Sensation of warmth, stinging, and sunburn-like reactions.
- Patients need to avoid bright sunlight for 2–3 days post-treatment and wear sunglasses to prevent eye damage.

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **"Suberythemogenic" spelling** — The source text on page 160 appears to read "suberythe-mogenic" (hyphenated across line break). The correct term is **suberythemogenic** (below the erythema-inducing threshold). Corrected in the output. (Ref: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, 9th ed.)

- **"80% of MED… with 20% increments"** — Verified. Standard NB-UVB protocols typically start at 50–80% of the minimal erythema dose (MED) and increase by 10–20% per session if no erythema occurs. The source's "80% with 20% increments" is within the accepted range but represents a more aggressive starting protocol. (Ref: Bolognia Dermatology, 4th ed.; British Photodermatology Group guidelines)

- **"90% show clearance after 20–30 sessions"** — This figure is optimistic. Literature more commonly reports **70–80% achieving PASI-75** (75% improvement) with NB-UVB over 20–35 sessions, not 90% complete clearance. Retained as written in the source but flagged as potentially overstated. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; Cochrane reviews of phototherapy in psoriasis)

- **Excimer laser dose "about 3 MED"** — Verified. Excimer laser for psoriasis typically uses supra-erythemogenic doses; starting doses of 2–4 MED are standard, with 3 MED being a common recommendation to balance efficacy and side effects. (Ref: Bolognia; Feldman SR et al.)

- **"Examine laser" heading (page 160)** — The source text reads "Examine laser in UVB therapy." This is likely intended as **"Excimer laser in UVB therapy."** The word "Examine" appears to be an OCR/autocorrect artefact for "Excimer." However, the heading is retained as per source with the correct term used in the body text below. (Ref: Standard dermatology literature)

- **PUVA indications "≥50 years"** — The source states indications as "Moderate-to-severe psoriasis; ≥50 years." This age qualifier is unusual; PUVA is generally indicated for moderate-to-severe psoriasis regardless of age (though older patients may be preferred over biologics for cost considerations). The ≥50 threshold is not a standard criterion in major references. Retained as per source but flagged. (Ref: Rook's; Andrews')

- **Verteporphyrin vs. Verteporfin** — The source reads "Verteporphyrin." The correct INN (international nonproprietary name) is **verteporfin** (trade name Visudyne). "Verteporphyrin" is an alternate chemical nomenclature that is acceptable but less commonly used in clinical literature. (Ref: WHO INN list; Bolognia)

- **"5-aminolaevulinic acid"** — Verified. This is the correct British spelling (also written as 5-aminolevulinic acid / 5-ALA). It is a prodrug that is metabolized to protoporphyrin IX, a photosensitizer used in PDT. (Ref: Rook's; British National Formulary)

- **Etretinate (REPUVA)** — Etretinate has been withdrawn from the market in many countries (including the US since 1998) due to its extremely long half-life and teratogenicity. Its mention here is historically accurate but clinically outdated. Acitretin (RE-PUVA with acitretin) has replaced it. Retained as per source but flagged. (Ref: Bolognia; Andrews')
