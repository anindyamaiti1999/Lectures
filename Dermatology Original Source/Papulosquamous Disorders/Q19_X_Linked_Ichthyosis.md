# Q19. Discuss X-linked ichthyosis.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 176–177 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Definition & Synonyms

- Also known as **steroid sulfatase deficiency** (STS deficiency), **X-linked recessive ichthyosis (XRI)**
- An **X-linked recessive** genodermatosis characterized by deficiency of the enzyme **steroid sulfatase (STS)**
- Occurs in **1 in 2,000–9,500** male births
- **Exclusively affects males**; females are **asymptomatic carriers**

### Pathogenesis

The source provides a **vertical pathogenesis flowchart**:

> **Deletion of STS gene on chromosome Xp22.31** (or point mutations)
> ↓
> **Steroid sulfatase deficiency**
> ↓
> **Impaired hydrolysis of cholesterol sulfate and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS)**
> ↓
> **Accumulation of cholesterol 3-sulfate in the epidermis**
> ↓
> **Inhibition of transglutaminase-1**
> ↓
> **Disruption in formation of cornified envelope and epidermal barrier**

#### Placental STS Deficiency & Obstetric Significance

- In pregnant women carrying an affected male fetus with **steroid sulfatase deficiency**:
  - **Low or absent levels of estrogen** (estriol) in urine and amniotic fluid — due to placental inability to cleave DHEAS to free DHEA (a precursor for estriol synthesis)
  - **Failure of spontaneous initiation of labor** — requires induction or cesarean section
  - Low maternal urinary estriol is a diagnostic clue during pregnancy

> **High-Yield Point**: The placental STS deficiency explains why pregnancies carrying XLI-affected males often show **low maternal estriol** and **failure to progress in labor**. This is because the placenta cannot convert DHEAS to DHEA, which is the obligate precursor for estriol synthesis. This is one of the few ichthyoses with a specific **prenatal/obstetric marker**.

### Clinical Features

#### Neonatal Presentation:
- **Mild erythroderma** and **generalized peeling**
- **Exfoliation of large, translucent scales**

#### Infancy & Childhood:
- **Typical polygonal, dark brown, adherent scales** — distributed symmetrically on **extremities**, **trunk**, and **neck**
- **"Dirty neck disease"** — characteristic darkening and scaling of the neck (pathognomonic feature)
- **Fine scaling of scalp** diminishes over time

#### Distribution Pattern:
- **Flexures unaffected** (unlike atopic dermatitis)
- Other features include **characteristic sparing of palms, soles, and face** (except preauricular area)
- **Preauricular area** involvement — pathognomonic

#### Temporal Evolution:
- **Improves in summer** but does **not improve with age** (unlike ichthyosis vulgaris, which improves at puberty)

#### Extracutaneous Manifestations:
- **Asymptomatic corneal opacities** — comma-shaped or dot-like Descemet membrane opacities; present in **female carriers** as well (slit-lamp examination)
- **Deuteranopia** (color blindness — rare association)
- **Cryptorchidism** — risk of **testicular cancer** and **hypogonadism**

#### Contiguous Gene Syndromes (Large Deletions at Xp22.31):

When larger deletions extend beyond the STS gene to involve contiguous genes, the following additional features may occur:

| Deleted Gene(s) | Associated Condition |
|---|---|
| **KAL1** (ANOS1) | **Kallmann syndrome** (hypogonadism, anosmia) |
| **NLGN4X** / other genes | **ADHD**, developmental delay, seizures |
| **SH3KBP1** region | **Pyloric hypertrophy** |
| Multiple contiguous genes | **Acute lymphoblastic leukemia**, abdominal wall defects |

> Reported cases of contiguous gene deletion syndromes include:
> - Seizures
> - ADHD
> - Developmental delay
> - Pyloric hypertrophy
> - Acute lymphoblastic leukemia
> - Abdominal wall defects

### Investigations

#### 1. Histopathology (HPE):
- **Hyperkeratosis** or **parakeratosis** overlying a **normal or slightly thickened granular layer**
  - This is a **key distinguishing feature from ichthyosis vulgaris (IV)**, where the granular layer is **absent or markedly reduced**
- **Follicular hyperkeratosis** may be present

#### 2. Electron Microscopy:
- **Increased size and number of B4 granules** (membrane-coating granules)
- **Retention of desmosomes** in the stratum corneum
- Cells contain a **large number of melanosomes** (explaining the dark color of scales)

#### 3. Genetic & Biochemical Testing:
- **Comparative genomic hybridization (CGH)** — detects Xp22.31 deletions
- **Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)** — confirms STS gene deletion
- **Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)** — detects gene deletions/mutations

#### 4. Prenatal Diagnosis:
- **Chorionic villous sampling (CVS)** — molecular testing
- **Amniotic fluid samples** — STS activity assay
- **Decreased serum estriol levels** — in maternal blood (due to placental STS deficiency)

#### 5. Maternal Urine Testing:
- **Presence of nonhydrolyzed sulfated steroids** in maternal urine — diagnostic clue

#### 6. Serum Lipoprotein Analysis:
- **Serum lipoprotein electrophoresis by chromatography** or **spectrophotometry** — elevated cholesterol sulfate migrates with LDL fraction

#### 7. Enzyme Activity Assay:
- **Steroid sulfatase enzymatic activity** measurement in **leukocytes**, **fibroblasts**, **keratinocytes**, and **placental tissue** — absent or markedly reduced in affected males

### Differential Diagnosis

- **X-linked recessive chondrodysplasia punctata** (*Conradi–Hünermann syndrome*)
- **Kallmann syndrome** (hypogonadism, anosmia) — when contiguous gene deletion involves KAL1/ANOS1
- **Rud syndrome** (hypogonadism, ichthyosis, seizures — historical overlap)
- **Ichthyosis vulgaris** — key distinction: IV has **absent/reduced granular layer**; XLI has **normal/thickened granular layer**

### Treatment

- **Topical humectants** — especially **propylene glycol**
- **Keratolytics** — effective alone or in combination (salicylic acid, urea, lactic acid)
- **Retinoids**: Rarely — systemic retinoids (acitretin) reserved for severe cases

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **Granular layer distinction — XLI vs IV (p. 177, HPE section)** → **Verification:** The source correctly states that XLI shows **hyperkeratosis or parakeratosis overlying a normal or slightly thickened granular layer**. This is a critical histopathological differentiating feature: in **ichthyosis vulgaris (IV)**, the granular layer is **absent or markedly reduced** (due to loss-of-function mutations in filaggrin — *FLG* gene), whereas in **XLI**, the granular layer is **preserved or even thickened**. This is one of the most commonly tested histologic distinctions among the ichthyoses. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; Andrews')

- **"B4 granules" (p. 177, electron microscopy section)** → **Correction:** The source text reads "Increased size and number of B4 granules." The likely intended term is **"84 granules"** or more precisely **membrane-coating granules (MCGs)** / **lamellar bodies** (also historically called Odland bodies). Some older nomenclature systems used numerical designations for ultrastructural granule types. In XLI, electron microscopy shows increased and abnormal lamellar bodies with retention of intercellular lipid material in the stratum corneum, reflecting the cholesterol sulfate accumulation that prevents normal desquamation. The retained desmosomes explain the adherent nature of the scales. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Cholesterol 3-sulfate" (p. 176, pathogenesis)** → **Verification:** The source correctly identifies **cholesterol 3-sulfate** (cholesterol sulfate) as the accumulating substrate. In normal epidermis, STS cleaves cholesterol sulfate → free cholesterol, which is essential for the lipid barrier of the stratum corneum and for the normal desquamation process. Cholesterol sulfate accumulation in XLI inhibits serine proteases (including KLK5/KLK7) and transglutaminase-1, disrupting both desquamation and cornified envelope assembly. The result is retention hyperkeratosis (scales stick rather than shed), explaining the adherent, dirty-brown appearance. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **Placental STS deficiency and labor failure (p. 176, pathogenesis)** → **Verification:** The source correctly notes that pregnant women carrying affected fetuses show **low or absent estrogen (estriol) in urine and amniotic fluid**, leading to **failure of spontaneous initiation of labor**. This is a well-established obstetric correlation: the placenta requires STS to convert fetal adrenal DHEAS → DHEA, which is then aromatized to estriol. Without STS, estriol levels remain low, and the normal estriol-mediated cascade for labor initiation is impaired. Low maternal serum estriol on prenatal screening (triple/quad screen) can be the first diagnostic clue. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **Corneal opacities (p. 177, extracutaneous manifestations)** → **Verification:** The source correctly mentions **asymptomatic corneal opacities**. These are specifically **comma-shaped** or **dot-like opacities** on **Descemet's membrane** (posterior corneal stroma), visible on **slit-lamp examination**. Critically, these opacities are also present in **female carriers** (~25% of heterozygous women), making slit-lamp examination a useful tool for carrier detection. The opacities do not impair vision. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; Andrews')

- **Cryptorchidism and testicular cancer risk (p. 177)** → **Verification:** The source correctly lists **cryptorchidism** as an extracutaneous feature and notes the associated risk of **testicular cancer**. Cryptorchidism occurs in approximately **10–20%** of males with XLI (higher in contiguous gene deletion syndromes). The cancer risk is attributed to the undescended testis itself (as with any cause of cryptorchidism) rather than a direct effect of STS deficiency. Orchidopexy should be performed early. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Deuteranopia" (p. 177)** → **Verification:** The source mentions **deuteranopia** (green color blindness) as a rare association. This is likely included because the STS gene at Xp22.31 is on the same X chromosome arm as opsin genes, though classical red-green color blindness genes (OPN1LW/OPN1MW) are at Xq28 (far telomeric). The association may reflect ascertainment bias or co-segregation in families with large X-chromosome structural variants. This is not a consistently reported feature in major references. (Ref: Bolognia; Andrews')

- **"SULTZBI" (p. 177, other mutations for lamellar ichthyosis)** → **Note:** The right column of page 177 lists genes associated with lamellar ichthyosis (in the Q20 section), including "SULTZBI." This likely represents **SULT2B1** (sulfotransferase family 2B member 1), which is involved in cholesterol sulfate metabolism. The OCR/typographic error converts "SULT2B1" → "SULTZBI." This gene is part of the ARCI gene panel but pertains to Q20 (lamellar ichthyosis), not Q19. (Ref: Rook's)

The "dirty neck" sign is virtually pathognomonic — the dark brown, adherent scales concentrated on the lateral neck give the appearance of unwashed skin. This is a commonly tested clinical vignette feature.

- **Page 177 (left column, extracutaneous features and contiguous gene syndromes):** The source lists corneal opacities (female carriers too), deuteranopia (rare), and cryptorchidism with testicular cancer risk. The contiguous gene syndromes section is important: it explains that larger deletions at Xp22.31 can extend to involve neighboring genes, producing a more complex phenotype. The reported cases (seizures, ADHD, developmental delay, pyloric hypertrophy, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, abdominal wall defects) suggest that any male presenting with ichthyosis plus neurologic or developmental features should be evaluated for a contiguous gene deletion syndrome, not just isolated STS deficiency.

- **Page 177 (left column, investigations — HPE granular layer):** The HPE section emphasizes the **normal or slightly thickened granular layer** — this is the single most important histologic feature distinguishing XLI from ichthyosis vulgaris (IV). In IV (FLG mutations), the granular layer is absent or reduced; in XLI (STS mutations), it is preserved. The electron microscopy findings (increased B4/lamellar granules, retained desmosomes, abundant melanosomes) provide the ultrastructural correlate of the clinical appearance: adherent, dark, difficult-to-shed scales.

- **Page 177 (left column, investigations — prenatal and biochemical):** The source lists a comprehensive investigation panel: CVS, amniotic fluid analysis, decreased maternal serum estriol, nonhydrolyzed sulfated steroids in maternal urine, serum lipoprotein electrophoresis, and STS enzyme activity in leukocytes/fibroblasts/keratinocytes/placental tissue. This reflects the dual diagnostic approach — biochemical (enzyme assay) and molecular (CGH/FISH/PCR). The decreased maternal estriol on prenatal screening is a critical clinical pearl: it is often the first clue that prompts further evaluation for XLI in the fetus.

- **Page 177 (left column, differential diagnosis):** The differential includes X-linked recessive chondrodysplasia punctata (Conradi–Hünermann), Kallmann syndrome (when contiguous deletion involves KAL1), and Rud syndrome. The Conradi–Hünermann association is clinically important because both conditions are X-linked with skin findings, though CDP is X-linked dominant in females (CDPX2/EBP gene at Xp11.23) and is distinct from the Xp22.31 deletion syndrome.

- **Page 177 (left column, treatment):** Treatment is straightforward and palliative: topical humectants (propylene glycol), keratolytics (effective alone or in combination), and rarely systemic retinoids. Unlike collodion babies (Q18), keratolytics are safe and effective in established XLI because the epidermal barrier, while abnormal, is not as catastrophically compromised as in the neonatal collodion membrane. This contrast is worth noting for exam purposes.
