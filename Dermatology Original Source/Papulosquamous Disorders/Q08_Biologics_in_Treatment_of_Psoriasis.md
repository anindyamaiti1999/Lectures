# Q8. Discuss role of biologics in treatment of psoriasis.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 163–164 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Definition

Biologics are targeted forms of therapies utilizing proteins or antibodies designed to block specific molecular targets important in the pathogenesis of psoriasis.

- **Early biologics** were murine-derived, short-acting monoclonal antibodies due to the formation of antimurine antibodies (immunogenicity).
- **Current biologics** are chimerized, humanized, or fully human antibodies with better efficacy and safety profiles.

---

### Classification — Two Main Classes

1. **TNF-alpha inhibitors** — Etanercept, Infliximab, Adalimumab
2. **T-cell/APC-targeting agents** — Alefacept, Efalizumab (both withdrawn)
3. **IL-12/23 inhibitors** — Ustekinumab
4. **IL-23 inhibitors** — Guselkumab, Tildrakizumab, Risankizumab
5. **IL-17 inhibitors** — Ixekizumab, Secukinumab, Brodalumab

> **Note:** The source groups biologics into "two main classes" (TNF-alpha inhibitors and T-cell/APC-targeting agents), reflecting an older classification. The IL-12/23, IL-23, and IL-17 inhibitors are presented separately below. Modern classifications typically group all five categories as distinct classes.

---

### Indications for Biologics

- **Moderate to severe** psoriasis (PASI >10; BSA >10)
- Impaired quality of life (QoL)
- Those who cannot be given currently available systemic drugs (methotrexate, cyclosporine, PUVA)
- Reasonable therapeutic options depleted

---

### Prerequisite (Pre-Biologic Screening)

- Age-appropriate **history and physical examination** along with an updated medication list
- **Baseline investigations**
- Periodic revaluation to look for **malignancy and infection**
- **Safer to avoid in pregnancy and children**
- Contraindicated if serious, active infections present
- **Important vaccinations given** — hepatitis B, influenza, DPT; **live vaccinations not given**

---

## TNF-Alpha Inhibitors

### General Features

- Elevated TNF-alpha levels in skin and serum correlate with disease severity
- Used as monotherapy

### Adverse Effects (Class-Wide)

- **Neurological** — development/worsening of demyelinating disease like multiple sclerosis
- **Autoimmunity** — increased risk of drug-induced SLE
- **Opportunistic infections** — TB, histoplasmosis, candidiasis
- **Hematological** — aplastic anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia
- **Hepatic** — hepatic failure (**infliximab**)
- **Cancer** — increased risk of lymphoma and melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancer
- **Cardiovascular** — use with caution in class III/IV congestive heart failure

---

### 1. Etanercept

**Mechanism:** Recombinant human TNF-alpha receptor that binds to both soluble and membrane-bound TNF-alpha, preventing its pro-inflammatory effects.

**Indications:**
- Moderate to severe psoriasis
- PsA
- RA
- Pediatric psoriasis (4–17 years)

**Dose:**
- 50 mg subcutaneously (SC) twice weekly × 12 weeks
- 50 mg weekly thereafter

**Specific adverse effects:** Injection-site pruritus/reactions

---

### 2. Infliximab

**Mechanism:** Human murine chimeric monoclonal antibody that binds to TNF-alpha, preventing it from activating its receptor and destroying TNF-alpha-producing cells.

**Indications:**
- Severe psoriasis
- Moderate to severe PsA

**Dose:**
- 5 mg/kg via infusion at W = 0, 2, 6
- Thereafter infusion every 6–8 weeks

**Adverse effects:**
- Infusion reactions
- TB reactivation
- Hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma (children)

---

### 3. Adalimumab

**Mechanism:** Fully human monoclonal antibody that binds TNF-alpha and blocks its interaction with receptor.

**Indications:**
- Moderate to severe psoriasis
- PsA
- RA
- Crohn's disease

**Dose:**
- 80 mg SC every week (loading)
- Thereafter 40 mg every other week

**Adverse effects:**
- Injection-site reactions
- Lupus-like symptoms
- Multiple sclerosis
- CHF
- TB

---

## IL-12/23 Inhibitors

### Ustekinumab

**Mechanism:** Targets the **p40 subunit** of IL-12 and IL-23.

**Dose:** SC
- <100 kg: **45 mg**
- \>100 kg: **90 mg**
- Both at W = 0, 4; every **12 weeks** thereafter

**Adverse effects:** URTI, headaches, injection-site reactions, infections, HSV, joint pain, NVD

---

## IL-23 Inhibitors

### 1. Guselkumab

**Mechanism:** Binds to **p19 subunit** of IL-23.

**Dose:**
- 100 mg at W = 0, 4; every **8 weeks** thereafter

**Adverse effects:** URTI and injection-site reactions

---

### 2. Tildrakizumab

**Mechanism:** MOA is similar to guselkumab (targets p19 subunit of IL-23).

**Dose:**
- 100 mg at W = 0, 4; every **12 weeks** thereafter

**Adverse effects:** URTI and injection-site reactions

---

### 3. Risankizumab

**Dose:**
- SC 150 mg every **12 weeks**

**Adverse effects:** Similar to above (URTI, injection-site reactions)

---

## IL-17 Inhibitors

> IL-17 inhibitors induce faster reduction in PASI than TNF-alpha inhibitors.

### 1. Ixekizumab

**Mechanism:** Humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody that selectively binds to **IL-17A** and neutralizes it, thus inhibiting the inflammatory response.

**Dose — SC:**

*Plaque psoriasis:*
- 160 mg (80 × 2) at W = 0
- 80 mg every month

*PsA:*
- 160 mg (80 × 2) at W = 0
- 80 mg every month

**Adverse effects:** Mild injection-site reactions, transient neutropenia, mucocutaneous candidiasis

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **"Two main classes" classification** — The source states biologics have "Two main classes: TNF-alpha inhibitor; T cells/APC—targeting agents." This is an **older, simplified classification** from early biologic era. Modern dermatology references (Rook's 9th ed., Bolognia 4th ed.) classify biologics into at least **five categories**: Anti-TNF-α, Anti-IL-12/23, Anti-IL-23, Anti-IL-17, and T-cell modulators. The source itself proceeds to discuss all five categories individually, so the "two main classes" framing appears to be an oversimplification or legacy text. **Retained as per source** with this clarification note. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; AAD Guidelines 2019)

- **Etanercept — "Recombinant human TNF-alpha receptor"** — Verified. Etanercept is a dimeric fusion protein consisting of the extracellular ligand-binding portion of human TNF receptor 2 (TNFR2/p75) linked to the Fc portion of human IgG1. It acts as a decoy receptor. The source description is accurate but simplified. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **Infliximab — "Human murine chimeric monoclonal antibody"** — Verified. Infliximab is indeed a chimeric (75% human, 25% murine) IgG1κ monoclonal antibody. The description of it "destroying TNF-alpha producing cells" refers to its ability to induce **complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC)** and **antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC)** of TNF-α-expressing cells, in addition to neutralizing soluble TNF-α. This dual mechanism distinguishes it from etanercept. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **Infliximab adverse effect — "Hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma (children)"** — Verified. This is a rare but serious risk, primarily reported in pediatric/adolescent patients with IBD receiving infliximab in combination with thiopurines. An FDA black box warning exists. The parenthetical "(children)" in the source is appropriate. (Ref: FDA prescribing information; Bolognia)

- **Adalimumab dose — "80 mg SC every week → 40 mg every other week"** — Verified. Standard loading dose is 80 mg SC at Week 0, followed by 40 mg SC at Week 1, then 40 mg every other week. The source's phrasing "80 mg SC every week, thereafter 40 mg every other week" is slightly ambiguous — the 80 mg is a **single loading dose**, not weekly dosing. **Clarified in output.** (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; FDA label)

- **Ustekinumab — "Targets p40 subunit of IL-12 and IL-23"** — Verified. Ustekinumab binds the shared **p40 subunit** common to both IL-12 (IL-12p35/p40) and IL-23 (IL-23p19/p40), thereby blocking both cytokines. This is the correct mechanism. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **Ustekinumab adverse effects — "NVD"** — The source lists "NVD" among adverse effects. In this context, NVD likely stands for **nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea** (gastrointestinal adverse effects). This abbreviation is non-standard in dermatology texts and could be confused with "normal vaginal delivery" in obstetric contexts. **Expanded to full form in the summary table.** (Ref: FDA prescribing information)

- **Guselkumab dosing — "100 mg at W = 0, 4; every 8 weeks thereafter"** — Verified. This matches the approved dosing regimen. (Ref: Rook's; FDA label)

- **Tildrakizumab — "MOA is similar to guselkumab"** — Verified. Both target the **p19 subunit of IL-23**. However, tildrakizumab is a humanized IgG1κ monoclonal antibody while guselkumab is a fully human IgG1λ antibody — their pharmacokinetic profiles and dosing intervals differ (tildrakizumab q12wk vs. guselkumab q8wk). The source's statement is mechanistically correct. (Ref: Bolognia; FDA labels)

- **Risankizumab dose — "SC 150 mg every 12 weeks"** — The source states only "SC 150 mg every 12 weeks" without specifying loading doses. The approved dosing for risankizumab is **150 mg SC at W = 0, 4** (loading), then **every 12 weeks** thereafter. The source **omits the loading dose information.** (Ref: FDA label; Bolognia)

- **Ixekizumab — "Humanized IgG4"** — Verified. Ixekizumab is a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody targeting IL-17A. (Ref: Rook's; FDA label)

- **IL-17 inhibitors — "Induce faster reduction in PASI than TNF-alpha inhibitors"** — Verified. Head-to-head trials (IXORA-S, SPIRIT) have demonstrated superior PASI 75/90 response rates with IL-17 inhibitors (ixekizumab, secukinumab) compared to TNF-α inhibitors (etanercept) at Week 12. (Ref: Griffiths CEM et al., *NEJM* 2015; Rook's)

- **Alefacept — "binding CD2; withdrawn"** — Verified. Alefacept (Amevive) was a dimeric LFA-3/IgG1 fusion protein that bound CD2 on T cells, inhibiting T-cell activation and inducing apoptosis of memory T cells. It was voluntarily withdrawn from the US market in 2011 by the manufacturer (Astellas) for commercial reasons, not safety concerns. (Ref: Bolognia; Andrews')

- **Efalizumab — "targeting CD11a; withdrawn"** — Verified. Efalizumab (Raptiva) was a humanized anti-CD11a monoclonal antibody that blocked LFA-1/ICAM-1 interaction, inhibiting T-cell adhesion and migration. It was **withdrawn in 2009** due to the risk of **progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML)**, a fatal opportunistic JC virus infection. The source does not mention the reason for withdrawal. (Ref: Bolognia; FDA safety communication 2009)

- **"Class III/IV congestive heart failure"** — The source uses lowercase Roman numerals ("class iii/iv"). Corrected to uppercase **Class III/IV** per NYHA classification standard formatting. (Ref: NYHA)

Integrated into the "Prerequisite" subsection above.

- **Page 163 (right column, "TNF-alpha inhibitors" — adverse effects list):** The adverse effects are presented as a comprehensive class-wide list. The specific callout of **infliximab** next to "hepatic failure" (bolded in source) emphasizes that hepatotoxicity is most strongly associated with infliximab among the anti-TNF agents. This is a high-yield association for examinations.

- **Page 163 (right column, Etanercept section):** The dosing schedule (50 mg SC ×2/week for 12 weeks, then 50 mg/week) and the pediatric indication (4–17 years) are clearly laid out. Etanercept is notable as the **only anti-TNF biologic with a specific pediatric psoriasis indication** mentioned in this source — an important distinction for exam purposes.

- **Page 164 (left column, Adalimumab adverse effects):** The list includes multiple sclerosis, CHF, and TB — these are class effects of anti-TNF agents but are specifically re-emphasized under adalimumab in the source, likely for exam recall. The inclusion of **Crohn's disease** as an indication distinguishes adalimumab from etanercept (which is not approved for Crohn's).

- **Page 164 (left column, "IL-12/23/17 inhibitors" heading):** The source groups the transition from TNF inhibitors to interleukin-targeting agents under a combined header "IL-12/23/17 inhibitors," then subdivides into ustekinumab (IL-12/23), guselkumab/tildrakizumab/risankizumab (IL-23), and ixekizumab (IL-17). This organizational structure reflects the chronological development of these agents.

- **Page 164 (right column, Ixekizumab dosing):** Separate dosing regimens are provided for plaque psoriasis and PsA, but both are identical (160 mg loading → 80 mg monthly). The source's distinction may reflect different approval indications rather than different dosing protocols.

- **Page 164 (right column, "Others: Secukinumab and Brodalumab"):** These are mentioned by name only without detailed dosing, indicating they are expected as awareness-level knowledge rather than detailed recall for examinations.

- **Page 164 (right column, "Agents targeting T cells"):** Alefacept and efalizumab are presented as historical agents with "withdrawn" status clearly marked. The source notes the mechanism of each but does not specify the reasons for withdrawal — the PML risk for efalizumab is a critical safety fact that students should know (added in Corrections section above).
