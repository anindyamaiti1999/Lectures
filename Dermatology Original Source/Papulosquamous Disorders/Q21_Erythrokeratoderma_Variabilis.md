# Q21. Describe erythrokeratoderma variabilis, variants, and management.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 178–179 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Definition & Synonyms

- **Erythrokeratoderma variabilis (EKV)**
- Also known as ***Mendes da Costa* syndrome** (after the Dutch dermatologist who first described it in 1925)
- Also referred to as **erythrokeratodermia variabilis et progressiva**
- A **rare** genodermatosis characterized by **two distinct cutaneous components**: (1) **transient, migratory erythematous patches** and (2) **fixed, hyperkeratotic plaques**
- **Predominantly inherited in an autosomal dominant (AD) manner**

### Inheritance & Genetics (Pathogenesis)

- **Autosomal dominant** inheritance (primary)

#### Causative Genes:

| Gene | Protein | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **GJB3** | Connexin 31 (Cx31) | Gap junction protein — forms transcellular channels |
| **GJB4** | Connexin 30.3 (Cx30.3) | Gap junction protein — forms transcellular channels |
| **GJA1** | Connexin 43 (Cx43) | Gap junction protein — forms transcellular channels |

#### Pathogenic Mechanism (Flowchart):

1. **Mutations in GJB3 / GJB4 / GJA1** (genes coding gap junction protein connexins)
2. ↓
3. **Form abnormal transcellular channels**
4. ↓
5. **Amino acid substitutions of conserved residues** → altered structure/activity of gap junctions
6. ↓
7. **Cell death, impaired epidermal differentiation**

> **High-Yield Point**: Gap junctions (connexins) allow direct cell-to-cell communication via small-molecule and ion transfer. Mutations disrupt this intercellular signaling, leading to defective epidermal proliferation and differentiation — manifesting as the characteristic dual phenotype of transient erythema and fixed hyperkeratosis.

### Clinical Features

#### Onset:
- **50% present at birth**; **90% manifest in the first year** of life

#### A. Erythema (Transient/Migratory Component):

- **Transient erythematous patches** — the more variable and dynamic component
- **Well-demarcated, geographic** appearance; may show a **circinate or target-like** pattern
- **Coalesce into large figurate patches**
- Sometimes surrounded by a **blanched (pale) halo**
- **Lasts for hours to days** — then resolves spontaneously
- **Variability** in number, size, location, and duration of patches — hence the name *"variabilis"*
- **Erythematous component subsides with age** — tends to become less prominent over time

#### B. Hyperkeratosis (Fixed/Stable Component):

- **Sharply demarcated yellow-brown to pink-brown thickened plaques** with **geographic borders**
- **Distribution**: limbs, buttocks, trunk, palms, and soles
- **Symmetrical**
- **Verrucous, velvety** texture with **fine, adherent collarette of scales**
- **Accentuation of skin markings**
- **Improvement over time → stabilizes** (the hyperkeratotic component tends to improve and then plateau)
- The **hyperkeratotic plaques** are **more stable** than the erythema and represent the "fixed" component

#### Triggers (for Erythematous Flares):

- Emotional stress
- Sudden temperature changes
- Mechanical friction
- Sun exposure

#### Additional Features:

- *Palms and soles are severely affected:*
  - **Diffuse fissuring keratoderma**
  - **Palmoplantar hyperlinearity**
- **Hypohidrosis and heat intolerance** due to obstruction of sweat ducts
- **Secondary nail dystrophy**
- **Onychomycosis**
- **Digital flexion contractures**

#### *Severe Exfoliative Erythroderma in Children:*

- Metabolic stress
- Growth failure
- Growth retardation
- Malnutrition

#### Adults:

- Risk of **basal cell carcinoma** and **squamous cell carcinoma**

### Investigations

#### 1. Histopathological Examination:

- **Orthokeratotic, basket-weave hyperkeratosis**
- **Moderate to severe acanthosis**
- **Prominent granular layer**
- **Papillomatous** epidermis — **severe papillomatous with suprapapillary thinning** (described as **"church-spire" papillomatosis**)
- **Dilated, elongated capillaries** with **minimal perivascular inflammation** in the papillary dermis

> **High-Yield Point**: The histologic pattern of "church-spire" papillomatosis refers to tall, narrow, spire-like epidermal projections — a relatively distinctive finding in EKV that helps distinguish it from other ichthyoses.

#### 2. Molecular Testing:

- Sequencing of **GJB3**, **GJB4**, and **GJA1** — definitive confirmation

### Differential Diagnosis

- **Extensive dermatophytosis** (tinea corporis)
- **MEDNIK syndrome** (Mental retardation, Enteropathy, Deafness, Neuropathy, Ichthyosis, Keratoderma — *AP1S1* mutations)
- **Ichthyosis linearis circumflexa** (associated with Netherton syndrome — *SPINK5* mutations)

### Treatment

- **Symptomatic and depends on severity and extent**

#### *Topical:*

- **α-Hydroxy acids (α-OH)** or **salicylic acid** — keratolytics
- **Urea** — humectant/keratolytic
- **Retinoids — tretinoin, tazarotene** — promote desquamation and normalize keratinization
- **Camouflage** — in case of erythema (cosmetic management)
- **Avoidance of triggers** — emotional stress, temperature changes, friction, UV exposure

#### *Systemic:*

- **Oral retinoids — Acitretin** — for extensive or severe disease

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **"Mendes da Costa syndrome" (p. 178, Q21 answer)** → **Verification:** The source correctly attributes EKV to *Mendes da Costa*, the Dutch dermatologist who first described the condition in 1925 in a large Dutch family showing autosomal dominant inheritance of migratory erythema and fixed hyperkeratotic plaques. The full eponym is *Mendes da Costa syndrome* or *erythrokeratodermia variabilis of Mendes da Costa*. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **GJB3, GJB4, GJA1 gene attribution (p. 178, pathogenesis)** → **Verification:** The source correctly lists the three gap junction genes. **GJB3** (Connexin 31) and **GJB4** (Connexin 30.3) were the classically identified genes for EKV. **GJA1** (Connexin 43) mutations have been more recently recognized as a cause of erythrokeratodermia variabilis et progressiva (EKVP). Current nomenclature from the 2016 First Ichthyosis Consensus Conference update merges the older "EKV" and "progressive symmetric erythrokeratoderma (PSEK)" into the single entity **erythrokeratodermia variabilis et progressiva (EKVP)**, reflecting the overlapping clinical and genetic spectrum. The source's use of the traditional "erythrokeratoderma variabilis" name is acceptable but students should be aware of the updated nomenclature. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"50% present at birth, 90% in first year" (p. 178, clinical features)** → **Verification:** The source's epidemiologic onset data is consistent with major references. The majority of EKV cases manifest in infancy, with about half presenting at or soon after birth. This early onset helps distinguish EKV from acquired erythrokeratodermas. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Church-spire papillomatosis" (p. 179, histopathology) — split across column break** → **Verification:** The term "church-spire papillomatosis" is correctly used. It refers to the tall, narrow papillomatous epidermal projections resembling church spires, with suprapapillary thinning. This is a histologic pattern seen in EKV and is also described in acrokeratosis verruciformis of Hopf and some epidermal nevi. The source material splits this description across the column break between pages 178–179 ("Severe papillomatous with suprapapillary thinning— / church spire"), but the meaning is intact. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Orthokeratotic, basket-weave hyperkeratosis" (p. 178, investigations HPE)** → **Verification:** The source correctly describes the pattern as orthokeratotic with basket-weave configuration. This contrasts with the compact orthokeratosis seen in lamellar ichthyosis (Q20) and the parakeratosis seen in CIE (Q22). The retained basket-weave pattern with acanthosis and papillomatosis is relatively characteristic of EKV. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **Risk of BCC and SCC in adults (p. 179)** → **Verification:** The source mentions the risk of basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma in adult EKV patients. While this is noted in some references for severe erythrokeratodermas, the malignancy risk in EKV specifically is considered **low** and not a prominent feature of the disease. The connection is more strongly established for other ichthyoses (e.g., Netherton syndrome, recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa). This statement should be interpreted cautiously — while cutaneous malignancy can develop in areas of chronic keratoderma/scaling in any ichthyosis, it is not a defining feature of EKV. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; Andrews')

- **"MEDNIK syndrome" in differential diagnosis (p. 179)** → **Verification:** The source correctly lists MEDNIK syndrome as a differential. MEDNIK (Mental retardation, Enteropathy, Deafness, Neuropathy, Ichthyosis, Keratoderma) is caused by mutations in **AP1S1** (adaptor-related protein complex 1, sigma-1 subunit). It can present with erythrokeratoderma-like skin changes and is an important syndromic differential for EKV. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Ichthyosis linearis circumflexa" in differential diagnosis (p. 179)** → **Verification:** Ichthyosis linearis circumflexa (ILC) is correctly listed as a differential. ILC is characterized by migratory, polycyclic erythematous plaques with a distinctive **double-edged scale** border, and is the hallmark cutaneous finding of **Netherton syndrome** (*SPINK5* mutations). The migratory erythematous plaques of ILC can resemble the transient erythema of EKV, making clinical distinction important. However, ILC has the characteristic double-edged scale, trichorrhexis invaginata (bamboo hair), and elevated IgE — features absent in EKV. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Erythrokeratodermia variabilis et progressiva" (p. 178, synonyms)** → **Clarification:** The source uses both the traditional name ("erythrokeratoderma variabilis") and the updated name ("erythrokeratodermia variabilis et progressiva"). In the current genetic classification, EKV (Mendes da Costa) and PSEK (progressive symmetric erythrokeratoderma, also known as Gottron syndrome) have been unified under the single entity **erythrokeratodermia variabilis et progressiva (EKVP)**, as both are caused by connexin gene mutations and represent a phenotypic spectrum. Students should be aware that older questions may refer to EKV and PSEK as separate entities, but genetically they overlap. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)
