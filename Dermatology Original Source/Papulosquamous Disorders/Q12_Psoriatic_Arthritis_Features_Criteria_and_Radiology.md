# Q12. Discuss psoriatic arthritis — clinical features, criteria, and X-ray findings.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 168–170 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Introduction

- Also known as **psoriatic arthropathy**
- A **seronegative inflammatory arthritis** affecting up to **40%** of patients with moderate to severe psoriasis
- Can lead to significant **impairment in quality of life**

---

### Epidemiology

- **Age of onset** = ~40 years
- Majority develop **psoriasis before joint disease** (skin precedes joints in ~70% of cases)
- Equal sex ratio overall, though axial disease is more common in males

---

### Pathophysiology

- **Immune activation** in the **synovium and enthesis**
- Driven by **innate and adaptive immune responses**
- Key cytokines: **TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23**
- **Angiogenesis** in synovium occurs
- **Genetic factors:** **HLA-B27** and **HLA-C*06:02** influence disease presentation
- **Environmental triggers** — trauma, smoking, and alcohol contribute to onset and progression

---

### Clinical Features

Clinically heterogeneous and classified according to **Moll and Wright criteria**, which defines **five subtypes:**

| Subtype | Features |
|---|---|
| **1. Asymmetric oligoarthritis** | Most common; affecting **<5 joints** |
| **2. Symmetric polyarthritis** | Similar to RA but **RF negative** |
| **3. Distal interphalangeal (DIP) arthritis** | Of fingers and toes; classic for PsA |
| **4. Arthritis mutilans** | Rare, severe, and **deforming** form of PsA |
| **5. Axial disease** | Affects **spine and sacroiliac joints** |

---

### Symptoms

- **Enthesitis** — inflammation where tendons or ligaments attach to bone, commonly seen in **Achilles tendon** and **plantar fascia**
- **Dactylitis** — swelling of entire fingers or toes, often referred to as **"sausage digits"**
- **Early morning stiffness** in joints
- **Joint swelling**

---

### Differential Diagnoses

- Early RA
- Ankylosing spondylitis
- Gout
- Osteoarthritis
- Fibromyalgia

---

### Investigations (Q.4)

- Routine psoriasis investigations
- Lab investigations — **CRP, ESR, uric acid, RF, CCP** levels
- **Plain X-rays**
- **USG or MRI** for enthesitis

---

### X-ray Findings in PsA

#### Peripheral Joints:

1. **Dactylitis** — soft-tissue swelling of entire digit
2. **Deformity of hands and fingers**
3. **Destructive changes** — subchondral cysts and erosions with distinctive **spike-like or fluffy proliferative alterations** on joint surface margins and **phalangeal tuft**
4. **Osteolytic lesions** of the phalanx which constitutes the **pencil-in-cup deformity**
5. **Bony ankylosis**
6. **Periostitis** along metaphyses and shafts of digits
7. **Periosteal and endosteal bone formation** — may increase bone density — **ivory phalanx**
8. **Juxtaarticular and gross osteoporosis**

#### Spine:

1. PSA syndesmophytes — **asymmetric, linear/curvilinear, fluffy**
2. **Squaring of vertebral bodies**
3. Cervical-intervertebral joint erosions, syndesmophytes, erosions, **ligamentous calcification**, rarely **atlantoaxial subluxation**

#### Sacroiliac Joints:

1. Ill-defined cortical bone layer
2. Shallow erosions
3. Subchondral osteosclerosis
4. Bony proliferations

#### Enthesis:

*Enthesopathy of Achilles tendon, plantar aponeurosis, pelvic attachments:*

1. Mineralized scars
2. Bony proliferations
3. Erosions
4. Cysts

---

### Diagnosis — CASPAR Criteria

***C**lassification of **P**soriatic **A**rthritis **S**tudy Group* (*CASPAR*)

**Prerequisite**: Established inflammatory articular disease (joint, spine, or entheseal)

**If ≥3 points are present, PsA is confirmed:**

| Criterion | Points |
|---|---|
| Evidence of **current skin psoriasis** | **(2)** |
| **Personal/family history** of psoriasis | (1) |
| Typical **psoriatic nail dystrophy** | (1) |
| **RA factor** by ELISA — **negative** | (1) |
| **Dactylitis** | (1) |
| Radiological evidence of **juxta-articular new bone formation** excluding osteophytes | (1) |

> **Note:** Current skin psoriasis scores **2 points** (double weight); all other criteria score 1 point each. A total of ≥3 points (in the presence of established inflammatory articular disease) confirms PsA. CASPAR has ~91% sensitivity and ~99% specificity.

---

### Treatment — EULAR/GRAPPA Guidelines

#### Phase 1:
- Start with **NSAIDs ± local glucocorticoid injections**

#### Phase 2:
- **Disease-modifying therapies (csDMARDs)** — **Methotrexate (MTX)**, Leflunomide, Sulfasalazine

#### Phase 3:
- **Biologics** — TNF-alpha inhibitors, IL-12/23 inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors
- **Targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs)** — PDE4 inhibitors (apremilast), JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib)

#### Phase 4:
- **Switch biologics/DMARD** × 3–6 months (if inadequate response, switch to alternate mechanism of action)

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **"psoriatic arthropathy is a seronegative inflammatory arthritis affecting up to 40% of patients"** — **Verified.** The prevalence of PsA among psoriasis patients is commonly quoted as 6–42%, with recent meta-analyses suggesting ~30%. The figure of "up to 40%" in the source is within the accepted upper range and is consistent with Rook's and Bolognia, which cite prevalence rates of 20–30% (population-based) to up to 40% (dermatology clinic-based cohorts). (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; Alinaghi F et al., *JAAD* 2019)

- **"Investigations: (Q.4)"** → **Clarification:** The source text writes "Investigations: (Q.4)" — this is a cross-reference to Q4 (Management of Psoriasis) where routine psoriasis workup investigations were detailed. It is not a separate question numbering error. Students should refer to Q4 for the complete baseline investigation panel.

- **PDE4 inhibitors and JAK inhibitors listed under "biologics" in the source (Phase 3)** → **Correction:** Apremilast (PDE4 inhibitor) and tofacitinib/upadacitinib (JAK inhibitors) are **targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs)**, not biologics. Biologics are large-molecule proteins produced by living cells (e.g., monoclonal antibodies like infliximab, secukinumab, ustekinumab). tsDMARDs are small-molecule oral drugs that target specific intracellular signaling pathways. The source conflates these two categories under "Biologics" in the Phase 3 section. The corrected classification is: **bDMARDs** (TNF-α inhibitors, IL-12/23 inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors) and **tsDMARDs** (PDE4 inhibitors, JAK inhibitors). (Ref: EULAR 2023 recommendations; GRAPPA 2021 guidelines)

- **CASPAR prerequisite** — **Verification and clarification:** The source lists the CASPAR scoring criteria but does not explicitly state the mandatory prerequisite — that the patient must have **established inflammatory articular disease** (joint, spine, or entheseal) before the scoring system is applied. Without this prerequisite, the criteria cannot be used. The CASPAR criteria have 91.4% sensitivity and 98.7% specificity for PsA. (Ref: Taylor W et al., *Arthritis Rheum* 2006; Bolognia)

- **"RA factor by ELISA-ve (1)"** → **Clarification:** The CASPAR criterion awards 1 point for a **negative RF** (by any method, not only ELISA). A negative RF helps distinguish PsA from RA (where RF is positive in ~80%). The source abbreviation "-ve" means "negative." (Ref: CASPAR original publication)

- **"Pencil-in-cup deformity"** — **Verified.** This is a pathognomonic X-ray finding of PsA, particularly in arthritis mutilans. It results from severe osteolysis of the proximal phalanx (whittled to a "pencil" point) articulating with a widened, eroded base of the adjacent bone ("cup"). It reflects the destructive-proliferative nature of PsA. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Ivory phalanx"** — **Verified.** Ivory phalanx refers to diffuse sclerosis of an entire phalanx on X-ray, caused by periosteal and endosteal new bone formation. It is a characteristic but uncommon radiographic sign of PsA. (Ref: Rook's)

- **"Fluffy syndesmophytes"** — **Verified.** PsA produces asymmetric, non-marginal, bulky/fluffy syndesmophytes, in contrast to the thin, marginal, symmetric syndesmophytes of ankylosing spondylitis. This is a key radiographic distinction tested in examinations. (Ref: Bolognia; Gladman DD, *Ann Rheum Dis* 2005)

- **"Atlantoaxial subluxation"** — **Verified.** Atlantoaxial subluxation is a rare but serious complication of cervical spine involvement in PsA. It can lead to cord compression and is an indication for surgical stabilization. More commonly seen in severe RA, but also occurs in PsA. (Ref: Rook's)

- **Enthesitis definition** — **Verified.** The source correctly identifies enthesitis as inflammation at the tendon/ligament-bone insertion points. Achilles tendon and plantar fascia are the most commonly affected sites. Enthesitis is a hallmark of the seronegative spondyloarthropathies (PsA, ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis) and helps distinguish PsA from RA (where enthesitis is uncommon). (Ref: Bolognia; McGonagle D, *J Rheumatol* 2001)

- **"Moll and Wright criteria"** — **Verification:** The Moll and Wright classification (1973) established the five clinical patterns of PsA. While still widely taught and used for clinical description, the CASPAR criteria (2006) have largely replaced Moll and Wright for formal diagnostic/classification purposes. Both are included in the source, which is appropriate for examination preparation. (Ref: Moll JM and Wright V, *Ann Rheum Dis* 1973)

- **EULAR Phase 3 agents** — The source lists "IL-12/23 inhibitors" and "IL-17 inhibitors" — **Verified.** Ustekinumab (IL-12/23), guselkumab/risankizumab (IL-23p19), secukinumab/ixekizumab (IL-17A), and bimekizumab (IL-17A/F) are all approved or in late-stage trials for PsA. (Ref: EULAR 2023; GRAPPA 2021)

## Integrated Margin Notes

- **Page 168 (bottom right, Q12 introduction):** The question begins at the bottom right of page 168 with the question text "Q12. Discuss psoriatic arthritis — clinical features, criteria, and X-ray findings." The introduction defines PsA as a seronegative inflammatory arthropathy affecting up to 40% of moderate-severe psoriasis patients. The key framing for examinations is that PsA belongs to the **seronegative spondyloarthropathy** group (along with ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, and enteropathic arthritis). The term "seronegative" refers to the absence of rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-CCP antibodies — this immediately distinguishes PsA from RA.

- **Page 169 (top left, skewed text — epidemiology/pathophysiology):** The text at the top left of page 169 is notably skewed/curved due to scan distortion. The key data points extracted: (1) Age of onset ~40 years; (2) majority develop psoriasis before joint disease — this temporal relationship (skin preceding joints by a median of ~10 years) is a clinically important point, as it means dermatologists are often the first to suspect PsA; (3) pathophysiology driven by immune activation in synovium/enthesis with TNF-α/IL-17/IL-23 as key cytokines — the same cytokines driving cutaneous psoriasis, explaining why biologic therapy can treat both skin and joint disease simultaneously; (4) HLA-B27 is associated with axial PsA and HLA-C*06:02 with cutaneous psoriasis/peripheral PsA.

- **Page 169 (left column, Moll and Wright subtypes):** The five subtypes are essential examination content. **Asymmetric oligoarthritis** is the most common pattern (~70% at presentation). **DIP involvement** is the most characteristic for PsA (DIP arthritis is uncommon in RA). **Arthritis mutilans** is the rarest but most severe — telescoping fingers from extensive osteolysis. **Symmetric polyarthritis** can closely mimic RA — distinguished by negative RF/CCP. Over time, many patients evolve from one pattern to another.

- **Page 169 (left column, symptoms — enthesitis/dactylitis):** Enthesitis and dactylitis are the two hallmark features that distinguish PsA from RA. **Dactylitis** ("sausage digit") involves inflammation of the entire digit (including tendons, joints, and soft tissues), not just the joint — this distinguishes it from joint-limited swelling in RA. Dactylitis occurs in ~40–50% of PsA patients and is uncommon in RA. **Enthesitis** is virtually pathognomonic of spondyloarthropathies.

- **Page 169 (left column, DDs):** The differential diagnosis list is concise and clinically practical. Key distinctions: (1) **vs. RA** — symmetric, RF+, CCP+, no enthesitis, no DIP; (2) **vs. AS** — predominantly axial, HLA-B27+, bilateral sacroiliitis; (3) **vs. gout** — monoarticular, tophi, urate crystals; (4) **vs. OA** — Heberden/Bouchard nodes, no inflammation, no enthesitis; (5) **vs. fibromyalgia** — no joint erosions, no inflammatory markers.

- **Page 169 (left bottom, investigations and X-ray findings):** The "Investigations: (Q.4)" notation cross-references back to Q4 where the routine psoriasis workup is detailed. The X-ray findings section is comprehensive and high-yield for examinations. The **pencil-in-cup deformity** is the pathognomonic radiographic sign — students must be able to describe it (osteolysis of proximal bone → pencil; erosion widening the cup of the adjacent bone). **Ivory phalanx** (diffuse sclerosis from periosteal + endosteal new bone) is another characteristic but lesser-known sign.

- **Page 169 (right column, spine and sacroiliac findings):** The distinguishing radiographic features of PsA spine disease vs. AS are critical: PsA produces **asymmetric**, bulky, **non-marginal syndesmophytes** ("fluffy"), whereas AS produces thin, symmetric, marginal syndesmophytes. Sacroiliac joint involvement in PsA may be **unilateral** (vs. bilateral in AS). These distinctions are frequently tested.

- **Page 169 (right column, CASPAR criteria):** CASPAR is the gold-standard classification system for PsA. The key teaching point is the **prerequisite** — established inflammatory articular disease must be present first. Then, ≥3 points from the six criteria confirm PsA. Current skin psoriasis uniquely scores **2 points**, reflecting its diagnostic weight. A negative RF is scored because it distinguishes PsA from RA. Radiographic new bone formation (excluding osteophytes) captures the proliferative component unique to PsA.

- **Page 169–170 (right column → top left p. 170, EULAR/GRAPPA treatment ladder):** The stepwise treatment approach is important for exam structuring: (1) NSAIDs first (unlike RA where early DMARD is recommended); (2) csDMARDs (MTX, leflunomide, sulfasalazine) — notably, **MTX works on skin and peripheral joints** but has limited evidence for axial disease; (3) biologics (TNF-α inhibitors, IL-17, IL-12/23, IL-23) and tsDMARDs (apremilast, JAKi) — the source incorrectly groups tsDMARDs under "biologics"; (4) switch mechanism if inadequate response. The GRAPPA guidelines additionally stratify treatment by **domain** (peripheral arthritis, axial, enthesitis, dactylitis, skin, nails) — each domain may require different optimal therapy.

- **Page 170 (top, Phases 3–4 and transition to Q13):** Phase 4 (switch biologics/DMARD × 3–6 months) reflects the clinical reality that not all PsA patients respond to first-line biologics. The 3–6 month reassessment window is standard. After this, Q13 begins (RA vs. PsA differentiation), providing a natural comparative framework that reinforces the Q12 content.
