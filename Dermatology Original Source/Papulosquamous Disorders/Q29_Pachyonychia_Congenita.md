# Q29. Describe the types and clinical features of pachyonychia congenita.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 185 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Definition

- **Pachyonychia congenita (PC)** is a **heterogeneous group of diseases** characterized by a triad of:
  1. **Painful focal keratoderma** (palmoplantar)
  2. **Nail dystrophy** (pachyonychia — thickened nails)
  3. **Oral leukokeratosis**
- Inheritance: **Autosomal dominant**

> **High-Yield Point — Defining Feature**: Despite the name emphasizing nails ("pachyonychia"), the **most debilitating symptom** is actually the **painful plantar keratoderma**, which can significantly impair ambulation and quality of life. The pain is characteristically out of proportion to the clinical appearance. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

---

### Historical Classification (Textbook-Based — Types Inverted in Source)

| Feature | Type I: *Jadassohn–Lewandowsky* | Type II: *Jackson–Lawlor* |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary keratins** | KRT6A, KRT16 | KRT6B, KRT17 |
| **Nail involvement** | Severe — all nails | Less severe — fewer nails |
| **Plantar keratoderma** | Present | Present (often painful) |
| **Oral leukokeratosis** | Prominent | Less prominent |
| **Natal teeth** | Absent | **Present** |
| **Cysts** | Follicular keratoses | **Steatocystomas, epidermal cysts, vellus hair cysts** |
| **Hair** | Generally normal | May have pili torti |

> ⚠️ **CRITICAL SOURCE ERROR — Types I and II Are Inverted in the Textbook**: The source page 185 labels **"Type I: Jackson-Lawlor"** and **"Type II: Jadassohn-Lewandowsky."** This is **reversed** from the established literature. The standard historical classification is:
> - **PC Type I (PC-1)** = ***Jadassohn–Lewandowsky*** type (KRT6A/KRT16)
> - **PC Type II (PC-2)** = ***Jackson–Lawlor*** type (KRT6B/KRT17)
>
> This has been corrected in the table above. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; IPCRR — International Pachyonychia Congenita Research Registry)

> **Modern Note — Revised Classification**: The historical PC-1/PC-2 nomenclature has been **abandoned** in favour of a **molecular classification** based on the specific keratin gene mutated. This avoids confusion from the type I/II mix-ups (as seen in this source) and better reflects genotype–phenotype correlations. The current system uses the format **"PC-K[gene]"** (e.g., PC-K6a, PC-K16, PC-K17). (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; McLean et al., *Br J Dermatol*, 2011)

---

### Modern Molecular Classification

PC is classified by the specific **keratin gene** involved:

| Molecular Subtype | Mutated Gene | Keratin Protein | Key Distinguishing Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PC-K6a** | *KRT6A* | Keratin 6a | Severe nail dystrophy; oral leukokeratosis; painful PPK |
| **PC-K6b** | *KRT6B* | Keratin 6b | Similar to K6a but less severe nail disease |
| **PC-K6c** | *KRT6C* | Keratin 6c | Milder phenotype; focal PPK |
| **PC-K16** | *KRT16* | Keratin 16 | Nail dystrophy + painful plantar keratoderma |
| **PC-K17** | *KRT17* | Keratin 17 | **Natal teeth**; steatocystomas; pilosebaceous cysts |

---

### Pathophysiology

- **Keratins 6 and 16** are expressed in:
  - **Nail bed** around nail progenitor cells
  - **Suprabasal layers** of palmoplantar skin
  - **Oral mucosal epithelium**
  - **Hair follicles**
- **Keratin 17** is expressed in the **pilosebaceous unit** → explains the association with **steatocystomas**, **epidermal cysts**, **vellus hair cysts**, and **natal teeth** in PC-K17

- **Pathogenetic mechanism**:

```
Mutation in KRT6A / KRT6B / KRT6C / KRT16 / KRT17
                    ↓
  Defective keratin intermediate filament assembly
                    ↓
  Traumatic damage to keratin → compromised cytoskeleton
                    ↓
  Fragile epithelial cells in nail bed, palms/soles, oral mucosa
                    ↓
  Compensatory hyperkeratosis → painful thickening (PPK)
  Abnormal nail plate formation → pachyonychia (thick nails)
```

### Clinical Features

#### 1. Painful Focal Palmoplantar Keratoderma (PPK)
- **Most disabling feature** — the defining symptom.
- Focal PPK usually presents in the **first years of life** when the child starts **weight-bearing and walking**.
- **Blisters develop beneath the keratoderma** resulting in **intense pain**.
- Pain usually becomes a significant problem **after 10 years of age**.
- Blisters and plantar pain usually **worsen in the summer** (heat, sweating).
- **Hyperhidrosis with fetid odor** accompanies the keratoderma.

#### 2. Nail Dystrophy (Pachyonychia)
- **Hypertrophic nail dystrophy** is predominant, with two phenotypes:
  - **Nails that grow to full length and slant upwards** — caused by prominent **distal subungual hyperkeratosis (SUHK)**.
  - **Nails with a nail plate that terminates prematurely**, leaving a **gently sloping distal region of hyperkeratosis**.

#### 3. Oral and Mucosal Involvement
- **Oral leukokeratosis**: Tongue and buccal mucosa affected.
- Babies have **difficulty in sucking, hoarseness of voice**.
- **RDS** (respiratory distress syndrome) — in severe neonatal cases.

#### 4. Follicular Hyperkeratosis
- Occurs in areas of **friction** — **elbows, knees, buttocks, and trunk**.

#### 5. Cystic Lesions (especially PC-K17)
- **Steatocystomas**
- **Epidermal cysts**
- **Milia**
- **Vellus hair cysts** — increase at **puberty**

#### 6. Natal/Neonatal Teeth
- Associated with **KRT17** (and also **KRT6A**) mutations.

#### 7. Additional Features (PC-K6A)
- **Ear pain and difficulty feeding** — reported in PC-KRT6A.

#### 8. Less Severe Variant (PC-K16)
- **Painful focal keratoderma** (present but less extensive)
- **Dystrophy of a few nails** (rather than all 20)
- **Splinter hemorrhages of fingernails**

#### 9. Paronychia
- Paronychia (periungual inflammation) is an associated finding.

---

### Differential Diagnosis (D/D)

- **Howel–Evans syndrome** (focal PPK + esophageal SCC; *RHBDF2* — no nail dystrophy)
- **Clouston syndrome** (hidrotic ectodermal dysplasia — nail dystrophy, alopecia, PPK; *GJB6*)

---

### Laboratory / Histopathological Findings

| Finding | Description |
|---|---|
| **Gross hyperkeratosis** | Massive thickening of stratum corneum |
| **Acanthosis** | Epidermal hyperplasia |
| **Patchy hypergranulosis** | Irregular thickening of the granular layer |
| **No epidermolysis** | Distinguishes PC from epidermolysis bullosa simplex (EBS) |

> **Histopathology Pearl**: The absence of **epidermolysis** (intraepidermal blister formation from cytolysis) on HPE is a key distinguishing feature from **epidermolysis bullosa simplex (EBS)**, which shares keratin 5/14 mutations and can also present with painful plantar blistering. In PC, the pain results from **subkeratotic blistering** (below the thickened stratum corneum) rather than from intraepidermal cytolysis. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

---

### Treatment

| Modality | Details |
|---|---|
| **Paring or filing of callouses** | Mechanical debridement — primary palliative measure |
| **Comfortable footwear** | **Customized insoles** to redistribute pressure |
| **Local keratolytics** | **Urea** (10–40%) — to soften thickened stratum corneum |
| **Botox for hyperhidrosis** | Botulinum toxin injection into plantar skin for hyperhidrosis |
| **Oral rapamycin (sirolimus)** | mTOR inhibitor — known to **reduce keratin expression** |
| **SiRNA therapy** | Small interfering RNA — **mutation-specific gene silencing** targeting the mutant keratin allele |

> **Emerging Therapy — siRNA Gene Silencing**: PC is a leading candidate for **mutation-specific siRNA therapy** in dermatology. Because PC is autosomal dominant (gain-of-function mutations), selectively silencing the **mutant allele** while preserving the normal allele can correct the phenotype. Phase Ib clinical trials (e.g., TD101 targeting KRT6A N171K) have demonstrated proof-of-concept with regression of plantar calluses at the injection site. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; Leachman et al., *Mol Ther*, 2010)

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **Types I and II INVERTED (p. 185)** → ⚠️ **MAJOR ERROR.** The source states **"Type I: Jackson-Lawlor"** and **"Type II: Jadassohn-Lewandowsky."** This is **reversed**. The correct historical classification is:
  - **PC Type I (PC-1)** = **Jadassohn–Lewandowsky** type (KRT6A/KRT16) — described first (1906)
  - **PC Type II (PC-2)** = **Jackson–Lawlor** type (KRT6B/KRT17) — described later (1951)
  - The source has swapped these eponyms. This is a **high-impact factual error** that could lead to incorrect exam answers. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; Smith et al., *J Invest Dermatol*, 2005)

- **"Jadahsson-Lewandowsky" (p. 185)** → **"Jadassohn–Lewandowsky"** — The source misspells the eponym as "Jadahsson" (with an 'h'). The correct spelling is **Jadassohn**, after the dermatologist **Josef Jadassohn** (1863–1936). Additionally, a hyphen is used where an **en-dash** is standard between two different surnames. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"PC-KRTI7" (p. 185)** → **"PC-KRT17"** — OCR/typographical error. The source renders the keratin 17 subtype as "PC-KRTI7" (with a capital 'I' instead of the digit '1'). The correct designation is **PC-KRT17** (keratin 17, encoded by the *KRT17* gene on chromosome 17q21.2). (Ref: Standard keratin nomenclature; Rook's)

- **"KRTIT" (p. 185)** → **"KRT17"** — In the line about natal teeth ("Natal teeth [KRTIT and KRT6A]"), the source garbles "KRT17" as "KRTIT." The correct gene is ***KRT17***. Natal teeth in PC are most strongly associated with *KRT17* mutations (PC-K17 / historical PC-2 / Jackson–Lawlor type). (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"PC-KRTI6" in the molecular classification list (p. 185)** → **"PC-KRT16"** — The source renders the KRT16 subtype as "PC-KRTI6" (capital 'I' instead of '1'). The correct designation is **PC-KRT16** (keratin 16, encoded by *KRT16* gene on chromosome 17q21.2). The pattern of 'I' for '1' substitution is consistent across multiple keratin entries on this page, suggesting a systematic font/OCR rendering error. (Ref: Standard keratin nomenclature)

- **"distal SUHK" (p. 185)** → This abbreviation is not standard in most textbooks. It likely refers to **distal subungual hyperkeratosis**. The standard abbreviation would be **DSUHK** or simply "subungual hyperkeratosis." The source uses "SUHK" without defining it. (Ref: Clinical context)

- **"RDS" (p. 185)** → **Verification**: The source mentions "RDS" in the context of oral leukokeratosis causing difficulty sucking and hoarseness. In this context, RDS likely refers to **respiratory distress syndrome** secondary to laryngeal involvement/leukokeratosis in severe neonatal PC, rather than the neonatal surfactant-deficiency RDS of premature infants. Laryngeal leukokeratosis in PC-K6a can cause neonatal stridor and respiratory compromise. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"SiRNA" (p. 185)** → **"siRNA"** — The source capitalizes "SiRNA." The standard nomenclature is **siRNA** (small interfering RNA) — lowercase 's' and 'i', uppercase 'RNA'. (Ref: Standard molecular biology nomenclature)

- **Oral rapamycin** → **Verification**: The source states "Oral rapamycin and SiRNA is known to reduce keratin expression." Rapamycin (sirolimus) is an **mTOR inhibitor** that has shown **reduction of plantar calluses** in PC in small case series and anecdotal reports. However, systemic rapamycin carries significant immunosuppressive side effects. Topical sirolimus (rapamycin) formulations have been investigated with more favorable risk profiles. (Ref: Rook's; Leachman et al.)
