# Q13. Differentiate between rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and psoriatic arthritis (PsA).

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 170–171 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Introduction

- **Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)** is an autoimmune systemic inflammatory disease characterized by **synovitis, bony erosions, and cartilage damage**
- **Psoriatic arthritis (PsA)** is a heterogeneous autoimmune systemic disease with **diverse clinical and radiographic manifestations**
- Both RA and PsA are common **chronic inflammatory diseases** characterized by **pain and swelling** in joints

---

### Epidemiology

- **RA** is more common than PsA overall
- **RA** — most common onset at **40–60 years**; PsA — most common in **30–50 years** age group
- **Sex ratio:** PsA — **M > W**; RA — **W > M** (female predominance)
- PsA is most common in **Caucasians**, whereas RA affects **all ethnic groups**

---

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **"Proinflammatory cytokines-TNF-2 PL-6, IL-1, UL-28, 12-3 CXCL-B drive joint damage PSA" (p. 170, RA immunopathology row)** → **Correction:** This is a heavily garbled OCR/scan artifact. The correct reading is: **"Proinflammatory cytokines — TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1, IL-18, IL-23, CXCL-8 drive joint damage in RA."** "TNF-2" → TNF-α; "PL-6" → IL-6; "UL-28" → IL-18; "12-3" → IL-23; "CXCL-B" → CXCL-8 (also known as IL-8, a CXC chemokine); "PSA" should read "RA" (the column is describing RA, not PsA). (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia; McInnes IB, *N Engl J Med* 2011)

- **"Ustekinumab (11-12/23 inhibitors)" (p. 171, Treatment row)** → **Correction:** "11-12/23" is a scan/OCR artifact — the correct notation is **"IL-12/23 inhibitors."** Ustekinumab is a monoclonal antibody targeting the **p40 subunit** shared by **IL-12** and **IL-23**. It is FDA-approved for PsA and has demonstrated efficacy in skin and peripheral joint domains but limited data for axial disease. Its lack of efficacy in RA despite targeting IL-12/IL-23 highlights the different immunopathogenic pathways (RA is more IL-6/TNF-α-driven). (Ref: Bolognia; Ritchlin CT et al., *N Engl J Med* 2014)

- **"Proinflammatory cytokines - IL-17, 23, 22, 1beta, 6 TNF-alpha and IFN gamma" (p. 170, PsA immunopathology row)** → **Clarification:** The source lists these as a comma-separated string without full interleukin prefixes. The correct expanded reading is: **IL-17, IL-23, IL-22, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ.** These cytokines define the **Th17/Th1 inflammatory axis** that is central to PsA pathogenesis, with IL-17 and IL-23 being the dominant drivers (hence the efficacy of secukinumab, ixekizumab, and guselkumab in PsA). (Ref: Rook's; Veale DJ, *Lancet* 2018)

- **"HLA-DR 1 associated with positive RF and CCP antibodies" (p. 170, RA Genetic factors row)** → **Verification and clarification:** The major HLA association for RA is **HLA-DRB1** (specifically the **shared epitope** alleles within HLA-DRB1, including HLA-DR1 and HLA-DR4 subtypes). The source separates HLA-DR1 and HLA-DR4 into two rows — this is acceptable for examination purposes. HLA-DR1 is associated with RF and anti-CCP seropositivity; HLA-DR4 is linked with increased severity and extra-articular manifestations. (Ref: Rook's; Gregersen PK et al., *Arthritis Rheum* 1987)

- **"HLA-B08 is associated with dactylitis and symmetric sacroiliitis" (p. 170, PsA Genetic factors row)** → **Verification:** HLA-B08 (also written HLA-B8) is associated with dactylitis-predominant PsA. However, the more commonly cited HLA associations for PsA in standard references are: **HLA-B27** (axial disease/sacroiliitis), **HLA-Cw6/C*06:02** (skin psoriasis and peripheral arthritis), **HLA-B38** and **HLA-B39** (polyarticular PsA). HLA-B08 is a less frequently cited association but is documented in the literature. (Ref: Bolognia; Gladman DD, *Ann Rheum Dis* 2009)

- **"Nail—SUHK, pitting, dystrophy, onycholysis" (p. 170, PsA clinical features row)** → **Clarification:** "SUHK" is an abbreviation for **subungual hyperkeratosis**. The full spectrum of nail changes in PsA includes: pitting, onycholysis, subungual hyperkeratosis, oil-drop sign (salmon patch), splinter hemorrhages, and nail crumbling/dystrophy. Nail disease is present in **80–90%** of PsA patients (vs. ~50% of psoriasis patients without arthritis) and is one of the CASPAR criteria. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"Cervical spine involvement in 80%" (p. 170, RA Axial involvement row)** → **Verification:** Cervical spine involvement is reported in up to **80%** of patients with longstanding RA (>10 years), although clinically significant cervical spine disease is less common. The most important cervical complication is **atlantoaxial subluxation** (present in ~25% of RA patients radiographically), which can lead to cord compression. This is a key differentiator from PsA, where any spinal segment (cervical, thoracic, lumbar) may be involved. (Ref: Rook's; Firestein GS, *Kelley and Firestein's Textbook of Rheumatology*)

- **"Synovitis in 60% (PsA) vs 90% (RA)" — radiological row (p. 171)** → **Verification:** These figures refer to the prevalence of synovitis detectable on imaging (ultrasound/MRI). RA characteristically shows prominent synovitis as the primary pathological process (pannus formation), whereas PsA has enthesitis as its hallmark pathology, with synovitis being secondary. The lower synovitis prevalence in PsA compared to RA is consistent with this mechanistic distinction. (Ref: Rook's; McGonagle D, *Arthritis Rheum* 1999)

- **"IL-6 inhibitors have limited efficacy (PsA)" (p. 171, Treatment row)** → **Verified.** Tocilizumab (anti-IL-6R) and sarilumab have not demonstrated significant efficacy in PsA, despite being highly effective in RA. This reflects the differential role of IL-6 in the two diseases: IL-6 is a major driver of RA synovitis and systemic inflammation, but plays a less central role in PsA where the IL-17/IL-23 axis predominates. (Ref: Mease PJ et al., *Ann Rheum Dis* 2017)

- **"Rituximab — Ineffective (PsA) vs Effective (RA)" (p. 171, Treatment row)** → **Verified.** Rituximab (anti-CD20, B-cell depleting) is effective in RA (particularly RF+ and anti-CCP+ disease) because B cells and autoantibodies are central to RA pathogenesis. In PsA, disease is primarily driven by T cells (CD8+ T cells, Th17), not B cells — hence rituximab has shown poor efficacy and even paradoxical worsening of skin psoriasis in some cases. (Ref: Rook's; Mease PJ, *J Rheumatol* 2010)

- **"Anakinra — limited efficacy in spondyloarthropathy (PsA)" (p. 171)** → **Verification:** Anakinra is an IL-1 receptor antagonist. While effective in RA, it has shown limited efficacy in PsA and other spondyloarthropathies. This is because IL-1 plays a more modest role in PsA pathogenesis compared to IL-17 and IL-23. Anakinra's primary indication remains RA and autoinflammatory conditions (e.g., CAPS, adult-onset Still's disease, gout flares). (Ref: Bolognia; Kavanaugh A et al., *Arthritis Res Ther* 2006)

- **NASH terminology (p. 171, Comorbidities row)** → **Note:** The source uses "NASH" (non-alcoholic steatohepatitis). Current nomenclature (2023 Delphi consensus) has renamed NASH to **MASH** (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis) and NAFLD to **MASLD** (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease). Both terms are acceptable for current examinations, but students should be aware of the updated nomenclature. (Ref: Rinella ME et al., *Hepatology* 2023)

## Integrated Margin Notes

- **Page 170 (top, Q12 Phase 3–4 continuation → Q13 transition):** The top of page 170 completes Q12's EULAR treatment ladder (Phases 3–4) before Q13 begins. The transition is natural — Q12 discusses PsA management, and Q13 immediately follows by comparing PsA to RA. This comparative framework is pedagogically designed to reinforce the distinguishing features of PsA. Students should study Q12 and Q13 as a paired unit for examination preparation.

- **Page 170 (right column, Introduction and Epidemiology):** The introductory text is presented in the right column alongside the beginning of the comparative table in the main body. Key examination-relevant epidemiological points: (1) RA is more common overall than PsA; (2) PsA has earlier onset (30–50 years) vs. RA (40–60 years); (3) PsA shows male predominance (M > W) vs. RA with female predominance (W > M, approximately 3:1); (4) PsA has a racial predilection for Caucasians, while RA is globally distributed. These demographic differences are commonly tested as MCQs.

- **Page 170 (comparative table, Etiopathogenesis section):** The genetic associations are presented side-by-side: PsA links to **HLA-B27** (Class I MHC, CD8+ T cells, axial disease) and RA links to **HLA-DRB1** (Class II MHC, CD4+ T cells, antibody-mediated). This fundamental immunological distinction — Class I/CD8+ T-cell-driven (PsA) vs. Class II/CD4+ T-cell/B-cell-driven (RA) — underpins all the clinical, serological, and therapeutic differences that follow in the table. The trigger profiles also differ: PsA triggers include infections and air pollution; RA triggers include gut dysbiosis and alcohol, reflecting different mucosal/environmental interactions.

- **Page 170 (comparative table, Immunopathology rows):** The **IL-9 row** for PsA is noteworthy — IL-9 is less commonly discussed but has been shown to promote pathological T-cell expansion and mast cell activation in PsA synovium. The **pannus formation** pathway in RA (chronic synovitis → fibroblast-like synoviocyte proliferation → pannus → cartilage/bone destruction) is fundamentally different from PsA's **entheseal-driven** pathology (enthesitis → periosteal new bone formation → ankylosis). This explains why radiographically PsA shows **proliferative** changes (new bone) whereas RA shows predominantly **erosive/destructive** changes.

- **Page 170 (comparative table, Clinical features rows):** The clinical features section contains several high-yield examination points: (1) **Tortuous bushy vessels (PsA) vs. straight branching vessels (RA)** in synovial biopsy/arthroscopy — this is a histological/arthroscopic distinction that reflects the different angiogenic patterns; (2) **SUHK** as a mnemonic for PsA nail changes (Subungual Hyperkeratosis, plus pitting, dystrophy, onycholysis); (3) **Uveitis** (PsA, especially with HLA-B27) vs. **keratoconjunctivitis/Sicca** (RA, as part of secondary Sjögren's syndrome); (4) **Rheumatoid nodules** are a cardinal feature of RA (present in ~25% of RA patients, especially seropositive disease) and do not occur in PsA.

- **Page 170–171 (table continuation, Serology and Radiology):** The serological distinction is straightforward and absolute for examination purposes: PsA is **seronegative** (RF−, CCP−), RA is **seropositive** (RF+ in ~80%, CCP+ in ~80%). Acute-phase reactants (ESR, CRP) are elevated in both but **significantly higher** in RA — reflecting the more intense systemic inflammatory burden in RA. Radiologically, the key contrast is: PsA = **proliferative** (new bone, ankylosis, periostitis) vs. RA = **erosive/destructive** (marginal erosions, osteopenia, joint space narrowing without new bone formation).

- **Page 171 (top, Radiological features continuation):** The MRI finding distinction is clinically important: PsA bone marrow edema occurs **near entheses** (reflecting entheseal-driven pathology), whereas RA bone marrow oedema occurs **near capsular attachments** (reflecting synovitis-driven pathology). This MRI pattern difference can help distinguish early PsA from early RA before classical radiographic changes develop.

- **Page 171 (Treatment response section):** The treatment response comparison is one of the most examination-relevant portions of this table. Key teaching points: (1) **IL-17A inhibitors work in PsA but not RA** — this confirms that IL-17 is a dominant pathway in PsA/SpA but not RA; (2) **IL-6 inhibitors work in RA but not PsA** — confirming IL-6 as a dominant RA pathway; (3) **Rituximab works in RA but not PsA** — confirming B-cell/autoantibody-driven pathogenesis in RA vs. T-cell-driven in PsA; (4) **MTX has mixed results in PsA** — it helps skin and peripheral joints but has limited evidence for axial PsA, whereas MTX is a cornerstone first-line DMARD in RA; (5) **Ustekinumab (IL-12/23) works in PsA but not RA** — further supporting the IL-23/Th17 axis as specific to PsA/psoriasis. This differential treatment response profile is a powerful framework for understanding the distinct immunopathogeneses of the two diseases.

- **Page 171 (table end, transition to Q14):** Q14 begins immediately after Q13's comparative table on the same page 171, transitioning from arthritis-focused content to a broader discussion of disorders of keratinization. This structural shift marks the chapter's pivot from the psoriasis/PsA disease block (Q1–Q13) to the keratinization disorders block (Q14 onwards).
