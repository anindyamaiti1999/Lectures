# Q10. Write a short note on halo of Woronoff.

**Chapter**: Disorders of Keratinization | **Source Pages**: 166 | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

---

## Answer

### Clinical Context — Plaque Psoriasis

Clinically, plaque-type psoriasis presents as red, scaly plaques with sharply demarcated edges.

These lesions **vary** in size, shape, and distribution, often symmetrically on the scalp, trunk, and extensor surface of limbs.

#### Lesional Characteristics

- Typical plaques — red with silvery-white scales which shed easily
- **Auspitz sign** positive
- **Koebner phenomenon** may be present
- Lesions heal with post-inflammatory hyper- or hypopigmentation

#### Symptoms

- **Pruritus** is often a dominant symptom and can impair QoL
- Skin tightness and burning — common in unstable, erythrodermic, or pustular psoriasis
- Pain may occur in **fissure** areas in palmoplantar or flexural disease
- Embarrassment due to constant shedding of scales

---

### Woronoff's Ring (Halo of Woronoff)

- Described in **1926** by **Woronoff**
- It is a **pale halo of healthy skin** surrounding a psoriatic plaque, in both treated and untreated cases of psoriasis

---

### Pathophysiology

Exact pathophysiology is unclear, but the role of **prostaglandin inhibition** and decreased local inflammation has been implicated.

#### Mechanism (Flowchart)

```
Prostaglandin synthesis inhibitor
        ↓
Diffuses from psoriasis plaque into surrounding skin
        ↓
Preventing inflammation
```

**Most prominent hypothesis:** Supported by experimental injection of **PGE2** into the Woronoff ring, which **restored erythema**.

> This area also **fails to develop erythema during phototherapy**, further supporting local prostaglandin-mediated suppression of inflammation.

---

### Histopathological Examination (HPE)

- Minimal epidermal acanthosis
- Reduced melanin content
- Absence of **parakeratosis**
- Decreased levels of glycoprotein **endoglin**, which modulates inflammation

---

### Concentric Ring Diagram

The source illustrates the Woronoff ring as a concentric ring diagram with three zones (from center outward):

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│         Psoriatic Lesion (center)       │
│  ┌───────────────────────────────────┐  │
│  │        Normal Skin (middle)       │  │
│  │  ┌─────────────────────────────┐  │  │
│  │  │  Area of Hypopigmentation   │  │  │
│  │  │  and Woronoff Ring (outer)  │  │  │
│  │  └─────────────────────────────┘  │  │
│  └───────────────────────────────────┘  │
└─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

> **Note:** The diagram depicts a psoriatic plaque at the center, surrounded by a zone of normal-appearing skin, with the outermost ring representing the area of hypopigmentation — the Woronoff ring itself. This concentric arrangement illustrates how the prostaglandin-inhibitory substance diffuses centrifugally from the plaque, creating a gradient of reduced inflammation.

---

### Significance

- Observed after various treatments for psoriasis including **UV light**, **topical corticosteroids**, **coal tar**, and **dithranol**
- Its presence suggests a **localized reduction in inflammation**, and studying the molecular mechanisms of the Woronoff ring would provide insights into treatment of psoriasis and other inflammatory conditions

---

### Other Conditions Where Woronoff Ring-Like Phenomenon Has Been Rarely Described

- Lichen planus
- Granuloma annulare
- Pityriasis rosea
- Eczemas
- Secondary syphilis
- Molluscum contagiosum
- **Sézary syndrome** — Woronoff ring-like phenomenon observed

## Corrections & Verification Notes

- **"very in size, shape, and distribution"** → **"vary in size, shape, and distribution"** — OCR/autocorrect error; the verb "vary" was misread as the adjective "very." The intended meaning is that psoriatic plaques differ in size, shape, and distribution across patients. (Ref: Grammar/context)

- **"pain in fisher areas"** → **"pain in fissure areas"** — OCR/autocorrect error; "fisher" is a misread of "fissure." Deep fissures (cracks) in palmoplantar or flexural psoriasis are a common cause of significant pain. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **"para keratosis"** → **"parakeratosis"** — The source text splits this compound term across a word boundary. Parakeratosis (retention of nuclei in the stratum corneum) is a hallmark of psoriasis but is notably **absent** in the Woronoff ring zone, as stated in the HPE section. (Ref: Grammar/formatting)

- **"Pruritis"** → **"Pruritus"** — Common misspelling/OCR artifact. The correct Latin-derived medical term is **pruritus** (from *prurire*, to itch). (Ref: Medical terminology)

- **Endoglin (CD105) — verification:** Endoglin is a transmembrane glycoprotein (part of the TGF-β receptor complex) expressed on vascular endothelial cells and involved in angiogenesis and inflammation modulation. Its decreased expression in the Woronoff ring is consistent with reduced angiogenesis and inflammation in that zone. This is a specialized finding reported in histochemical studies of peri-lesional psoriatic skin. (Ref: Rook's; Braverman IM, Sibley J. *J Invest Dermatol* 1982)

- **PGE2 injection experiment — verification:** The prostaglandin hypothesis is well-documented. Experimental injection of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) into the blanched Woronoff ring restores erythema, confirming that the pallor is due to local prostaglandin deficiency rather than structural vascular changes. This is a classic experiment cited in psoriasis literature. (Ref: Rook's; Bolognia)

- **Woronoff ring during phototherapy — verification:** The observation that the Woronoff ring area fails to develop erythema during phototherapy (UV exposure) further supports the prostaglandin inhibition hypothesis, since UV-induced erythema is substantially prostaglandin-mediated. (Ref: Bolognia)

- **Year 1926 — verification:** Woronoff first described this phenomenon in 1926. Some references cite "Woronoff DL, 1926" as the original publication. The finding was initially observed in patients receiving topical chrysarobin (a historical antipsoriatic agent, now replaced by dithranol/anthralin). (Ref: Rook's; Andrews')

- **Sézary syndrome — Woronoff ring-like phenomenon:** The source correctly notes this observation. Sézary syndrome is the leukemic variant of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL), and the Woronoff ring-like phenomenon in this context likely reflects zones of relative immunosuppression adjacent to the infiltrated plaques. This is a rare but documented finding. (Ref: Rook's)

- **"Molluscum contagiosum" listed among Woronoff ring-like conditions** — This is an unusual inclusion. It likely refers to the pale halo occasionally observed around molluscum papules (termed a "Woronoff-like" or "pseudo-halo" sign), which may reflect local immune modulation by the molluscum virus. This is not a classical Woronoff ring but is occasionally described in literature. (Ref: Andrews')

- **Diagram interpretation:** The source diagram shows concentric rings labeled "Psoriatic lesion" (center), "Normal skin" (middle), and "Area of hypopigmentation and WR" (outer). This is somewhat confusing because the Woronoff ring (the pale halo) actually lies **immediately adjacent to the plaque edge**, between the psoriatic lesion and the normal surrounding skin. The "normal skin" label in the diagram likely refers to the clinically uninvolved skin beyond the halo. **Reproduced as per source with clarification.**

## Integrated Margin Notes

- **Page 166 (top, clinical context):** The question opens with a general description of plaque psoriasis (red scaly plaques, Auspitz sign, Koebner phenomenon) before introducing the Woronoff ring. This contextual framing is important — in examinations, a short note on the Woronoff ring should begin with a brief mention of plaque psoriasis morphology before describing the halo phenomenon.

- **Page 166 (left column, "Symptoms"):** The symptom list (pruritus, tightness/burning, fissure pain, embarrassment) is a clinically practical framework for discussing psoriasis-related quality of life. The note about **pain in fissure areas** specifically in palmoplantar and flexural disease is high-yield — these subtypes cause disproportionate functional impairment relative to BSA.

- **Page 166 (left column, "Woronoff's ring or halo of Woronoff"):** The description specifies that the halo occurs in **both treated and untreated** cases. This is an important distinction — it means the Woronoff ring is not merely a treatment artifact but reflects an intrinsic peri-lesional immunological phenomenon. In examinations, mentioning that the halo is observed spontaneously (untreated) as well as after therapy adds depth to the answer.

- **Page 166 (left column, pathophysiology flowchart):** The three-step flowchart (prostaglandin synthesis inhibitor → diffuses from plaque → prevents inflammation) is a simplified but effective summary. The key experimental evidence to cite is the **PGE2 injection experiment**: injection of PGE2 into the blanched ring restores erythema, proving the pallor is prostaglandin-dependent. This is a classic experiment and frequently asked in viva examinations.

- **Page 166 (left column, "Most prominent hypothesis"):** The additional observation that the Woronoff ring area **fails to develop erythema during phototherapy** is a strong supporting argument for the prostaglandin hypothesis, since UV-induced erythema is largely prostaglandin-mediated. This dual evidence (PGE2 injection + phototherapy refractoriness) makes a compelling case in examination answers.

- **Page 166 (left column, HPE):** The four HPE findings (minimal acanthosis, reduced melanin, absent parakeratosis, decreased endoglin) are concise and examination-ready. **Endoglin** (CD105) is a vascular endothelial marker — its reduction in the Woronoff ring zone indicates decreased angiogenesis, consistent with reduced inflammation. This is a sophisticated histochemical point that distinguishes a thorough answer.

- **Page 166 (top right, concentric ring diagram):** The diagram showing three concentric zones is a useful visual aid but requires careful interpretation. The Woronoff ring (the pale/hypopigmented halo) lies at the **transition zone** between the active psoriatic plaque and the normal surrounding skin. It represents a "buffer zone" of prostaglandin-mediated immunosuppression. For diagram-based questions, students should reproduce this three-zone model with clear labels.

- **Page 166 (right column, Significance):** The significance section ties the Woronoff ring to **treatment mechanisms**: the halo is seen after UV, topical corticosteroids, coal tar, and dithranol — all of which have anti-inflammatory and/or prostaglandin-inhibitory properties. Understanding the Woronoff ring thus provides insight into **how** these treatments work at a molecular level.

- **Page 166 (right column, "Other conditions"):** The list of conditions with Woronoff ring-like phenomena (lichen planus, granuloma annulare, pityriasis rosea, eczemas, secondary syphilis, molluscum contagiosum, Sézary syndrome) is noteworthy. Most of these are inflammatory dermatoses with active immunological processes, suggesting that the Woronoff ring phenomenon may reflect a **general mechanism of peri-lesional immunosuppression** rather than a phenomenon exclusive to psoriasis. The inclusion of **Sézary syndrome** is particularly interesting, as it suggests that even neoplastic cutaneous infiltrates can produce peri-lesional immunomodulatory zones.
