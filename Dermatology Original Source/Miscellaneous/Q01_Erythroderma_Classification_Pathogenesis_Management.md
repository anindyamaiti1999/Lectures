# Q1. Classify Erythrodermas. Discuss Pathogenesis, Complications, and Management.

**Chapter**: 11 — Eczemas | **Source Pages**: Image 1 (~p.197) – Image 2 (p.198) | **References**: Rook's Textbook of Dermatology, Bolognia Dermatology, Andrews' Diseases of the Skin

## Answer

### Definition

Erythroderma (exfoliative dermatitis) is defined as **generalized erythema and scaling involving more than 80–90% of the body surface area (BSA)**.

- Males are more commonly affected than females, with a **male-to-female ratio of 2–4:1**.
- Commonly occurs as a generalization of pre-existing dermatoses (e.g., atopic dermatitis, psoriasis).
- When no specific aetiology is identified, it is referred to as **idiopathic erythroderma**.

---

### Classification

**1. By Aetiology**
- **Primary (idiopathic) erythroderma** — No identifiable underlying cause.
- **Secondary erythroderma** — Generalization of a pre-existing dermatosis.

**2. By Onset / Duration**
- **Acute erythroderma**
- **Chronic erythroderma**

### Pathogenesis

- **Expression of adhesion molecules** in epithelial cells → increases dermal inflammation and epidermal proliferation.
- An increase in **mitosis** causes:
  - ↑ Overall epidermal cell number
  - ↑ Cell turnover
  - ↓ Transit time through the epidermis
- This results in the characteristic **dramatic exfoliation** seen in erythrodermic patients.

---

### Clinical Features

#### Skin
- Diffuse **generalized erythema and scaling** involving >80–90% BSA.
- **Pruritus** is present in up to **90% of cases**, leading to lichenification.
- Disease-specific cutaneous clues:
  - *Palmoplantar keratoderma* → Pityriasis rubra pilaris
  - *Nail pitting* → Psoriasis

#### Nails
- **Shiny nails** — most commonly observed.
- Others: Beau's lines, brittleness, subungual hyperkeratosis, complete nail shedding.

#### Hair
- **Diffuse non-scarring alopecia** — chronic erythroderma.
- **Scarring alopecia** — mycosis fungoides.

#### Systemic Manifestations

| Feature | Mechanism / Notes |
|---|---|
| **Pedal oedema** | Hypoalbuminaemia |
| **Facial oedema** | Mycosis fungoides or drug reaction |
| **High-output cardiac failure** | ↑ Cutaneous blood flow → ↑ vascular permeability → ↑ transepidermal fluid loss |
| **Hypothermia** | Massive heat loss through inflamed skin |
| **Anaemia** | Protein loss + chronic disease (chronic erythroderma) |
| **Peripheral lymphadenopathy** | Dermatopathic lymphadenopathy (reactive) |
| **Hepatomegaly** | Seen in ~20% of cases |

---

### Complications

- **High-output cardiac failure**
- **Adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)**
- **Sepsis**

---

### Management

#### 1. General Supportive Measures

**a) Prevention of hypothermia**
- Maintain environmental temperature at **30–32°C**.
- Use infrared lamps, air-fluidized beds, or a burn cage.

**b) Haemodynamic monitoring**
- Monitor pulse, blood pressure, and urine output continuously.
- Target urine output: **50–100 mL/h**.
- Tachycardia + reduced urine output (in absence of sepsis) → **hypovolaemia**.

**c) Fluid and electrolyte management**
- Initial fluids of choice: **Colloids** (human albumin or fresh frozen plasma) **+ normal saline (NS)**.
- Daily fluid requirement calculated based on the previous day's output.

**d) Nutritional support**
- High-protein diet: **~2–3 g/kg/day** supplementation.

**e) Prevention of infection**
- Barrier nursing to prevent secondary sepsis.

#### 2. Specific Treatment (targeting underlying cause)

- Systemic corticosteroids
- Cyclosporine
- *(Other disease-specific agents depending on confirmed aetiology)*
