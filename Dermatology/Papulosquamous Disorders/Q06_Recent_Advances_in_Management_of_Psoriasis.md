# Recent Advances in the Management of Psoriasis
### A Mini-Lecture (Disorders of Keratinization — Rook's / Bolognia / Andrews')

---

## 0. Before we begin: why this question is really being asked

When an examiner says *"Discuss recent advances in the management of psoriasis,"* they are not testing whether you can recite a list of new drug names. They are testing whether you understand **the logic of the disease**. Every "advance" in psoriasis over the last 30 years has come from one of three moves:

1. **Aiming better** — hitting a more specific molecule in the inflammatory cascade (TNF → IL-12/23 → IL-23p19 → IL-17A → IL-17A *and* F → intracellular TYK2 → transcription factor RORγt).
2. **Delivering better** — getting an existing drug to where it's needed without flooding the whole body (microneedles, nanocarriers, novel vehicles).
3. **Choosing better** — predicting *which* patient needs *which* drug, and *when* (biomarkers, multiomics, treat-to-target, early intervention).

Your source text captures exactly this with its opening line — **new biological agents, biomarkers for personalised treatment, and gene- and cell-based therapies**. Hold on to those three pillars; they are the skeleton of the whole answer, and I'll keep returning to them.

## 1. The big picture: the map you need before the drugs make sense

You cannot appreciate why bimekizumab or deucravacitinib are clever unless you can see the circuit they interrupt. So let me walk the pathogenic pathway once, slowly, because **every single drug below is a pair of scissors applied to one specific wire in this circuit.**

### 1.1 The initiation phase (the spark)

- Something stresses the keratinocyte — trauma (Koebner), infection, drugs (lithium, beta-blockers, antimalarials), stress, smoking.
- Stressed keratinocytes release **antimicrobial peptides**, most importantly **LL-37 (cathelicidin)**. LL-37 binds the patient's own DNA/RNA and turns it into a self-adjuvant — essentially, it makes the body's own nucleic acid *look like a virus*.
- **Plasmacytoid dendritic cells** sense this complex and pour out **interferon-α**.
- IFN-α matures **myeloid dendritic cells**, which migrate to lymph nodes and secrete the two master cytokines: **IL-23** and IL-12.

> **Analogy:** think of LL-37 + self-DNA as a false alarm pulled inside a fire station. The alarm is genuine-sounding but the fire isn't real — and the immune "fire brigade" that responds never stands down.

### 1.2 The amplification phase (the self-sustaining loop)

- **IL-23** is the *survival and maintenance* cytokine for **Th17 cells** (and Tc17, γδ T cells, MAIT cells, ILC3s). It signals through the **IL-23 receptor → JAK2/TYK2 → STAT3**, and STAT3 switches on the master transcription factor **RORγt**.
- **RORγt** is the "identity switch" of the Th17 lineage. Once on, the cell commits to producing **IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, TNF-α, and IL-26**.
- **IL-17A and IL-17F** act on the keratinocyte — the true *effector* target. The keratinocyte responds by:
  - proliferating rapidly and failing to mature → **acanthosis, parakeratosis, loss of granular layer**;
  - releasing chemokines (CXCL1, CXCL8/IL-8) → **neutrophil influx → Munro's microabscesses, spongiform pustules of Kogoj**;
  - releasing more LL-37, S100 proteins, and β-defensins → **which re-feeds the dendritic cells.**
- **IL-22** drives the epidermal hyperplasia; **TNF-α** amplifies everything; **VEGF** drives the dilated dermal capillaries that give you the **Auspitz sign**.

That last step is the crucial insight: **psoriasis is a feed-forward loop.** The keratinocyte is not a passive victim; it is an active participant that keeps the immune fire burning. This is why psoriasis relapses at the same sites (tissue-resident memory T cells, TRM, sit in "healed" skin) and why *stopping* therapy usually means relapse.

### 1.3 The special case: the IL-36 axis and pustular disease

Plaque psoriasis is an **IL-23/IL-17-driven** disease. **Generalised pustular psoriasis (GPP)** is, by contrast, largely an **IL-36-driven, innate, neutrophilic autoinflammatory** disease.

- IL-36α/β/γ are IL-1 family cytokines; their action is normally restrained by **IL-36Ra**, encoded by ***IL36RN***.
- Loss-of-function *IL36RN* mutations cause **DITRA** (Deficiency of IL-36 Receptor Antagonist) — a Mendelian cause of GPP.
- Unopposed IL-36 signalling → massive IL-8/CXCL chemokine release → **sterile neutrophilic pustules, fever, systemic toxicity.**

**Why this matters clinically:** it explains why IL-17/IL-23 blockers work only modestly and slowly in GPP, whereas an **IL-36 receptor blocker clears pustules in days.** Different disease, different wire, different scissors.

### 1.4 The systemic dimension

Psoriasis is no longer "a skin disease." The same IL-17/TNF axis drives **psoriatic arthritis (up to 30%), metabolic syndrome, NAFLD, uveitis, IBD, depression, and accelerated atherosclerosis.** Hence a genuine "advance" in management includes **comorbidity screening and cardiometabolic risk reduction**, not just skin clearance.

## 2. Why do we need advances at all? (The unmet needs — and the mechanism behind each)

Your source lists four reasons. Let me turn each from a bullet into an *understanding*.

| Unmet need | Why it happens (mechanism) | What the "advance" must therefore do |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary treatment failure** (never responds) | Wrong pathway dominant in that patient (e.g., an IL-17-low, IL-36-high pustular phenotype given an IL-17 blocker); or genetic non-responder (e.g., HLA-C*06:02-negative patients respond less well to ustekinumab) | **Biomarker-guided selection** — pillar 2 |
| **Secondary failure** (response then loss) | **Immunogenicity** — anti-drug antibodies neutralise the biologic and speed its clearance (classically infliximab > adalimumab > etanercept; rare with IL-23 blockers); also cytokine-pathway switching | **Less immunogenic, longer-acting, or oral small molecules**; therapeutic drug monitoring |
| **Difficult-to-treat sites: scalp, nails, genital, palmoplantar, intertriginous** | Barrier and anatomy problems: thick scalp hair + scale blocks topicals; the **nail matrix is 5 mm under the proximal fold**, so topicals never arrive; genital/flexural skin is thin and occluded, so steroids atrophy it; palmoplantar stratum corneum is 10–20× thicker | **Better delivery (microneedles, nanocarriers, new vehicles)** and **steroid-free topicals** — pillar 1 of delivery |
| **Cumulative toxicity & inability to use long-term** | Methotrexate → hepatic fibrosis, cytopenias; ciclosporin → nephrotoxicity, hypertension (limit ~1–2 years); acitretin → teratogenicity (3-year contraception), hyperlipidaemia; PUVA → cumulative SCC/melanoma risk; potent topical steroids → atrophy, striae, tachyphylaxis, HPA suppression | **Targeted therapies with narrow immunological footprints**, and **dose-sparing delivery** |

Add two more the source implies but doesn't state, and which score marks:

- **Cost and access** — biologics are expensive; hence the rise of **biosimilars** (adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, ustekinumab biosimilars) as a genuine "advance in management" in the Indian and global context.
- **The wish for drug-free remission / disease modification** — all current drugs are *suppressive*, not *curative*. Hence interest in **early intervention (the "window of opportunity")**, Treg-based therapy, and gene-directed approaches.

## 3. Pillar 1a — Advances in topical therapy

### 3.1 Roflumilast cream — the non-steroidal PDE4 inhibitor

**What it is:** a highly potent, selective **phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) inhibitor**, formulated as a **0.3% cream (Zoryve)**, applied **once daily**; FDA-approved 2022 for plaque psoriasis (including intertriginous psoriasis), with a **0.3% foam** for scalp and body.

**How it works — the cAMP story:**
- PDE4 is the enzyme that **degrades cyclic AMP (cAMP)** inside immune cells and keratinocytes.
- Inhibit PDE4 → **intracellular cAMP rises** → activates **protein kinase A** → phosphorylates CREB and **suppresses NF-κB**.
- Net effect: **down** goes TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23, IFN-γ, IL-8; **up** goes the anti-inflammatory **IL-10**.

> **Analogy:** cAMP is the immune cell's internal "calm-down" signal. PDE4 is the drain that empties the calm-down bath. Block the drain, and the cell stays calm.

**Why it matters (this is the exam point):** it is a **steroid-free topical with no atrophy, no striae, no tachyphylaxis, and no HPA-axis suppression**, so it can be used **indefinitely and on delicate sites** — face, genitals, inframammary and gluteal folds — exactly where potent steroids are most dangerous.

> ⚠️ **Correction to your notes:** the source reads *"a nonsterile option."* This is certainly a transcription error for **"non-steroidal option."** Roflumilast's headline advantage is precisely that it is **not a corticosteroid.** (Also note: the source's "PDE four inhibitor" should be written **PDE4**.)

**Efficacy anchor:** in the DERMIS-1/2 trials, ~**37–42%** achieved IGA success at week 8 versus **6–8%** with vehicle. Main side effects are mild application-site reactions, diarrhoea, headache — far less GI upset than **oral apremilast**, its systemic cousin, because systemic exposure is low.

**Worth adding (companion advance, same category):** **tapinarof 1% cream** — a first-in-class **aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) agonist**. Binding AhR in keratinocytes down-regulates Th17 cytokines, up-regulates barrier proteins (filaggrin, loricrin), and reduces oxidative stress. It is also steroid-free, and notably produces a **"remittive effect"** — a drug-free interval of ~4 months after clearance in trials. Mentioning roflumilast *and* tapinarof together shows you know the whole modern non-steroidal topical class.

### 3.2 Newer vehicles (a small but real advance)

Adherence is the silent killer of topical therapy. Reformulating old molecules into elegant vehicles — **calcipotriol/betamethasone aerosol foam**, and **PAD (polyaphron dispersion) technology cream** — improves penetration, cosmetic acceptability, and therefore real-world efficacy. An "advance in management" need not be a new molecule.

## 4. Pillar 1b — Advances in drug **delivery**: microneedles and nanocarriers

This is the section students skip and examiners love, because it shows you understand **pharmacokinetics at the skin level**.

### 4.1 The problem to be solved

The **stratum corneum** is a "brick-and-mortar" wall: corneocyte bricks in a lipid-lamellar mortar. It admits only molecules that are **small (<500 Da) and moderately lipophilic**. Now add the psoriasis paradox:

> A psoriatic plaque is covered by **thick, loosely adherent, parakeratotic scale**. Intuitively you'd think the defective barrier lets drugs in easily — and the *barrier function* is indeed impaired. But the **bulk of hyperkeratotic scale physically obstructs** drug contact with living epidermis, and drug is lost with each desquamating flake. So plaques are simultaneously "leaky" and "blocked."

Hence two engineering solutions: **punch through the wall (microneedles)** or **smuggle through the wall (nanocarriers)**.

### 4.2 Microneedles (MN)

**What they are:** arrays of micron-scale projections (typically **25–900 µm**) on a patch, which pierce the stratum corneum and viable epidermis to create transient aqueous **microchannels**, but are **too short to reach dermal nerve endings and most blood vessels** — hence **minimally painful and bloodless.**

> **Analogy:** instead of trying to soak a raincoat, you make a hundred pinholes in it and pour the drug straight through.

**The four types — learn them with their "verbs":**

| Type | Mechanism ("verb") | Practical note |
|---|---|---|
| **Solid** | *Poke and patch* — pierce first, remove, then apply drug/gel over the microchannels | Simplest; channels reseal in ~1–2 h |
| **Coated** | *Coat and poke* — drug dried onto needle surface dissolves off in the skin | Rapid delivery; limited payload (thin coat) |
| **Dissolving** | *Poke and release* — needles made of biodegradable polymer (PVP, PVA, hyaluronic acid, chitosan) carrying drug, dissolve *in situ* | **No sharps waste**, self-application friendly, sustained release; currently the most promising for psoriasis |
| **Hollow** | *Poke and flow* — micro-injection of liquid through bores | Largest payload; can deliver biologics/macromolecules |

> ⚠️ **Correction:** the source lists *"solid, **cod**, dissolving, or hollow."* "Cod" is an OCR/autocorrect artefact for **"coated."**

**Drugs successfully delivered by MN in psoriasis research:** **corticosteroids** (e.g., triamcinolone, betamethasone), **calcipotriol**, and **methotrexate** — plus experimentally tacrolimus, cyclosporin, curcumin, and even anti-TNF/siRNA constructs.

**Why this matters — the benefits chain:**
> Bypass stratum corneum → **higher local intra-plaque concentration** → **lower total dose needed** (dose sparing) → **less systemic absorption** → **fewer systemic adverse effects**; plus avoidance of hepatic **first-pass metabolism**, and painless self-administration → **better adherence.**

**Especially attractive for:** **nail psoriasis** (MN can breach the nail plate or periungual skin, replacing painful intralesional triamcinolone injections) and **thick palmoplantar/scalp plaques.**

### 4.3 Nanocarriers

**What they are:** colloidal carriers in the **1–1000 nm** range that encapsulate the drug, protect it, hold it in the skin, and release it slowly. Your source lists them in abbreviated form; here they are in full:

| Nanocarrier | Composition | Distinguishing property |
|---|---|---|
| **Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLN)** — source's "solid" | Solid lipid core + surfactant | Occlusive, film-forming, controlled release; can expel drug on lipid crystallisation |
| **Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLC)** — source's "nanostructured" | Blend of solid + liquid lipids → imperfect crystal lattice | **Higher drug loading, less expulsion** than SLN (2nd-generation SLN) |
| **Liposomes** | Phospholipid bilayer vesicles | Biocompatible; but rigid ones mostly stay in stratum corneum → good *depot*, poor penetrator |
| **Transferosomes** | Liposome + **edge activator** (e.g., sodium cholate, Span 80) | **Ultradeformable** — squeeze through intercellular gaps along the hydration gradient |
| **Ethosomes** | Phospholipid + **high ethanol (20–45%)** | Ethanol fluidises SC lipids *and* the vesicle → deep penetration |
| **Nanofibers** | Electrospun polymer mats | Huge surface area, act as a **drug-loaded dressing**, sustained release, mechanical protection |

> **Analogy:** a liposome is a rigid coach that gets stuck at the gate; a **transferosome** is a deflatable inflatable boat that squeezes through the gap; an **ethosome** brings its own solvent to soften the gate.

**The flagship example your source gives — nanocarrier-delivered methotrexate.** Why is this so appealing? Oral/parenteral MTX works, but its toxicity is **systemic** (hepatic fibrosis, myelosuppression, pneumonitis, mucositis). MTX is also **hydrophilic and ionised at skin pH**, so it barely penetrates skin on its own. Encapsulate it in an NLC/ethosome and you get **high concentration in the plaque with minimal plasma levels** → *the therapeutic benefit of MTX without the bone marrow and liver risk.* That single sentence is the whole point of nanodermatology.

**Additional advantages worth stating:** protection of labile drugs from degradation, targeting of the **pilosebaceous unit** (useful for scalp), improved solubility of poorly soluble drugs (calcipotriol, ciclosporin), and reduced irritancy (e.g., dithranol, tazarotene).

## 5. Pillar 2 — Biomarkers, multiomics and precision dermatology

### 5.1 The problem

Today, choosing a biologic is largely **trial and error** guided by comorbidities and cost. Roughly **20–40%** of patients do not reach PASI 90 with any given first agent. Each failed trial costs the patient 4–6 months of disease, and costs the system a fortune. **Precision medicine asks: can we know in advance?**

### 5.2 What a biomarker is, and the three questions it can answer

A biomarker is any objectively measurable characteristic — a gene variant, a transcript, a protein, an imaging finding — that reports on biology. In psoriasis we want biomarkers that are:

1. **Prognostic** — will this disease progress, extend, or develop **psoriatic arthritis**? (Huge unmet need: earlier diagnosis of PsA prevents irreversible erosion.)
2. **Predictive / theranostic** — will *this patient* respond to *this drug*?
3. **Comorbidity-risk** — what is this patient's cardiometabolic or hepatic risk?

**Worked examples that make the concept concrete:**
- **HLA-C\*06:02** — the *PSORS1* risk allele. Carriers tend to have early-onset disease, and in several cohorts respond **better to ustekinumab (IL-12/23)** than to adalimumab. The prototype pharmacogenetic marker in dermatology.
- ***IL23R*, *TNFAIP3*, *TNIP1*, *ERAP1* variants** — modulate response to anti-TNF agents.
- ***IL36RN* mutation status** — identifies DITRA/GPP patients likely to respond dramatically to **IL-36 blockade**. This is precision medicine already in the clinic.
- **Serum drug levels + anti-drug antibodies (therapeutic drug monitoring)** — distinguishes "under-dosed" from "immunised" from "truly refractory," which changes management from *dose escalation* to *switch class*.
- **Body weight/BMI** — a crude but powerful pharmacokinetic biomarker: heavier patients need weight-based or higher-dose regimens (e.g., ustekinumab 90 mg, infliximab mg/kg).
- **CRP and GlycA**, and imaging (**FDG-PET/CT aortic uptake**) — track the systemic inflammatory burden and cardiovascular risk, and improve with effective biologic therapy.

### 5.3 Multiomics — what the word actually means

"Omics" = studying an entire class of molecules at once, rather than one at a time. **Multiomics** = integrating these layers, usually by machine learning, to reconstruct the patient's actual biology.

| Layer | What it measures | Psoriasis application |
|---|---|---|
| **Genomics** | DNA sequence/variants (GWAS; >80 psoriasis susceptibility loci, most in the IL-23/IL-17/NF-κB axis) | Risk, endotype, pharmacogenetics |
| **Transcriptomics** | mRNA expression (bulk, **single-cell RNA-seq**, spatial) | Defines the "psoriasis transcriptome"; **molecular residual disease** in clinically clear skin explains relapse |
| **Proteomics** | Actual proteins (Olink, mass spectrometry) | Circulating signatures of severity and PsA risk |
| *(also)* **Epigenomics, metabolomics, microbiomics, lipidomics** | Methylation, metabolites, skin/gut flora | Environmental interface; the "gut–skin axis" |

> ⚠️ **Correction:** your source reads **"protonomics."** There is no such field. The correct term is **PROTEOMICS** — the study of the proteome (all the proteins of a cell/tissue). Write it correctly in the exam; "protonomics" will read as a howler.

> **Analogy for multiomics:** genomics tells you the *blueprint* of the house; transcriptomics tells you which *orders* have been issued today; proteomics tells you which *workers are actually on site swinging hammers*; metabolomics shows you the *rubble and sawdust* they produced. Only by overlaying all four do you know what is truly being built.

### 5.4 The clinical philosophy that biomarkers enable

Two modern concepts worth naming:

- **Treat-to-target:** the old goal was PASI 75; the modern target is **PASI 90/100** or **absolute PASI ≤ 2**, plus DLQI 0/1 — because residual disease at visible sites still destroys quality of life. Defining a target changes behaviour.
- **Early intervention / disease interception:** the hypothesis that treating early, aggressively, before tissue-resident memory T cells and epigenetic "scars" are entrenched, may **modify the disease course** and even permit drug-free remission. This is being tested (e.g., the STEPIn study with early NB-UVB vs early biologic). If true, it will be the single biggest conceptual advance of the decade.

## 6. Pillar 3a — New biologics and small molecules (the heart of the answer)

Here is the organising principle: **we started broad and became progressively narrower and deeper.**

> **TNF-α (broad cytokine) → IL-12/23 p40 → IL-23 p19 (upstream master) → IL-17A (effector) → IL-17A + F (dual effector) → IL-36R (pustular pathway) → intracellular TYK2 (signal) → RORγt (transcription) → ROCK2 (cytoskeletal/metabolic switch)**

Notice the trajectory: from *extracellular cytokines* (biologics, injectable) to *intracellular signalling* (small molecules, **oral**). That shift — **oral drugs with biologic-like efficacy** — is arguably *the* theme of current psoriasis therapeutics.

### 6.1 IL-23 (p19) inhibitors — **mirikizumab**

**What it is:** a humanised IgG monoclonal antibody against the **p19 subunit of IL-23**, which is unique to IL-23 (p40 is shared with IL-12). Thus p19 blockers **spare IL-12/Th1 immunity**, which is thought to be protective.

**Why upstream blockade is elegant:** IL-23 does not damage the skin itself; it is the **survival factor** for pathogenic Th17/Tc17 and IL-23R⁺ resident memory cells. Remove IL-23 and the pathogenic T-cell population **withers** rather than being merely silenced.

**Two clinical consequences you should be able to predict from that mechanism:**
1. **Very durable remission and long dosing intervals** (risankizumab every 12 weeks; guselkumab every 8) — because you are depleting a *cell population*, not neutralising a *fast-turnover cytokine*.
2. **No paradoxical IBD** — in fact IL-23 blockers *treat* Crohn's/UC (the exact opposite of IL-17 blockers, which can worsen IBD — see §9 misunderstandings).

> ⚠️ **Important nuance on mirikizumab:** pharmacologically the source is right — it *is* an IL-23p19 inhibitor, and its psoriasis phase-3 programme (**OASIS-1/OASIS-2**) showed excellent efficacy (PASI 90 in ~60–70%). **However, mirikizumab (Omvoh) was licensed for ulcerative colitis (2023) and Crohn's disease, and was *not* taken to approval for psoriasis.** The **approved** IL-23p19 inhibitors for psoriasis are **guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab**. In an exam, name mirikizumab as the source does, but add that sentence — it shows genuine reading.

**On the horizon (bonus marks):** **icotrokinra (JNJ-2113)** — the first **oral peptide antagonist of the IL-23 receptor**. An orally bioavailable peptide blocking a cytokine receptor was thought nearly impossible; the ICONIC trials show biologic-range efficacy. This is the frontier.

### 6.2 TYK2 inhibition — **deucravacitinib**

**What it is:** an **oral, first-in-class, allosteric TYK2 inhibitor** (Sotyktu), FDA/EMA-approved 2022 for moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis.

**Mechanism — and why "allosteric" is the whole story:**
- TYK2 is one of four Janus kinases (JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, TYK2). It is the **obligate partner** for signalling by **IL-23, IL-12, and type I interferons** — i.e., precisely the three psoriasis-relevant cytokine families.
- Classical JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib) bind the **catalytic (ATP-binding, JH1) domain**. Because all JAK catalytic sites look alike, these drugs inevitably spill over onto JAK1/2/3 — and JAK2 is needed for **erythropoietin, thrombopoietin, GM-CSF**, while JAK1/3 serve IL-2, IL-7, IL-15. Hence the class effects: **anaemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, dyslipidaemia, herpes zoster, VTE, and the boxed warnings on malignancy and MACE.**
- Deucravacitinib instead binds the **regulatory pseudokinase (JH2) domain** of TYK2 — a structurally *unique* pocket. Binding there **locks TYK2 in its auto-inhibited conformation**, so it cannot be activated at all.

> **Analogy:** classical JAK inhibitors jam a key into the *ignition* — but all four cars have similar ignitions, so you stall vehicles you didn't intend to. Deucravacitinib instead engages the **steering lock** of one specific car, a part that only that car possesses. Exquisite selectivity by targeting a *different* part, not a *tighter fit* on the same part.

**Consequences (highly examinable):** >100-fold selectivity for TYK2 over JAK1/3 and >2000-fold over JAK2 at therapeutic doses → **no routine laboratory monitoring required, no boxed warning** (unlike JAK inhibitors), no meaningful cytopenias or lipid changes. Efficacy (POETYK PSO-1/2): **PASI 75 ~58% at week 16**, versus apremilast ~35% and placebo ~13% — i.e., clearly superior to the older oral option, with good scalp and nail response. Common AEs: nasopharyngitis, acne-form eruptions, oral herpes, folliculitis.

> ⚠️ **Correction of emphasis:** your source files deucravacitinib under the heading *"JAK inhibitors."* This is **imprecise and a common exam trap.** Deucravacitinib is a **selective allosteric TYK2 inhibitor** — mechanistically and safety-wise it must be *contrasted with*, not grouped with, JAK inhibitors. The body of the source text (which says "selectively targets TYK2… avoiding the broad immunosuppressive side effects associated with JAK inhibitors") is correct; only the heading misleads.

**Also emerging:** **zasocitinib (TAK-279)**, a second oral allosteric TYK2 inhibitor in phase 3, and topical TYK2/JAK agents.

### 6.3 IL-36 receptor blockade — **spesolimab** (and imsidolimab)

**What it is:** a humanised IgG1 monoclonal antibody against the **IL-36 receptor (IL-36R)**, blocking signalling by all three agonists (IL-36α, β, γ) at once.

**Why the target is right:** as explained in §1.3, GPP is an **IL-36-driven innate neutrophilic autoinflammatory disease**, not simply "psoriasis with pustules."

**The clinical result is one of the most dramatic in modern dermatology (Effisayil-1, *NEJM* 2021):** a **single intravenous dose** produced **complete pustular clearance at week 1 in 54% vs 6% with placebo**, with fever and systemic toxicity resolving in parallel. Compare that with historical GPP management — acitretin, ciclosporin, methotrexate, infliximab — in a condition with appreciable mortality from sepsis, ARDS, cardiorespiratory failure, and hypocalcaemia.

> ⚠️ **Update to your source:** the notes say spesolimab is *"currently under trials"* for GPP. In fact **spesolimab (Spevigo) was FDA-approved in 2022 for GPP flares in adults**, with subsequent approval of **subcutaneous dosing for flare *prevention*/chronic treatment (2024)**. It is now the **first-ever licensed therapy specifically for GPP** — say that, and you convert a stale bullet into a headline. A related agent, **imsidolimab (anti-IL-36R)**, is also in development.

**Broader relevance:** IL-36R blockade is also being explored in **palmoplantar pustulosis, acrodermatitis continua of Hallopeau, and hidradenitis suppurativa** — i.e., the neutrophilic/pustular spectrum.

### 6.4 Dual IL-17A/IL-17F blockade — **bimekizumab**

**What it is:** a humanised IgG1 monoclonal antibody that **neutralises both IL-17A and IL-17F** — the first of its class (Bimzelx; approved EU 2021, FDA 2023).

**Why dual blockade is more than a marketing point:**
- IL-17A and IL-17F are structurally homologous (~50%) and bind the **same IL-17RA/RC receptor complex**. IL-17F is *individually* weaker (~10–30× less potent) **but is expressed at far higher levels in lesional skin**, so its *total* contribution to keratinocyte activation is substantial.
- They also form the **IL-17A/F heterodimer**, and IL-17F synergises with TNF-α.
- Therefore blocking IL-17A alone (secukinumab, ixekizumab) leaves a **residual IL-17F-driven inflammatory tone** — a plausible explanation for incomplete clearance and secondary loss of response.

> **Analogy:** IL-17A is a loud trumpet; IL-17F is a quieter trumpet, but there are ten of them. Silencing only the loud one leaves the melody audible. Bimekizumab silences the whole section.

**Result:** the highest complete-clearance rates yet recorded — in **BE RADIANT**, **PASI 100 at week 16 in ~59–62%** versus ~49% for secukinumab, sustained to week 48; head-to-head superiority also over adalimumab (BE SURE) and ustekinumab (BE VIVID). Also effective in PsA, axial SpA and hidradenitis suppurativa.

**The safety trade-off you must state — mechanistic, not memorised:** IL-17 is essential for **mucosal antifungal immunity** (recall that patients with *IL17RA*/*IL17F* mutations or anti-IL-17 autoantibodies in APECED get chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis). Block IL-17A **and** F and you get more **oral candidiasis (~10–20%, dose- and duration-dependent)** than with IL-17A-only agents. It is usually mild, mucosal, and responds to topical/oral azoles — but it is a *predictable consequence of the mechanism*, which is exactly how an examiner wants you to frame it. Also, like all IL-17 blockers, **caution/avoid in inflammatory bowel disease.**

> ⚠️ **Correction:** the source reads *"**Normal** biologic that inhibits both IL-17A and IL-17F."* "Normal" is an OCR artefact for **"Novel."** Better still, write **"first-in-class dual IL-17A/F inhibitor."**

### 6.5 RORγt inhibitors — targeting the transcription factor itself

**The idea:** rather than mopping up cytokines after they are made, **switch off the gene programme that makes them.** **RORγt** (retinoic-acid-receptor-related orphan receptor gamma-t) is the **master transcription factor of Th17 lineage commitment** — the "master switch" downstream of IL-23/STAT3 that transcribes *IL17A*, *IL17F*, *IL23R* and *CCR6*.

**Mechanism:** RORγt is a **nuclear receptor** with a ligand-binding pocket (its natural ligands are cholesterol-biosynthesis intermediates like desmosterol). Small-molecule **inverse agonists** occupy this pocket, destabilise the co-activator helix (H12), and recruit co-repressors → **transcription of the entire Th17 cassette is silenced.**

> **Analogy:** blocking IL-17 is intercepting the letters at the post office. Blocking RORγt is shutting down the printing press. One intervention, many cytokines silenced.

**Advantages:** orally available, small, cheap to manufacture, **broad within-pathway coverage** (IL-17A, F, IL-22, GM-CSF all fall together), and formulatable as a **topical** — which is uniquely attractive, since a topical that switches off local Th17 transcription could treat plaques without systemic immunosuppression.

**Status:** genuinely emerging. Oral and topical candidates (e.g., **JTE-451**, and various topical inverse agonists) have shown **significant reductions in PASI and in lesional IL-17/IL-23 cytokine levels** in phase 1/2 studies — exactly as your source states. Development has been slowed by two issues worth knowing: **thymic/lymphoid toxicity in animals** (RORγt is also needed for thymocyte survival and lymphoid tissue organogenesis) and concern about **impaired antifungal and possibly antitumour immunity** if suppression is too complete. Selectivity and topical delivery are the ways around this.

> ⚠️ **Correction to your verification note:** the note states "JTE-451 (zasocitinib)." These are **two different drugs**: **JTE-451** is an oral **RORγt inverse agonist**; **zasocitinib (TAK-279)** is an oral **allosteric TYK2 inhibitor**. Do not conflate them.

### 6.6 ROCK2 inhibitors — **belumosudil (KD025)**

**What it is:** an oral selective inhibitor of **Rho-associated coiled-coil-containing protein kinase 2 (ROCK2)**. Licensed for **chronic GVHD**; studied in psoriasis in phase 2 (KD025-208).

**Mechanism — the Th17/Treg rheostat:** ROCK2 sits downstream of RhoA and does two relevant things:
- it promotes **STAT3 phosphorylation and IRF4 expression** → drives **Th17** differentiation and IL-17/IL-21 production;
- it restrains **STAT5** signalling and **FoxP3** expression → limits **regulatory T cells**.

Inhibit ROCK2 → **STAT3 down, STAT5/FoxP3 up** → the balance **tips from Th17 towards Treg**, with secondary falls in IL-17 and IL-23. It also reduces profibrotic and pro-angiogenic signalling (hence its GVHD indication).

> **Analogy:** most drugs cut the brake line of inflammation. ROCK2 inhibition works the **seesaw** — it simultaneously lifts the regulatory end while lowering the inflammatory end.

**Why this is conceptually important:** it is **immunomodulation rather than immunosuppression** — restoring tolerance instead of deleting a cytokine. That is the same philosophy as Treg cell therapy (§7), and it is where the field wants to go.

**Result:** phase 2 data showed roughly **a 50% reduction in PASI**, with corresponding falls in lesional **IL-17 and IL-23** — precisely the figures in your source. Efficacy is more modest than biologics, so its future may be in combination or in patients wanting an oral, non-immunosuppressive option.

## 7. Pillar 3b — Gene- and cell-based therapies (the third pillar of your opening line)

Your source's opening sentence promises these, so you must deliver on them.

- **Nucleic-acid therapeutics:** **siRNA and antisense oligonucleotides** silencing *IL17A*, *IL23A/IL23R*, *TNF*, *STAT3* or *NF-κB* mRNA — often packaged in the very **nanocarriers and dissolving microneedles** discussed in §4, so that gene silencing happens *only in the plaque*. This is the elegant convergence of the delivery and targeting stories.
- **CRISPR/Cas9 and gene editing:** experimental; conceptually most attractive in **monogenic phenocopies** such as **DITRA (*IL36RN*)**, **CARD14-associated papulosquamous eruption (CAMPS)**, and **DITRA-like AP1S3 disease** — where a single gene is at fault and correction could be curative.
- **Mesenchymal stem/stromal cell (MSC) therapy:** umbilical-cord or adipose-derived MSCs infused or injected act as "immunological thermostats" — secreting IDO, PGE₂, TGF-β and IL-10, shifting macrophages to M2 and expanding Tregs. Small trials report sustained PASI improvement with drug-free intervals.
- **Regulatory T-cell (Treg) therapy:** psoriatic Tregs are numerically present but **functionally defective and prone to converting into IL-17 producers.** Adoptive transfer of expanded, stabilised autologous Tregs aims at **restoring tolerance** — the nearest thing to a cure in concept.
- **Microbiome-directed therapy:** psoriasis shows gut dysbiosis (reduced *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, *Akkermansia*) and cutaneous dysbiosis. Probiotics, postbiotics and even faecal microbiota transplantation are under study via the **gut–skin axis** (short-chain fatty acids → Treg induction).
- **Vaccine-like and tolerogenic approaches:** anti-IL-23/IL-17 "cytokine vaccines" and tolerogenic dendritic cells — very early, but they aim at the holy grail: **long-term, drug-free remission.**

**Honest framing for the exam:** these are **investigational**, with small numbers, short follow-up, and unresolved questions of cost, safety (off-target editing, oncogenic risk) and regulation. Say so — examiners reward calibrated enthusiasm.

## 8. Advances you should not forget (the non-drug ones)

Because "management" ≠ "drugs":

- **Phototherapy:** **home-based NB-UVB** with remote monitoring (equal efficacy to hospital NB-UVB, far better adherence — the PLUTO/LITE trials), **targeted excimer laser (308 nm)** for scalp/palmoplantar and localised plaques, and interest in **blue light** for limited disease.
- **Difficult-site-specific strategies:** intralesional and MN-delivered triamcinolone for nails; foams and shampoos for scalp; **choice of biologic by domain** — e.g., **IL-17 and IL-23 blockers for nail and scalp disease; IL-17 blockers where axial arthritis coexists; IL-23 blockers where IBD coexists; avoid IL-17 in IBD; avoid TNF where demyelination.**

- **Access and equity:** **biosimilars** have cut biologic costs dramatically — for most of the world's patients, this is the single most consequential "recent advance."
- **Digital tools:** teledermatology, smartphone PASI/photographic scoring, AI-based severity assessment, and machine-learning models integrating multiomic data for drug selection.

## 9. Common misunderstandings — let me clear these up now

1. **"IL-17 blockers and IL-23 blockers are interchangeable."** No. IL-23 is *upstream* (maintains the pathogenic T cell); IL-17 is the *effector*. Clinical fallout: **IL-23 blockers have longer dosing intervals and more durable off-drug remission; IL-17 blockers act faster. IL-23 blockers help IBD; IL-17 blockers may precipitate or worsen it. IL-17 blockers cause candidiasis; IL-23 blockers rarely do.**
2. **"Deucravacitinib is a JAK inhibitor."** The single most common error here. It is a **selective allosteric TYK2 inhibitor** binding the *pseudokinase* domain — hence *no* boxed warning, *no* routine blood monitoring. Grouping it with tofacitinib misses the entire point of the drug.
3. **"IL-17F blockade adds nothing because IL-17F is weak."** Weak *per molecule*, but abundant in lesional skin, and it forms heterodimers with IL-17A. Potency × abundance = meaningful contribution.
4. **"Psoriatic plaques are barrier-defective so drugs get in easily."** Barrier *function* is impaired, but the thick parakeratotic scale is a physical obstacle and drug is lost with desquamation — hence the need for microneedles and nanocarriers.
5. **"Microneedles are just tiny injections."** No — most types (solid, coated, dissolving) deposit drug **without any liquid injection**, and they are engineered to stay **above the nerve/vessel plexus**, so they are essentially painless and bloodless.
6. **"Topical roflumilast = topical apremilast."** Same class (PDE4), very different exposure: roflumilast's topical formulation gives high skin and low systemic levels, avoiding apremilast's nausea, diarrhoea, weight loss and depression signal.
7. **"GPP is just severe plaque psoriasis."** Mechanistically distinct — **IL-36-driven innate neutrophilic autoinflammation**, sometimes monogenic (*IL36RN*/DITRA). This is why spesolimab works in days.
8. **"Biologics cure psoriasis."** They control it. Tissue-resident memory T cells persist in clinically normal skin ("molecular residual disease"), which is why relapse typically occurs at the *same anatomical sites* — and why disease-modification research (early intervention, Treg therapy) exists at all.
9. **"'Protonomics' and 'nonsterile'"** — as flagged: read **proteomics** and **non-steroidal**. Small words, big marks.

## 10. Drawbacks and limitations (do not skip — this is where balanced candidates score)

Your source lists four; here they are with the *reasoning* attached:

1. **Much of this is still investigational.** RORγt inhibitors, ROCK2 inhibitors, microneedles, nanocarriers, gene/cell therapies are at phase 1–2 or preclinical stage. Trial populations are selected (moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis, adults, few comorbidities), follow-up is short (16–52 weeks in a lifelong disease), and **real-world effectiveness is consistently lower than trial efficacy.**
2. **Primary and secondary failure still occur**, even with the newest agents — because of pathway heterogeneity, immunogenicity, obesity-related pharmacokinetics, and non-adherence.
3. **Cost.** Biologics and novel orals are expensive, often not reimbursed, and in resource-limited settings **methotrexate, phototherapy and topicals remain the backbone.** Biosimilars help but do not solve it.
4. **Adverse effects are real and mechanism-specific:** IL-17 blockers → candidiasis, IBD flare; IL-23 blockers → generally very clean but upper respiratory infection; TNF blockers → TB reactivation (hence mandatory latent TB and hepatitis B/C screening in India), demyelination, paradoxical psoriasis; JAK inhibitors → cytopenias, zoster, VTE, MACE/malignancy warnings; PDE4 → GI/neuropsychiatric (oral); RORγt → theoretical lymphoid/antifungal/antitumour concerns; MSC/gene therapy → oncogenic and off-target risks.
5. **Additional practical limitations:** requirement for **screening and monitoring infrastructure** (TB, viral serology, malignancy history), **need for parenteral administration** for most biologics, **pregnancy/lactation data gaps**, **paediatric licensing lags**, and the fact that **no therapy yet achieves durable drug-free remission.**

## 12. The core idea in one picture (your summary anchor)

Picture the psoriasis pathway as a **river running from a mountain spring down to a flooded village.**

- The **spring** is the stressed keratinocyte + LL-37 + dendritic cell.
- The **main channel** is **IL-23**, feeding the **Th17 reservoir**, whose **sluice gate** is the transcription factor **RORγt**, operated by the internal lever **TYK2**.
- The **water that floods the village** is **IL-17A and IL-17F** (with IL-22 and TNF), and the flooded village is the **hyperproliferating keratinocyte** — which then rains back onto the mountain, keeping the spring alive.
- A **separate tributary, IL-36**, floods a different village entirely: **generalised pustular psoriasis.**

Now place the advances on that map:

> **Dam the channel** → IL-23p19 blockers (mirikizumab; guselkumab/risankizumab/tildrakizumab) and the oral IL-23R peptide icotrokinra.
> **Jam the lever** → deucravacitinib (allosteric TYK2).
> **Close the sluice gate** → RORγt inverse agonists.
> **Tilt the whole landscape back towards drainage** → ROCK2 inhibitors (Th17 ↓, Treg ↑).
> **Sandbag the village** → bimekizumab (both IL-17A *and* F).
> **Block the separate tributary** → spesolimab (IL-36R) for GPP.
> **Sprinkle water only where needed** → roflumilast/tapinarof creams, microneedles, nanocarriers.
> **Decide which repair *this* village needs** → biomarkers and multiomics (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics).
> **Rebuild the riverbed itself** → gene editing, MSCs, Treg therapy.

And the one-sentence thesis to open and close your answer with:

> **"Psoriasis therapy is moving from broad immunosuppression to precision immunomodulation — narrower molecular targets (IL-23p19, IL-17A/F, IL-36R, TYK2, RORγt, ROCK2), smarter delivery (microneedles, nanocarriers, non-steroidal topicals), biomarker-guided personalisation (multiomics, treat-to-target, early intervention), and the emerging prospect of true disease modification through gene- and cell-based therapy — while cost, long-term safety and the absence of a cure remain the defining limitations."**

If you can draw that river, label the drugs on it, and then honestly state its limitations, you have not merely answered the question — you have demonstrated that you understand the disease.
