# Acquired Palmoplantar Keratodermas
### A Mini‑Lecture: "When Palms and Soles Turn to Stone — and the Cause Comes From Outside the Genes"

---

## PART 1 — THE BIG PICTURE: Why This Topic Exists at All

Let me start by orienting you, because "palmoplantar keratoderma" sounds like a single disease and it absolutely is not. It is a **morphological pattern** — a *reaction pattern* — in which the horny layer (stratum corneum) of the palms and soles becomes abnormally thick.

Think of it like the word **"jaundice."** Jaundice is not a diagnosis; it is a visible signal that something upstream — liver, red cells, bile ducts — has gone wrong. Palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK) is exactly the same kind of signal for acral skin. Your job as the clinician is not to say "this is a keratoderma" and stop. Your job is to say, **"this is a keratoderma — now, which of the ten upstream stories is telling it?"**

### Why do palms and soles behave so distinctively?

To understand PPK, you must first appreciate that **acral (volar) skin is a specialized tissue**, structurally unlike the rest of your body:

| Feature of palmoplantar skin | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Stratum corneum is **many times thicker** than elsewhere | Already at the edge of "hyperkeratosis" physiologically; small insults produce visible thickening |
| **No hair follicles, no sebaceous glands** | No sebum → no natural surface lipid film → prone to dryness and fissuring |
| **Highest density of eccrine sweat glands** in the body | Drugs and toxins excreted in sweat are delivered in high concentration *precisely here* |
| Expresses a **unique keratin, K9** (plus K6/K16 in stress) | Explains why many *hereditary* PPKs are K9/K1 mutations — and why acral skin has its own repair programme |
| Bears the body's **mechanical load** | Chronic pressure amplifies any tendency to hyperkeratosis |
| Thick **rete ridges / dermatoglyphics** | Anatomically anchored, so thickening looks "ridged," "pebbly," or "honeycombed" |

Hold onto that eccrine-gland point — it will explain drug-induced PPK *and* aquagenic keratoderma later. It is one of the most powerful unifying ideas in this whole topic.

### The two mechanical routes to any hyperkeratosis

Whatever the cause, thickened cornified layer arises by one of only two mechanisms (and often both):

1. **Proliferative hyperkeratosis** — the basal keratinocytes divide faster; more cells are pushed up than can be shed. *(Psoriasis, paraneoplastic growth-factor drive, arsenic.)*
2. **Retention hyperkeratosis** — production is normal but **desquamation fails**; corneocytes stay stuck together. *(Hypothyroidism, ichthyotic and nutritional states, estrogen deficiency.)*

Cause → effect → outcome: *a faster conveyor belt* or *a jammed exit door* → corneocyte accumulation → thick, inelastic, brittle horn → because that horn cannot flex over the arches and joints of the hand and foot, it **fissures** → fissures are painful and are portals for infection. That final clinical chain — thick → rigid → fissured → painful/infected — is the reason keratoderma matters to the patient, regardless of aetiology.

### Hereditary versus acquired: the dividing line

- **Hereditary PPKs** (Vörner, Unna–Thost, Mal de Meleda, Papillon–Lefèvre, Olmsted, Vohwinkel, punctate PPKs, etc.) are caused by germline mutations in keratins, connexins, cathepsin C, loricrin, TRPV3 and so on. They typically begin in infancy or childhood, are **symmetrical, stable or slowly progressive, lifelong**, and often have a **family history** and extracutaneous syndromic features.
- **Acquired PPKs** are the subject of today's lecture: they appear **later in life, often asymmetrically or abruptly, evolve, and — crucially — are potentially reversible** if you remove or treat the cause.

That word *reversible* is the entire clinical pay-off. Nobody's life changes when you label a hereditary keratoderma. But finding an underlying gastric carcinoma behind tripe palms, or hypothyroidism behind a soggy, thickened sole, or arsenic in a village well — that changes everything.

## PART 2 — THE DEFINITION, DISSECTED WORD BY WORD

> **Acquired palmoplantar keratodermas are non-hereditary, non-frictional hyperkeratoses of the palms and soles involving ≥50% of the surface, which may or may not be associated with clinical and histological inflammation.**

This is a deceptively dense sentence. Let me pull it apart, because each clause is a deliberate exclusion.

**"Non-hereditary"** — You must actively exclude genodermatoses. Take a three-generation family history, examine the parents' and siblings' hands if you can, and ask about age of onset. Beware: some inherited forms (e.g., punctate PPK) declare themselves only in the second or third decade, so "adult onset" alone does not prove "acquired."

**"Non-frictional"** — This clause excludes the everyday **callus/clavus** world: the guitarist's fingertips, the labourer's palm, the ill-fitting-shoe corn. Those are *physiological adaptive* hyperkeratoses, localized to the exact point of load. A true PPK is a *disease process*, not an adaptation. (Note the subtlety we will revisit: keratoderma climactericum *starts at pressure points*, yet is not a frictional keratoderma — friction is the **trigger that unmasks** a hormonally primed epidermis, not the cause.)

**"≥50% of the surface"** — This is an arbitrary but useful quantitative threshold that separates a *keratoderma* (a regional, confluent, diffuse disorder) from *keratotic papules/plaques* (focal, punctate, or discrete lesions). It forces you to distinguish "the whole sole is thick" from "there are six warts on the sole."

**"May or may not be associated with inflammation"** — This is the clause that tells you what the biopsy will and will not do. Some acquired PPKs are **purely hyperkeratotic** (climactericum: compact orthokeratosis with hypergranulosis, no inflammation). Others are **inflammatory dermatoses wearing a keratodermatous mask** (psoriasis: parakeratosis, Munro microabscesses, neutrophils; lichen planus: lichenoid interface dermatitis). So histopathology, in this field, is less about "confirming keratoderma" (which the eye already did) and more about **identifying the inflammatory signature that names the cause.**

### 1 ▪ KERATODERMA CLIMACTERICUM (Haxthausen's Disease)

#### What it is
A distinctive acquired keratoderma of the **menopausal woman**, described by Haxthausen, classically accompanied by **obesity** and **hypertension**. Remember the eponym: examiners love it, and the triad *menopause + obesity + hypertension* is the single best hook for recall.

#### Why it happens — the mechanism
The proposed driver is **hormonal dysregulation**, specifically the **oestrogen withdrawal of the climacteric**.

Here is the cause → effect → outcome chain in full:

> Keratinocytes and fibroblasts carry **oestrogen receptors**. Oestrogen supports epidermal proliferation kinetics, dermal collagen and glycosaminoglycan (and therefore water) content, barrier lipid synthesis, and normal corneocyte shedding. **When oestrogen falls at menopause**, the epidermis becomes thinner and drier, barrier lipids decline, and **desquamation slows** — a *retention* hyperkeratosis. The stratum corneum therefore accumulates and, being poorly hydrated, becomes **rigid rather than pliable**. Now superimpose **mechanical load**: at plantar pressure points, and amplified by **obesity** (greater force per unit area) and possibly by the microvascular/oedematous milieu of **hypertension**, that rigid horn cannot deform — so it splits. Splits = **fissures** = pain on walking.

This is the neatest illustration of the earlier caveat: friction is not the *cause*, but it decides the *map*. The hormone sets the stage; gravity and pressure choose where the curtain rises.

**Analogy:** imagine the sole's horny layer as leather on a shoe. Well-oiled leather flexes forever. Let it dry out and it stiffens, thickens at the crease points, and finally cracks exactly where it is asked to bend. Oestrogen is the oil.

#### Clinical features — build the picture in order
1. **Round to oval keratotic lesions of varying size**, appearing **first on plantar pressure points** (heel, metatarsal heads, ball of the foot).
2Those lesions then develop **erythema, hyperkeratosis and fissuring**, which makes **walking painful** — this is the patient's actual complaint, not "my skin is thick."
3. **Hyperkeratosis subsequently appears on the palms.** Note the sequence: soles lead, palms follow (pressure-first logic again).
4. **Pruritus ±** — itch may or may not be present. This is worth stating precisely in an exam: it is a variable, not a defining, feature.
5. **Accompanying menopausal symptoms** — and you must ask for them, because they corroborate the diagnosis: **hot flushes, genital (vulvovaginal) atrophy, osteoporosis, loss of libido.**

Teaching point: a keratoderma is an *examination* finding; the menopausal symptom cluster is a *history* finding. Climactericum is diagnosed when the two are laid side by side. Never diagnose it from the feet alone.

#### Histopathology (HPE)
- **Compact orthokeratotic hyperkeratosis** — "orthokeratotic" means the corneocytes have lost their nuclei properly (normal maturation), and "compact" means they are densely stacked rather than loose and basket-woven. Translation: *the factory is working correctly; the loading dock is blocked.* This is the histological fingerprint of **retention** hyperkeratosis.
- **Hypergranulosis** — a thickened granular layer, indicating enhanced/prolonged terminal differentiation feeding that compact horn.
- **Note what is absent:** no parakeratosis, no neutrophils, no lichenoid infiltrate. That "quiet" biopsy is itself the clue — it separates climactericum from psoriasis and lichen planus.

#### Management
- **Conjugated oestrogens** — replace what was withdrawn, and the keratoderma improves. This is a beautiful, almost experimental confirmation of the pathophysiology: correct the hormone, correct the skin.
- In practice you also give **keratolytics** (urea, salicylic acid, lactic acid), **emollients**, reduction of mechanical load (cushioned insoles, weight reduction), and topical or systemic retinoids in resistant cases — but for the examination, *the answer is conjugated oestrogens*, with a nod to modern menopausal-hormone-therapy risk counselling (breast cancer, thromboembolism) before you prescribe.

### 2 ▪ CHEMICALS (Toxic / Occupational)

Two entries: **arsenic** and **chloracnegens**. Arsenic is important enough that it is discussed again in detail later, but let me give you the whole story here.

#### 2a. Arsenical keratosis

**Source of exposure:** contaminated **groundwater / tube wells** (a genuine public-health problem across the Gangetic plain, Bangladesh and West Bengal), **occupational** exposure (smelting, pesticides, wood preservatives, glass manufacture), and historically **Fowler's solution** (potassium arsenite, once prescribed for psoriasis and asthma — an iatrogenic time bomb).

**Mechanism:** trivalent arsenic avidly binds **sulphydryl (–SH) groups**, poisoning thiol-dependent enzymes; it generates oxidative stress, causes DNA damage while simultaneously **inhibiting DNA repair**, alters methylation patterns, and disturbs keratinocyte differentiation and apoptosis. Net result: a proliferative, dysplasia-prone epidermis. Arsenic is a **complete carcinogen** in skin.

**Clinical evolution — follow the sequence:**
1. **Small, corn-like (punctate, horny) areas of hyperkeratosis** on palms and soles — like grains of sand or tiny corns embedded in the skin.
2. Lesions **enlarge, thicken and increase in number**.
3. They **spread to the dorsal surfaces of hands and feet**.
4. **Squamous cell carcinoma develops**, which may **ulcerate**.

**The full arsenical skin syndrome — look for the companions:**
- **"Rain-drop" pigmentation** — scattered guttate hypopigmented macules on a diffusely hyperpigmented background, classically on the trunk. Utterly characteristic.
- **Mees' lines** — transverse white bands across the nails (also seen in thallium poisoning).
- **Bowen's disease** (in situ SCC), including at non-sun-exposed sites; multiple SCCs; and less commonly **BCCs** (often superficial and multiple).
- **Latency of 10–20 years or more** between exposure and malignancy — so the patient may present decades after leaving the exposure, and you must *ask about the distant past*.
- **Internal malignancies:** screen for **bladder, lung and liver** cancers (and be aware of arsenic-associated peripheral vascular disease — "blackfoot disease").

**Confirmation:** arsenic levels in **hair and nails** reflect chronic exposure (blood/urine reflect recent exposure).

> **High-Yield Point:** Arsenical keratoses are **premalignant**. Every punctate palmar keratosis in a patient from an arsenic-endemic area deserves long-term surveillance, biopsy of any indurated or ulcerating lesion, and a systemic malignancy screen.

#### 2b. Chloracnegens

These are the halogenated aromatic hydrocarbons that cause **chloracne** — **dioxins (TCDD), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), polychlorinated dibenzofurans**, chlorophenols. They are classic agents of industrial accidents and deliberate poisonings.

**A necessary clarification** (and a fair exam-room question): chloracne itself is defined by **comedones and cysts**, especially in the malar/retroauricular region and axillae — *not* by palmoplantar hyperkeratosis. However, these compounds are potent **aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) agonists**, and AhR signalling is a master regulator of keratinocyte differentiation; hence chloracnegens can produce **generalized derangement of keratinization**, which may include palmoplantar hyperkeratosis, along with porphyria cutanea tarda-like changes, hyperpigmentation and hypertrichosis. So the term "chloracnegens" in a PPK list is **uncommon but defensible** — reproduce it as printed, and add the explanation if asked.

### 3 ▪ IDIOPATHIC

A **diagnosis of exclusion** — and it deserves respect rather than embarrassment. Declaring a PPK idiopathic is legitimate **only after** you have documented:
- no family history and no childhood onset (excluding hereditary),
- no occupational or chemical exposure,
- a full drug history,
- normal thyroid function,
- negative KOH and syphilis serology,
- nutritional adequacy,
- no features of a primary dermatosis on biopsy or elsewhere on the body,
- and an **age-appropriate malignancy screen**.

**And here is the crucial caveat:** "idiopathic" in acquired PPK is a *provisional* label. Paraneoplastic keratodermas can **precede** the detectable tumour by months. So an apparently idiopathic acquired PPK in an older patient, especially one that is progressive or has velvety/psoriasiform morphology, requires **planned re-evaluation**, not discharge.

### 4 ▪ MALNUTRITION

Skin is a high-turnover organ; it is therefore an early casualty of nutritional failure. Several deficiencies converge on acral hyperkeratosis:

| Deficiency | Mechanism | Clinical clue |
|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin A** | Retinoids are the master regulators of epithelial differentiation; deficiency → follicular and diffuse hyperkeratosis | **Phrynoderma** ("toad skin"), night blindness, xerophthalmia, Bitot's spots |
| **Niacin (pellagra)** | NAD/NADP depletion → impaired energy metabolism and photoprotection | Thickened, fissured, pigmented **"glove" and "boot"** hyperkeratosis (glove of Casal), Casal's necklace, diarrhoea, dementia |
| **Zinc** | Cofactor for >300 enzymes incl. differentiation and repair | Acral and periorificial psoriasiform/eczematous plaques, alopecia, diarrhoea |
| **Essential fatty acids** | Cannot synthesize ceramides/acylceramides → barrier failure → compensatory hyperkeratosis | Diffuse xerosis, ichthyosiform scaling |
| **Protein–energy malnutrition** | Substrate deficit for keratin synthesis and normal turnover | Flaky-paint dermatosis, oedema, hair changes |

**Mechanistic bottom line:** deficiency states impair *orderly* terminal differentiation and lipid-dependent desquamation → **retention hyperkeratosis** of the already-thick acral horn. These are eminently **reversible** with repletion — another reason never to skip a dietary and malabsorption history (alcoholism, bariatric surgery, coeliac disease, eating disorders, poverty).

### 5 ▪ DRUGS

The examinable list from the source:

| Drug | Note |
|---|---|
| **Glucan** | *As printed in the source — see the verification note below* |
| **Fluorouracil (5-FU)** | Antimetabolite; acral toxicity |
| **Lithium** | Psoriasiform/keratodermatous eruptions |

| **Venlafaxine** | SNRI; case-reported |
| **Verapamil** | Calcium-channel blocker; case-reported |
| **Quinacrine** | Antimalarial; keratodermatous and pigmentary change |

#### Why do drugs pick on palms and soles? — the mechanism you should be able to state
Return to our anatomy table. Palmoplantar skin has the **highest eccrine gland density in the body**. Many cytotoxics and their metabolites are **excreted in sweat**, where they are then **concentrated** — and the thick, poorly-perfused-at-the-surface acral horn holds them in prolonged contact with basal keratinocytes. Add high mechanical load and high baseline proliferative rate, and you have the perfect storm. This is precisely the mechanism behind the far more familiar **hand–foot syndrome (acral erythrodysaesthesia)** of cytarabine, capecitabine and doxorubicin, and the **hand–foot *skin* reaction** of multikinase inhibitors — and chronic, low-grade versions of the same insult manifest as **keratoderma** rather than acute erythema.

For **lithium**, the mechanism is different and worth contrasting: lithium inhibits inositol monophosphatase and adenylate cyclase, lowering intracellular cAMP; cAMP restrains keratinocyte proliferation, so its reduction **drives proliferation** and also enhances neutrophil chemotaxis. Hence lithium's signature is a **psoriasiform/pustular** acral eruption — a *proliferative*, inflammatory keratoderma, histologically quite unlike climactericum.

#### Modern additions you should mention for credit
- **Tegafur** (a 5-FU prodrug) — a classically reported cause of acquired PPK
- **Bleomycin**
- **Practolol** (a historical β-blocker withdrawn for its oculomucocutaneous syndrome)
- **MEK inhibitors** (trametinib, cobimetinib)
- **Multikinase / VEGFR inhibitors** — **sorafenib, sunitinib** (hyperkeratotic hand–foot skin reaction at pressure points, often with blistering and callus-like plaques)

> **Verification note on "Glucan":** the source lists *glucan* among drug causes. Standard references do not routinely list glucan as a cause of drug-induced PPK; the entry may be a transcription artefact (note that fluorouracil is listed separately, so it is not simply a garbled "5-FU"), though rare reports exist with beta-glucan preparations. **Reproduce it as printed if you are answering from this text, but be prepared to name the better-established agents above.**

**Clinical practicality:** drug-induced PPK is diagnosed by **temporal correlation** — onset after initiation, improvement on withdrawal, recurrence on rechallenge (rarely done deliberately). Therefore the drug history must be *complete*: prescription, over-the-counter, herbal, supplements, and chemotherapy from years past.

### 6 ▪ MALIGNANCY — THE PARANEOPLASTIC KERATODERMAS

This is the highest-stakes category in the whole classification. Get it right and you save a life.

#### The unifying mechanism
Tumours are metabolically indiscreet: they **secrete growth factors** — **EGF, TGF-α, IGF-1**, and others — into the circulation. These ligands bind receptors on keratinocytes (EGFR, IGF-1R), switching on proliferation programmes. Acral epidermis, with its high receptor density and high baseline turnover, responds first and most visibly. There may also be an immunological component: **cross-reactive antibodies** against tumour antigens that share epitopes with epidermal or basement-membrane structures (the favoured explanation for Bazex syndrome).

**Analogy:** the tumour is shouting "GROW!" into the bloodstream. Every tissue hears it, but the palms and soles are the loudspeakers — the tissue that most conspicuously converts that signal into visible thickening.

#### The specific entities

**(i) Acrokeratosis paraneoplastica — Bazex syndrome**
- **Morphology:** violaceous to erythematous, **psoriasiform keratoderma** of acral sites — **ears, nose, fingers, toes, and the nails** (nail dystrophy, subungual hyperkeratosis, onycholysis, sometimes total nail loss). The violaceous hue plus the involvement of *helices and nasal tip* is what separates it from psoriasis.
- **Classical association:** **squamous cell carcinoma of the upper aerodigestive tract** — pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, tongue, lung; also cervical lymph node metastases from an occult primary.
- **The hallmark:** the skin **parallels the tumour** — it improves when the malignancy is treated and flares with recurrence. That behaviour is both diagnostic and prognostic.
- **Do not confuse** with **Bazex–Dupré–Christol syndrome**, a completely different entity: an X-linked dominant **genodermatosis** with follicular atrophoderma, hypotrichosis, hypohidrosis and multiple basal cell carcinomas. Same surname, utterly different disease. Examiners test this.

**(ii) Tripe palms (acanthosis palmaris / pachydermatoglyphy)**
- **Morphology:** **velvety, rugose, ridged thickening of the palms**, with exaggerated dermatoglyphic ridges and prominent edges — said to resemble the **bovine foregut lining (tripe)**, hence the name. Run your fingers over it: the texture is the diagnosis.
- **Association:** up to **90% of cases have an underlying malignancy** — an extraordinarily high figure that makes tripe palms one of the most reliable cutaneous paraneoplastic markers in all of dermatology.
- **The two-rule discrimination you must memorise:**
  - **Tripe palms alone → lung carcinoma** (most common).
  - **Tripe palms + acanthosis nigricans → gastric carcinoma.**
- The source also notes association with **bladder, breast, stomach** and **genitourinary tract** malignancies, and that lesions can **progress to SCC**.

**(iii) Filiform palmoplantar keratoderma**
- Multiple **spiny, filiform (thread-like or "music-box spine") keratotic projections** on the palms and soles.
- Associated with **breast, colon and kidney** cancer.

**(iv) Mycosis fungoides (cutaneous T-cell lymphoma)**
- Here the mechanism is **different and important to distinguish**: this is not a remote paraneoplastic effect but **direct malignant infiltration** of acral skin. **Palmoplantar MF** (and hypertrophic/hyperkeratotic variants) can present as a hyperkeratotic, fissured, psoriasiform keratoderma that has been "treated as eczema" for years.
- **Practical lesson:** a recalcitrant, asymmetric, therapy-resistant hand-and-foot dermatosis deserves a **biopsy with immunophenotyping and T-cell receptor gene rearrangement studies**, not a fifth steroid cream.

#### Red flags that should trigger a malignancy work-up
Abrupt onset · rapid progression · older patient · asymmetry · velvety or violaceous morphology · nail destruction · associated acanthosis nigricans, Leser–Trélat sign, or weight loss · failure to respond to conventional therapy.

### 7 ▪ HYPOTHYROIDISM

Often under-taught, yet it is one of the most satisfying diagnoses because a cheap test and a cheap tablet cure it.

**Mechanism — the fullchain:**
> Thyroid hormone is a **primary regulator of epidermal cell turnover, sebaceous and sweat gland activity, and dermal glycosaminoglycan turnover**. In hypothyroidism: (a) **epidermal proliferation and desquamation slow** → corneocytes are retained → hyperkeratosis; (b) **eccrine and sebaceous secretion fall** → **anhidrosis and xerosis** → the horny layer becomes under-hydrated, stiff and scaly; (c) **hyaluronic acid and dermatan sulphate accumulate in the dermis** (the "mucinous derangement" referred to in the source) → doughy, non-pitting **myxoedematous** thickening that supports and exaggerates the surface change; (d) reduced cutaneous blood flow and **carotenaemia** (impaired conversion of carotene to retinol) add pallor and a **yellow-orange tint to the palms**.

**Clinical picture to look for alongside the keratoderma:** cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, bradycardia, hoarseness, delayed relaxation of ankle jerks, madarosis (loss of the **lateral third of the eyebrows** — Queen Anne's sign), diffuse alopecia, coarse dry hair, macroglossia, periorbital puffiness.

**Action:** measure **TSH and free T4** in every unexplained acquired PPK. It is a near-zero-cost test with a transformative yield. Levothyroxine reverses the skin change over weeks to months.

### 8 ▪ DERMATOSES — "The Great Imitators"

Here the keratoderma is not a mysterious systemic signal but simply **a common skin disease behaving unusually because it is on unusual skin**. When psoriasis lands on volar skin, it cannot make silvery scale on a thick horny layer — it makes a **keratoderma**.

| Dermatosis | How it produces PPK | Clues to catch it |
|---|---|---|
| **Psoriasis** (palmoplantar; also palmoplantar pustulosis) | Keratinocyte hyperproliferation + defective maturation → *proliferative* hyperkeratosis with parakeratosis | Sharply demarcated erythematous borders, nail pitting/onycholysis, scalp/elbow/knee/gluteal cleft plaques, family history; biopsy shows parakeratosis, neutrophils, Munro microabscesses/spongiform pustules of Kogoj |
| **Pityriasis rubra pilaris** | Abnormal keratinization with alternating ortho-/parakeratosis | **Orange-red waxy "sandal" or "keratodermic sandal"** appearance, islands of sparing on trunk, follicular "nutmeg-grater" papules, cephalocaudal spread |
| **Atopic dermatitis** | Barrier dysfunction (filaggrin), chronic scratching → **lichenification and hyperkeratosis** | Flexural involvement, personal/family atopy, raised IgE, itch-dominant history |
| **Chronic hand dermatitis** (incl. hyperkeratotic/tylotic eczema, contact dermatitis) | Repeated irritant/allergen injury → chronic inflammation → lichenified hyperkeratosis and fissuring | Occupational/irritant history; **patch testing** is mandatory; often asymmetric and hand-predominant |
| **Lichen planus** (palmoplantar variant) | Cytotoxic T-cell attack on basal keratinocytes → compensatory hyperkeratosis | Violaceous tint, oral/genital/nail involvement, Wickham's striae elsewhere; biopsy: lichenoid interface dermatitis, sawtoothing, Civatte bodies |
| **Lichen nitidus** | Focal lymphohistiocytic ("ball-in-claw") inflammation; palmar variant can be hyperkeratotic/pitted | Pinpoint shiny flat-topped papules elsewhere; characteristic histology |
| **Lupus erythematosus** (discoid, chilblain) | Interface dermatitis + follicular plugging → adherent hyperkeratosis | Photodistributed lesions, scarring alopecia, ANA, DIF (lupus band) |
| **Aquagenic** | *Listed here in the source, but best treated as a separate entity — see below* |

**Also worth knowing** (frequently asked as "what else?"): **keratoderma blennorrhagicum** of reactive arthritis, **secondary syphilis** (below), **hyperkeratotic scabies** (below), **Norwegian-pattern crusted lesions**, **palmoplantar lichen sclerosus** and **acquired ichthyoses**.

**Key teaching point:** in this category the keratoderma is a *distribution*, not a diagnosis. **Always examine the rest of the patient** — scalp, nails, mouth, genitals, flexures, trunk. The answer is almost never confined to the palms; the palms are just where the patient noticed it.

### 9 ▪ INFECTIONS

| Infection | Mechanism → clinical expression |
|---|---|
| **HPV** (human papillomavirus) | Viral E6/E7 proteins drive keratinocyte proliferation → **mosaic warts / confluent palmoplantar verrucae**; when extensive and coalescent, indistinguishable at a glance from a keratoderma. Look for **thrombosed capillaries ("black dots")** and **loss of dermatoglyphics** over the lesion |
| **Syphilis (secondary)** | Immune response to *T. pallidum* → papulosquamous eruption with a notorious **palmoplantar predilection**; copper-red, scaly, often with **Biett's collarette**; indurated conical lesions = **clavi syphilitici**. Ask about painless genital ulcer, generalized lymphadenopathy, mucous patches, condylomata lata. Always order **VDRL/RPR + TPHA** in an unexplained acral papulosquamous eruption — the "great imitator" earned its name here |
| **Crusted (formerly "Norwegian") scabies** | **Massive** *Sarcoptes scabiei* infestation (thousands to millions of mites) in the **immunocompromised, elderly, institutionalized, neurologically impaired or HIV-positive** host, in whom the normal itch–scratch clearing response fails → hyperkeratotic, crusted, warty plaques on palms, soles and periungual regions, **mimicking PPK**. Diagnosis: skin scraping teeming with mites. **Extremely contagious** — a public-health issue for wards and nursing homes. *(Preferred modern terminology is **crusted scabies**; the "Norwegian" eponym is now regarded as geographically misleading, though it is still widely printed.)* |
| **Leprosy** | **Autonomic denervation** of the affected limb → **anhidrosis** → severe xerosis → **retention hyperkeratosis and fissuring** of palms and soles. Mechanistically elegant: the keratoderma here is a *neuropathic* consequence, not an infiltrative one. Expect **anaesthesia** (test it!), thickened nerves, trophic ulcers, claw hand |
| **Trichophytosis (dermatophytosis)** | *T. rubrum* chronic infection → **"moccasin" (mocassin-type) tinea pedis** with diffuse scaly plantar hyperkeratosis, and the classic **"two feet–one hand" syndrome**. This is the **single most commonly missed cause of a "keratoderma"** — and a KOH mount costs almost nothing. *Never diagnose an acquired PPK without a KOH examination* |
| **Tuberculosis** | **Tuberculosis verrucosa cutis** — inoculation TB of the hands (classically in butchers, pathologists, farmers) producing verrucous hyperkeratotic plaques; also tuberculids. Diagnosis: histology with granulomas, AFB/culture, PCR, Mantoux |

**The overarching lesson of this category:** infections are the **most treatable and most frequently overlooked** causes. Three cheap tests — **KOH, skin scraping for mites, syphilis serology** — should be reflexive in any new acquired PPK.

### 10 ▪ AQUAGENIC PALMOPLANTAR KERATODERMA

A gem of a diagnosis, because the history alone makes it and because it can point to a serious systemic disease.

#### Nomenclature (know all of these — they are synonyms/variants of the same phenomenon)
- **Transient reactive papulotranslucent acrokeratoderma** (the formal descriptive name)
- **Aquagenic syringeal acrokeratoderma**
- **Aquagenic wrinkling of the palms and soles**

#### Associations
- **Inheritance / association: autosomal recessive or autosomal dominant** patterns reported — so despite sitting in the "acquired" classification, there is a clear **genetic substrate**.
- **Cystic fibrosis (CFTR mutations)** — the *principal and most important* association, including in **CF carriers (heterozygotes)**.
- **COX-2 inhibitors** (celecoxib, rofecoxib) — reported as triggers, but only at the level of **isolated case reports**. Mention it for completeness; do not let it overshadow CF.

#### Mechanism — why does water do this?
> Palmoplantar skin has the densest eccrine apparatus in the body. In **CFTR dysfunction**, chloride (and hence sodium) transport across the sweat duct epithelium is impaired, so **salt is not reabsorbed** from ductal sweat — it accumulates in the sweat and in the stratum corneum. Salt is **osmotically active**. When the hand is immersed, water is drawn **osmotically into the hypertonic corneum and dilated eccrine ostia**, which swell rapidly. Because the horny layer is anchored down at the sweat-duct openings and dermatoglyphic ridges, this swelling is uneven — producing **translucent white papules over the ostia with exaggerated wrinkling between them**. Add increased **aquaporin** expression and defective barrier lipids, and you get a hand that behaves like a **sponge with pinholes**.

**Analogy:** think of a salted sponge left in a puddle. The salt pulls water in; the sponge swells; wherever it is tacked down, it puckers. Take it out of the puddle and, as the water evaporates, everything returns to normal — which is exactly why the condition is **transient**.

#### Clinical features
- Within **minutes of water immersion** (2–5 minutes is typical), the palms develop **thickening with white, translucent, "pebbly" or cobblestoned papules** and exaggerated wrinkling — the **"hand-in-the-bucket sign."**
- Accompanied by **oedema and a burning pain or tightness** — this is not merely cosmetic; it is symptomatic.
- **Plantar involvement is rare** — palms dominate. (Ask yourself why: palms are immersed far more often, and plantar corneum is thicker and differently loaded.)
- **Females are significantly more prone** than males.
- **Fully reversible** within 10–60 minutes of drying.

#### Histopathology
- **Normal skin**, *or* **dilated eccrine ostia with a mildly hyperkeratotic stratum corneum**.
- Interpret this properly: a near-normal biopsy is not a failure of the test — it is **confirmation that the pathology is functional (osmotic/transport) rather than structural**. This is one of the few dermatoses where "the biopsy is normal" *supports* the diagnosis.

#### Treatment
- **20% aluminium chloride hexahydrate lotion**, combined with **urea cream** — the aluminium salt acts as an antiperspirant by occluding/precipitating within the eccrine duct, reducing the sweat (and therefore the salt) delivered to the corneum; the urea normalizes hydration and keratolysis.
- **Botulinum toxin (Botox) injections** — chemically denervate the eccrine glands by blocking acetylcholine release at the neuroglandular junction, abolishing sweat production. Highly effective, though painful to administer in the palm and requiring repetition every several months.
- Plus **avoidance of prolonged water exposure**, barrier gloves, and emollients.

> **Clinical pearl:** **aquagenic wrinkling of the palms is used as a clinical screening sign for cystic fibrosis and CF carriage.** In a child or young adult presenting with this sign — particularly with any history of recurrent chest infections, sinus disease, steatorrhoea, failure to thrive or male infertility — **order a sweat chloride test and CFTR genotyping.** A three-minute bedside water-immersion test can open the door to a life-changing diagnosis.

## PART 5 — A PRACTICAL CLINICAL ALGORITHM

This is how you convert the classification into bedside competence.

**Step 1 — History (the highest-yield instrument here)**
- Age at onset; family history; rate of progression; symmetry
- **Drug history** — everything, including chemotherapy years ago
- **Occupation and water source** (arsenic, chloracnegens, irritants)
- **Water-exposure relationship** — "does it change when you wash up?" (aquagenic)
- **Menopausal status and symptoms** (climactericum)
- **Thyroid and systemic symptoms**; weight loss, appetite, smoking, dysphagia, hoarseness, haemoptysis (paraneoplastic)
- Diet, alcohol, malabsorption, bariatric surgery
- Sexual history; immunosuppression; institutional residence

**Step 2 — Examination**
- Is it **diffuse, focal or punctate**? Is there a **transgredient** spread onto dorsa (arsenic, hereditary forms)?
- **Texture:** velvety and ridged (tripe palms) · spiny (filiform) · compact and fissured (climactericum) · pebbly on wetting (aquagenic) · crusted and warty (crusted scabies, TB, warts)
- **Colour:** violaceous (Bazex, lichen planus) · orange-red (PRP) · copper-red (syphilis) · yellow (hypothyroid carotenaemia)
- **Then examine everything else:** nails, scalp, mouth, genitals, flexures, trunk pigmentation, peripheral nerves, sensation, lymph nodes

**Step 3 — Investigations (tiered)**
- *Always cheap and first:* **KOH mount**, skin scraping for mites, **TSH/free T4**, **VDRL/RPR + TPHA**, CBC
- *Usually:* **skin biopsy** (to detect the inflammatory signature — psoriasiform, lichenoid, spongiotic, or the "quiet" compact orthokeratosis)
- *Directed:* patch testing (contact dermatitis); ANA (lupus); hair/nail arsenic; sweat chloride and CFTR genotype (aquagenic); nutritional panel (vitamin A, zinc, niacin status, albumin); immunophenotyping + TCR gene rearrangement (suspected MF)
- *Malignancy screen when red flags present:* chest imaging, upper GI endoscopy, ENT/laryngoscopic examination, urine cytology/cystoscopy, mammography, colonoscopy — **guided by the specific morphology** (tripe palms → chest and stomach; Bazex → upper aerodigestive tract; filiform → breast, colon, kidney)

**Step 4 — Treatment: two parallel tracks**
1. **Treat the cause** — oestrogen, levothyroxine, antifungal, penicillin, permethrin/ivermectin, nutrient repletion, withdraw the drug, remove the toxin exposure, **treat the tumour**. This track is what makes acquired PPK *reversible*.
2. **Treat the keratoderma symptomatically** — soaking then **keratolytics** (urea 20–40%, salicylic acid, lactic acid, propylene glycol), generous **emollients**, mechanical **paring/debridement**, cushioned footwear and load redistribution, topical retinoids or calcipotriol, and **systemic acitretin** for severe disabling disease. Manage **fissures** actively (they are the main source of pain and of secondary bacterial infection).

## PART 6 — COMMON MISUNDERSTANDINGS (Let Me Correct These Before You Meet Them in an Exam)

1. **"Adult onset means acquired."** No. Punctate PPK and several hereditary forms first appear in the second or third decade. Conversely, acquired forms (aquagenic, nutritional) can appear in childhood. **Age is a clue, not a criterion** — family history, evolution, and identification of a cause are what decide.

2. **"A callus is a keratoderma."** No. Calluses are **frictional and adaptive**, focal to the point of load. The definition explicitly excludes them by the words *non-frictional* and *≥50% of surface*.

3. **"Pressure-point lesions = friction."** In **keratoderma climactericum**, lesions begin at plantar pressure points — yet it is a hormonally driven disease. Pressure determines the *map*, not the *mechanism*.

4. **Tripe palms ≠ acanthosis nigricans.** They are distinct signs that frequently coexist. Tripe palms is **palmar, velvety and ridged**; acanthosis nigricans is **flexural, hyperpigmented and velvety**. The *combination* is what shifts your suspicion from **lung** to **gastric** carcinoma.

5. **Bazex syndrome ≠ Bazex–Dupré–Christol syndrome.** Paraneoplastic acrokeratosis versus an X-linked dominant genodermatosis with follicular atrophoderma and BCCs. Same name, opposite worlds.

6. **"The biopsy will diagnose the keratoderma."** It rarely names the *cause* by itself. In this field, histology is used to detect the **inflammatory pattern** (psoriasiform, lichenoid, spongiotic) or to *exclude* one (the quiet compact orthokeratosis of climactericum). The **history** is the more powerful instrument.

7. **A normal biopsy in aquagenic PPK is not a failed test** — it is diagnostically supportive, because the defect is functional/osmotic rather than structural.

8. **Do not over-weight the COX-2 inhibitor association of aquagenic PPK.** It is case-report level. **Cystic fibrosis/CFTR is the association that matters clinically.**

9. **"Norwegian scabies" is the correct term.** It is the *traditional* term; **crusted scabies** is now preferred, since the eponym is geographically misleading.

10. **"Aquagenic PPK is a dermatosis."** The source lists it both under dermatoses and as a standalone entity. Conceptually it is best classified as a **distinct entity** — an *exaggerated physiological response of eccrine ducts to water*, frequently with a genetic (CFTR) substrate — rather than a primary inflammatory dermatosis.

11. **Chloracne itself is comedonal, not keratodermatous.** Chloracnegens appear in the PPK list because they cause **generalized keratinization derangement** via AhR signalling — know the nuance.

12. **"Idiopathic means finished."** It means *provisional*. Re-examine, especially in an older patient with a progressive lesion, because paraneoplastic PPK can precede the detectable tumour.

## PART 8 — THE CORE IDEA IN ONE PICTURE

Picture the palm as a **thick, dry, tightly packed brick wall** — the most heavily armoured skin on the body, unusually rich in sweat ducts, unusually poor in oil, and unusually hard-working.

Now picture **ten different messengers** arriving at that wall:

- a **hormone that has stopped coming** (climacteric),
- a **toxin in the drinking water** (arsenic),
- a **drug dripping out of the sweat glands** (chemotherapy),
- a **growth-factor telegram from a hidden tumour** (tripe palms, Bazex),
- a **sluggish thyroid** that has slowed the demolition crew (hypothyroidism),
- a **missing vitamin** (malnutrition),
- an **inflammatory disease** that simply happened to arrive here (psoriasis, PRP, lichen planus),
- a **microbe or mite** setting up residence (tinea, syphilis, scabies, leprosy, HPV, TB),
- a **bucket of water** meeting salty, leaky ducts (aquagenic),
- and sometimes **no messenger you can find** (idiopathic).

Every one of these messengers delivers the same final instruction — **"add more bricks"** (proliferate faster) or **"stop removing bricks"** (retain) — and so every one of them produces the *same* visible wall: thick, rigid, fissured, painful.

**Therefore the skin can only ever tell you the answer once. The classification is your way of asking the question ten times.**

And the reason you must ask all ten is the single line that should stay with you from this entire lecture:

> **Unlike hereditary keratodermas, acquired palmoplantar keratodermas are reversible — and some of them are the first visible sign of a cancer that is still curable.**

That is why this topic sits in your syllabus. Not to test your memory of a ten-item list, but to make sure that when you see thick, fissured palms in a 58-year-old, your hand reaches for a KOH slide, a TSH form, and a chest X-ray — and not simply for a tube of emollient.
