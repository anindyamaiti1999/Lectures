# Confluent and Reticulate Papillomatosis (Gougerot–Carteaud Syndrome)
### A Mini-Lecture on the "Brown Net on the Chest"

---

## 1. The Big Picture: Why This Disease Exists in the Syllabus

Let me start by placing this condition on the map, because that's where most students get lost.

Dermatology has a large family of conditions called the **disorders of keratinization** — diseases in which the epidermis fails to perform its one great trick correctly. Normally, a basal keratinocyte divides, climbs upward over about four weeks, manufactures keratin and lipids, dissolves its nucleus, and dies gracefully into a flat brick of cornified protein. That orderly death is called **terminal differentiation**, and it produces the stratum corneum — our barrier against water loss and microbes.

When that program goes wrong, you get one of two visible consequences: **too much stubborn scale** (hyperkeratosis) or **too much heaped-up, velvety surface** (papillomatosis). Confluent and reticulate papillomatosis (CRP) is a disorder in which *both* occur, in a very specific place, in a very specific pattern, in a very specific age group.

So the one-sentence identity:

> **CRP is an uncommon disorder of epithelial keratinization in which small hyperkeratotic pigmented papules arise on the trunk, fuse into confluent plaques centrally, and break up into a net-like (reticulate) pattern at the edges.**

Why does it matter clinically? Three reasons:

1. **It is a great mimic.** It sits in the differential of acanthosis nigricans, tinea versicolor, and Darier disease — conditions with wildly different implications (insulin resistance, fungal infection, autosomal dominant genodermatosis). Getting it right saves the patient unnecessary workups.
2. **It is a beautiful lesson in host–microbe interaction.** CRP taught dermatology that finding an organism on a lesion does not mean the organism *caused* the lesion.
3. **It is a lesson in pharmacology.** It responds to antibiotics for reasons that have little to do with killing bacteria — a classic illustration of the **anti-inflammatory** use of tetracyclines.

## 2. Names and History: Decoding the French

The synonyms look intimidating but they are simply descriptive French, and if you translate them you have already memorized the clinical picture.

| Synonym | Literal translation | What it teaches you |
|---|---|---|
| **Papillomatose pigmentée innominée** | "Unnamed pigmented papillomatosis" | The original authors could not classify it — it was a *new*, nameless entity |
| **Papillomatose pigmentée confluente et réticulée** | "Confluent and reticulate pigmented papillomatosis" | The full clinical description in four words: pigmented + papillomatous + confluent + net-like |
| **Gougerot–Carteaud syndrome** | Eponym (French dermatologists, 1927) | The historical name still used in exams and journals |

CRP is regarded as a **specific form of cutaneous papillomatosis** — that is, it belongs to the broader group of diseases with heaped, warty, velvety epidermal surfaces, but it has earned its own name because of its distinctive site and pattern.

*Note the diacritics:* **pigmentée**, **confluente et réticulée**, **innominée**. Examiners in dermatology are notorious for French spellings; write them with accents.

---

## 3. Epidemiology: Who Walks Into Your Clinic

- **Age:** young adults, with peak onset around **19 years**. Think late teens to twenties.
- **Sex:** **M = F** (equal). This is worth emphasizing because older literature sometimes suggested a female or male predominance; current references state no meaningful difference.

**Why age matters mechanistically:** the peak onset coincides with **maximal sebaceous gland activity** after puberty. The trunk becomes oily, and the lipophilic yeast *Malassezia* flourishes. So the epidemiology and the pathophysiology are quietly telling the same story.

---

## 4. Pathophysiology: Four Threads That Braid Into One Idea

This is the part students memorize as a disconnected list. Let me instead show you how the four observations are four *arguments in a single debate*: **is CRP an infection, or is it an abnormal host reaction?**

### Thread 1 — *Malassezia*: the abnormal host reaction hypothesis

***Malassezia furfur*** (and other *Malassezia* species) is repeatedly isolated from affected skin, and **fungal stains show the yeasts sitting on the surface of the epidermis**. Since *Malassezia* is also lipophilic and loves the presternal and interscapular regions — exactly where CRP appears — this looked like an open-and-shut case of fungal disease.

**But here is the crucial catch:** *Malassezia* lives on essentially everyone's trunk, and **antifungal therapy alone is often insufficient** to clear CRP. If killing the organism doesn't cure the disease, the organism cannot be the whole story.

This forces a shift in thinking, and the shift is the key concept of the whole topic:

> CRP is best understood as an **abnormal host reaction *to* *Malassezia***, not an infection *by* *Malassezia*. The yeast is the trigger; the disease is the keratinocyte's exaggerated, disordered reply.

**Analogy:** Think of pollen and hay fever. Pollen is on everyone's face in spring; only some people mount an extravagant reaction. You don't call hay fever a "pollen infection," and you don't cure it by sterilizing the air. Similarly, *Malassezia* is the pollen of the chest; CRP is the exaggerated epidermal sneeze.

### Thread 2 — Actinomycetes: *Dietzia papillomatosis*

A named actinomycete, ***Dietzia papillomatosis*** (formerly classified within *Rhodococcus*), has been **isolated from CRP lesions** (Natarajan et al.). This organism gave a bacterial candidate for the trigger and neatly explained why antibiotics work.

Note the taxonomy detail: **it was reclassified out of *Rhodococcus* into the genus *Dietzia***, so older texts and newer texts use different names for the same bug. Many sources say only "actinomycete bacteria may play a role" — you score marks by naming the organism.

### Thread 3 — Metabolic and endocrine abnormalities

CRP has been associated with **thyroid dysfunction** and **Cushing disease**. The mechanistic chain is:

> Endocrine/metabolic derangement → altered hormonal signalling to keratinocytes → **abnormal keratinocyte differentiation and increased epidermal turnover** → hyperkeratosis + papillomatosis.

Both thyroid hormone and cortisol are potent regulators of epidermal proliferation and differentiation, so disturbing them disturbs the orderly keratinization program. This is also why **weight reduction** appears among the management measures: the metabolic milieu is a genuine cofactor, not a coincidence.

### Thread 4 — The antibiotic response is *anti-inflammatory*

CRP responds strikingly to antibiotics — but the drugs that work (minocycline, azithromycin) are precisely the antibiotics with the strongest **non-antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory** profiles. Minocycline:

- suppresses **neutrophil chemotaxis**
- inhibits **TNF-α**
- **downregulates matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)**

If cure depended on sterilizing the skin, any effective antibacterial would work. The fact that anti-inflammatory antibiotics specifically work supports an **inflammatory / host-response aetiology** rather than a simple infection.

### Braiding the threads

Putting all four together:

> A genetically or metabolically predisposed young adult, with oily seborrhoeic trunk skin, is colonized by *Malassezia* (and possibly *Dietzia papillomatosis*). Instead of ignoring these commensals, the epidermis mounts a low-grade inflammatory response. That inflammation drives **increased keratinocyte proliferation and abnormal terminal differentiation**, producing hyperkeratotic papillomatous papules with increased basal melanin. Drugs that damp the inflammation (minocycline) succeed; drugs that only kill the yeast often fail.

Hold on to that sentence — it explains the histology, the immunohistochemistry, and the entire treatment ladder.

## 5. Clinical Features: What You Actually See

### Location — and why *there*

The plaques are located **mainly on the trunk**, specifically:

- **Presternal** (front of chest, over the breastbone)
- **Interscapular** (upper back, between the shoulder blades)
- **Epigastric** (upper central abdomen)

These are the classic **seborrhoeic, sebum-rich, "high-friction, high-humidity" zones** — the same territory as pityriasis versicolor and seborrhoeic dermatitis. That is not a coincidence: it is the *Malassezia* habitat. **Site follows sebum follows yeast follows disease.**

### Morphology — the primary lesion

- **1–2 mm in diameter** (note: some printings read "1.2 mm"; the standard references describe papules of **1–2 mm**)
- **Pigmented** — brown to greyish
- **Hyperkeratotic** — rough, scaly, slightly warty to the finger

If you run your fingertip across early CRP, it feels like fine sandpaper made of tiny brown dots.

### Pattern — the diagnostic signature

This is the single most examinable feature, so let us build it slowly:

1. **Stage 1:** discrete 1–2 mm pigmented hyperkeratotic papules appear in the seborrhoeic trunk zones.
2. **Stage 2:** in the *centre* of the affected area, where papules are densest, they **coalesce** into **greyish-blue confluent plaques**. Confluence means the individual papules can no longer be told apart — the plaque looks solid.
3. **Stage 3:** at the *periphery* — spreading outward onto the **abdomen, face, limbs and pubic areas** — the papules are less dense, so instead of solid plaques you see them joined in interlacing strands with normal skin showing through the holes. That is **reticulation** — a net, mesh or fishnet pattern.

> **The hallmark is therefore a gradient: solid, confluent, greyish-blue plaque in the centre → net-like reticulate pattern as you travel to the edge.**

**Analogy:** Imagine raindrops on dry pavement. In the middle of the shower, drops merge into one continuous wet patch (confluence). At the edge of the shower, individual drops merge into squiggly rivulets around dry islands (reticulation). CRP is a rain-shower of papules centred on the sternum and the interscapular region.

**Symptoms:** The condition is essentially **asymptomatic or mildly itchy**; the complaint is cosmetic and psychosocial, not physical. This fact is the entire justification for the treatment philosophy we reach later.

## 6. Histopathology: Reading the Biopsy Mechanistically

The "defining histologic triad" of CRP:

**(1) Hyperkeratosis and papillomatosis**
- *Hyperkeratosis* = thickened stratum corneum: too many corneocytes accumulating because turnover is accelerated and shedding is disordered. This is the **scale** you feel.
- *Papillomatosis* = upward, finger-like undulation of the dermal papillae and overlying epidermis, so the surface becomes hilly rather than flat. This is the **velvety, warty texture** and the name of the disease.

**(2) Decrease in thickness or complete disappearance of the granular cell layer**
- The **granular layer (stratum granulosum)** is the assembly line where keratohyalin granules and lamellar bodies are made just before a keratinocyte dies into a corneocyte. Losing it means cells are being pushed into the cornified layer **too fast to complete their differentiation program** — the hallmark of hyperproliferative, abnormal keratinization (the same principle as parakeratosis in psoriasis).
- **High-yield discriminator:** in **acanthosis nigricans**, the granular layer is typically **preserved**; in **CRP it is thinned or absent.** When the clinical picture is ambiguous, this is one histologic pivot point.

**(3) Increased melanin in the basal cell layer (and above it)**
- Note carefully: the pigment comes from **more melanin**, not from more melanocytes and not from dermal melanin deposition. The inflammation/irritation stimulates melanin transfer into basal keratinocytes.
- **This explains the brown colour clinically** — and it explains why the colour is *epidermal* (brown-grey and surface-level) rather than the slate-blue of dermal pigment.

**(4) Dermis: at most a mild, non-specific inflammatory infiltrate**
- This is a **negative finding of great value.** CRP is an *epidermal* disease. If you saw dense lichenoid or eosinophil-rich inflammation, you should be thinking prurigo pigmentosa or a drug reaction instead. It also tells you CRP is **not a florid infection** — a true fungal or bacterial invasion would summon a much angrier dermal response.

## 7. Immunohistochemistry and Special Studies: Proof of the Mechanism

These four findings are not trivia — together they constitute the laboratory *proof* that CRP is a hyperproliferative disorder of differentiation.

| Marker | What it normally indicates | What its increase means in CRP |
|---|---|---|
| **Involucrin** | A protein of the cornified envelope, switched on during **terminal differentiation** in the upper spinous/granular layers | Increased and often expressed in the wrong (lower) layers → **premature, abnormal cornification** |
| **Keratin 16 (K16)** | An "activation" or **hyperproliferative keratin**, normally absent from quiet epidermis; appears in psoriasis, wound healing, friction | The epidermis is in an **activated, regenerative state** — as though perpetually healing a wound |
| **Ki-67** | Nuclear antigen present only in **cycling (dividing) cells**; the standard proliferation index | Increased → literal proof of **increased epidermal turnover** |
| **Odland bodies (lamellar bodies/granules)** | Lipid-filled organelles in granular keratinocytes that secrete the intercellular lipid "mortar" of the barrier | Increased **number** → the cell is frantically trying to build barrier lipids, consistent with an accelerated, stressed differentiation program |

**Fungal stains:** demonstrate ***Malassezia*** **yeasts on the surface of the epidermis.**

> **Read this correctly!** The yeasts are **on the surface**, not invading. Their presence **does not confirm causation.** This histologic detail is exactly consistent with the pathophysiologic conclusion: CRP is a host-response disorder, not a primary infection. Students routinely see "fungus on stain" and conclude "fungal disease" — resist that leap.

**Synthesis in one line:** ↑Ki-67 (more division) + ↑K16 (activated phenotype) + ↑involucrin and ↑Odland bodies (rushed, disordered differentiation) + lost granular layer = **a fast, sloppy epidermis** — which is precisely what "disorder of keratinization" means.

## 8. Differential Diagnosis: Eight Lookalikes and How to Cut Them Away

Because CRP is brown, scaly and truncal, almost every brown scaly truncal disease is a competitor. Learn the *discriminating feature* for each rather than the list.

| # | Condition | How it resembles CRP | How to separate it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Acanthosis nigricans (AN)** | Velvety brown thickening; can be truncal | AN favours **flexures — axillae, neck, groin**; associated with **insulin resistance/obesity/malignancy**; lesions are **soft and velvety, not papular-reticulate**; histologically **granular layer preserved** |
| 2 | **Prurigo pigmentosa** | Reticulate pigmented truncal eruption, upper back/chest | **Intensely pruritic**, evolves from **urticarial papules/vesicles** to net-like pigmentation; linked to **ketosis/dieting/diabetes**; dermal **inflammatory infiltrate present**; also responds to minocycline (a source of confusion) |
| 3 | **Macular amyloidosis** | Rippled/reticulate brown pigmentation on the back | **Non-palpable, non-keratotic** "rippled" macules; often from chronic friction; histology shows **amyloid in papillary dermis** (Congo red positive) |
| 4 | **Darier disease** | Greasy hyperkeratotic papules in seborrhoeic areas that coalesce | **Autosomal dominant** (*ATP2A2*), **nail V-nicks and longitudinal red/white streaks**, **palmar pits**, mucosal papules; histology shows **suprabasal acantholysis, dyskeratosis (corps ronds and grains)** |
| 5 | **Epidermal naevus** | Papillomatous, hyperpigmented plaque | **Present since birth/early childhood**, **unilateral and linear along Blaschko's lines**, static, not reticulate |
| 6 | **Plane warts (verruca plana)** | Multiple small flat-topped papules | **Skin-coloured/slightly pigmented flat papules**, face/dorsal hands, **linear koebnerization**, no confluent-reticulate gradient; HPV histology (koilocytes) |
| 7 | **Pityriasis versicolor** | Same *Malassezia*, same seborrhoeic sites, fine scale, dyschromia | **Macules/patches, not hyperkeratotic papules**; **fine furfuraceous scale** on stretching; **KOH shows "spaghetti and meatballs"** with abundant hyphae + spores; **responds fully to antifungals** — the therapeutic trial itself is discriminatory |
| 8 | **Dermatitis neglecta (terra firma-forme)** | Dirty brown adherent hyperpigmented plaques on trunk | **Wipes off with alcohol/vigorous scrubbing** — the single most satisfying bedside test in dermatology; caused by **inadequate cleansing**, not keratinization |

**A useful practical trick (supplementary, from the wider literature):** Davis and colleagues proposed diagnostic criteria for CRP — scaly brown reticulated/confluent macules and papules, involvement of trunk and neck, **negative fungal staining**, **failure to respond to antifungals**, and an **excellent response to minocycline**. Notice that two of the five criteria are *therapeutic* — the response to treatment is part of the diagnosis.

## 9. Course and Prognosis: The Chronicity That Shapes Everything

- CRP is a **chronic disease with remissions and exacerbations** — it waxes and wanes over years.
- Lesions that **respond to treatment may relapse when treatment is withdrawn.** So "cured" often means "suppressed."
- **Persistent remission** is most reliably achieved with **tetracyclines** (minocycline being the workhorse) — sometimes requiring repeated or maintenance courses.
- **Spontaneous improvement has been reported**, which is both reassuring to the patient and a caution to the clinician evaluating any new therapy (some "successes" may be natural remission).

**Reassuring counterpoint:** although chronic, CRP is **benign**. There is no risk of malignant transformation, no systemic organ involvement, no mortality. It never threatens the patient's health — only their appearance and confidence.

## 10. Management: Why "Only Cosmetic Treatment"

Here is the logic that governs therapy, stated as a chain:

> CRP is **chronic** + **asymptomatic** + **benign** → so the goal of treatment is **cosmetic/psychosocial improvement**, not cure or prevention of complications → therefore therapy should be **effective but low-risk**, and the intensity of therapy must be proportionate to how much the appearance bothers *this particular patient*.

### General and background measures

- **Weight reduction**, where relevant — addressing the **metabolic cofactors** (obesity, insulin resistance, associated endocrinopathy). Simultaneously reduces skin-fold friction and occlusion in the affected zones.
- **Screen for and treat metabolic/endocrine disease** where the history suggests it (thyroid dysfunction, Cushing disease) — since these drive the abnormal keratinocyte differentiation.
- **Counselling:** explain benignity, chronicity, likelihood of relapse, and that **spontaneous improvement can occur.** Setting this expectation prevents the patient from drifting through escalating, unnecessary treatments.

### First line

- **Oral antibiotics:** **minocycline** (the drug of choice) or **azithromycin**
- **Topical mupirocin ointment** (useful for limited disease or where systemic therapy is undesirable)

> **Key Point — Why minocycline works.**
> Minocycline is first-line not because it sterilizes the skin but because it is a potent **anti-inflammatory** agent. It:
> - **suppresses neutrophil chemotaxis** (fewer inflammatory cells recruited into the epidermis)
> - **inhibits TNF-α** (breaks the pro-inflammatory cytokine loop that drives keratinocyte activation and K16 expression)
> - **downregulates MMPs** (reduces tissue remodelling)
>
> Result: the inflammatory signal driving hyperproliferation is switched off, the epidermis returns toward a normal differentiation program, the granular layer re-forms, hyperkeratosis resolves, and the pigmentation fades.
>
> This is the same pleiotropic, "non-antibiotic" action of tetracyclines exploited in acne, rosacea, bullous pemphigoid and pyoderma gangrenosum.

*Commonly cited regimens in the wider literature (for orientation, not dogma):* minocycline ~100 mg daily for about 6 weeks; azithromycin 500 mg three times weekly for a few weeks. Remember minocycline's own risks — vestibular upset, photosensitivity, **blue-grey pigmentation** with prolonged use, drug-induced lupus and DRESS — which is another reason to keep courses finite in a purely cosmetic disease.

### Second line

- **Topical and systemic antifungals** — targeting the *Malassezia* trigger; helpful in some patients, but remember they are **often insufficient alone**, which is precisely the observation that reframed the pathophysiology.
- **Selenium sulfide shampoo** — used as a body wash; reduces *Malassezia* load on seborrhoeic skin, and is mildly keratolytic.
- **Topical retinoids** (e.g., tretinoin, tazarotene) — normalize keratinocyte differentiation, reduce hyperkeratosis and papillomatosis, and lighten the epidermal pigment by accelerating turnover. Mechanistically, they attack the disease at exactly the level the histology shows it: **differentiation**.
- **Vitamin D analogues** (calcipotriol) — bind the vitamin D receptor on keratinocytes to **inhibit proliferation and promote normal differentiation**; the same rationale as in psoriasis, another Ki-67/K16-high disease.

### Third line

- **Isotretinoin — in both high and low dose** regimens. Systemic retinoid therapy powerfully reprograms keratinization and reduces sebum (thereby shrinking the *Malassezia* niche). Reserved for third line because of teratogenicity and systemic toxicity — a serious drug for a cosmetic problem, justified only in extensive, refractory, psychologically distressing disease.

### The management ladder, at a glance

> **Minocycline / azithromycin (± topical mupirocin)** → **antifungals, selenium sulfide, topical retinoids, vitamin D analogues** → **isotretinoin (high or low dose)**, with **weight reduction and metabolic correction running alongside all rungs.**

Notice the internal logic of the ladder: **first** damp the inflammation (antibiotics), **then** reduce the trigger and correct differentiation topically (antifungals + retinoids/vitamin D), **finally** reprogram keratinization systemically (isotretinoin).

## 11. Common Misunderstandings — Let's Clear These Up

1. **"*Malassezia* is on the biopsy, so it's a fungal infection."** No. The yeast sits **on the surface**, lives on everyone's trunk, and antifungals alone frequently fail. It is a **trigger of an abnormal host response**, not an invader.
2. **"It responds to antibiotics, so it must be a bacterial infection."** No. The efficacy tracks with **anti-inflammatory potency**, not antibacterial spectrum. *Dietzia papillomatosis* may contribute, but the drug is working mainly as an immunomodulator.
3. **"CRP is just truncal acanthosis nigricans."** No. Different age peak, different distribution (**seborrhoeic trunk vs. flexures**), different primary lesion (**hyperkeratotic papules coalescing into a reticulate net vs. velvety flexural thickening**), different histology (**granular layer lost vs. preserved**), and different implications (AN mandates a search for insulin resistance or internal malignancy).
4. **"Confluent and reticulate are two separate diseases/patterns."** They are **two zones of the same lesion at the same time** — confluent centrally, reticulate peripherally. The gradient *is* the diagnosis.
5. **"The pigment is dermal."** No — the pigment is **increased melanin in the basal layer**, i.e. epidermal. That is why topical retinoids, which speed epidermal turnover, can lighten it.
6. **"It's benign and asymptomatic, so ignore it."** Benign ≠ unimportant. It strikes young adults on the chest and back at the age of maximum body-image sensitivity. Treatment is legitimate; it is simply *cosmetic in aim*, which dictates choosing safe therapies first.
7. **"If minocycline works, we're done."** Relapse on withdrawal is common; counsel the patient in advance and be prepared for repeat courses.
8. **"Prurigo pigmentosa is easy to distinguish."** It is the trickiest mimic: also truncal, also reticulate, also minocycline-responsive. The separators are **marked pruritus**, an **inflammatory papulovesicular prodrome**, a **dermal infiltrate on biopsy**, and a link to **ketosis/dieting**.

## 12. Summary Anchor: The Core Idea in One Picture

Picture a **19-year-old with an oily chest and back**. On the sternum and between the shoulder blades, *Malassezia* (and perhaps *Dietzia papillomatosis*) flourishes in the sebum. In this particular person — perhaps nudged by thyroid or cortisol imbalance or obesity — the epidermis does not tolerate these commensals quietly. It mounts a **low-grade inflammatory response**.

That inflammation pushes basal keratinocytes to **divide too fast (↑Ki-67), adopt an activated phenotype (↑K16), and differentiate prematurely and sloppily (↑involucrin, ↑Odland bodies, lost granular layer)**, while simultaneously **loading melanin into the basal layer**. The visible output is a shower of **1–2 mm brown hyperkeratotic papules**, which **fuse into greyish-blue confluent plaques at the centre** and fray into a **reticulate net at the periphery**.

Because the engine is **inflammation, not infection**, the cure is an **anti-inflammatory antibiotic — minocycline** — supported by antifungals to lower the trigger, retinoids and vitamin D analogues to normalize differentiation, and isotretinoin as the last resort. Because the disease is **chronic and benign**, the goal is **cosmetic control with the safest effective step**, and the patient is warned about **relapse on withdrawal**.

> **One line to carry out of this lecture:**
> **CRP = a hyperproliferative, abnormally differentiating epidermis reacting to *Malassezia* on seborrhoeic trunk skin → central confluent + peripheral reticulate brown hyperkeratotic papules → granular layer lost on biopsy → treated with anti-inflammatory minocycline.**
