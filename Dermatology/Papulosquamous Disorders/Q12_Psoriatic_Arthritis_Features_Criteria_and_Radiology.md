# Psoriatic Arthritis — A Mini-Lecture

*(Disorders of Keratinization | Rook's · Bolognia · Andrews')*

---

## Part 1 — The Big Picture: Why a Dermatology Chapter Talks About Joints

Let me start with the single most important framing sentence of this entire topic:

> **Psoriasis is not a skin disease. Psoriasis is a systemic inflammatory disease that happens to be most *visible* in the skin.**

Once you accept that, psoriatic arthritis (PsA, also called **psoriatic arthropathy**) stops being a random add-on and becomes the logical second act of the same story. The very cytokines that drive a scaly plaque on an elbow — **TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23** — can also drive inflammation in a synovial lining or at the point where the Achilles tendon grips the heel bone. Same fire, different room in the house.

Now, where does PsA sit in the taxonomy of arthritis? It belongs to the family called the **seronegative spondyloarthropathies**, alongside:

* Ankylosing spondylitis (AS)
* Reactive arthritis
* Enteropathic arthritis (IBD-associated)

**"Seronegative" means what, exactly?** It means the patient's blood does **not** contain the classic rheumatoid autoantibodies — **rheumatoid factor (RF)** and **anti-CCP antibodies**. This is not a trivial laboratory footnote; it is a *conceptual* statement. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an antibody-driven, synovium-centred, symmetric, erosive disease. The spondyloarthropathies are **entheseal-centred** (the tendon-to-bone anchor points), **asymmetric**, and characteristically produce *both* destruction **and** new bone formation. Hold onto that last phrase — "destruction *and* new bone" — because it will explain almost every X-ray finding later in this lecture.

**Why it matters clinically:** PsA affects **up to 40%** of patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis. Population-based studies put it nearer 20–30%, and dermatology-clinic cohorts (where disease is more severe) push it to the 40% figure quoted here; recent meta-analyses converge around ~30%. Whichever number you pick, the message is identical: **if you see ten psoriasis patients in a clinic, roughly three of them have or will develop joint disease.** And PsA is not benign — it erodes bone, deforms hands, and causes major **impairment of quality of life**, often disproportionate to the skin disease.

## Part 2 — Epidemiology: The Clock and the Order of Events

Three facts, each with a reason attached.

**1. Age of onset ≈ 40 years.**
This sits about 10–15 years after the typical peak of psoriasis onset — which is exactly what you'd expect if skin disease usually comes first.

**2. Psoriasis precedes joint disease in ~70% of cases.**
The skin leads, the joints follow, often by a median of about a decade. There are three orders of events, and it's worth naming them:

* **Skin first (~70%)** — the common pattern.
* **Simultaneous (~15%)**.
* **Joints first (~15%)** — the diagnostic nightmare, because you have an inflammatory arthritis with no skin clue. This is where *family history* and *nail changes* rescue you (and it is precisely why CASPAR awards points for both).

**Why this single fact is so important:** because the **dermatologist is usually the first doctor in the room.** You are seeing these patients years before a rheumatologist will. That makes screening for joint symptoms — morning stiffness, heel pain, swollen digits — a core dermatological responsibility, not an optional extra. Early PsA is erosive within the first two years; a missed diagnosis is permanent bone lost.

**3. Sex ratio is roughly equal overall — but the *pattern* differs by sex.**
**Axial (spinal) disease is commoner in males**; symmetric peripheral polyarthritis tends to be commoner in females. Compare this with RA (female predominance ~3:1) and AS (strong male predominance) — PsA sits in between, which again reflects its hybrid nature.

## Part 3 — Pathophysiology: Following the Fire

Let's build the mechanism as a cause → effect chain, because that is how you'll remember it.

### Step 1 — The trigger

**Genetics loads the gun:**

* **HLA-C\*06:02** — strongly associated with **cutaneous psoriasis** (especially early-onset) and with **peripheral** joint disease.
* **HLA-B27** — associated with **axial** disease (spine and sacroiliac joints), just as in ankylosing spondylitis.

Notice how elegantly this maps onto phenotype: the "skin gene" tracks with peripheral arthritis, the "spine gene" tracks with axial arthritis. Genotype is quietly predicting which of the five clinical subtypes your patient will develop.

**Environment pulls the trigger:**

* **Mechanical trauma** — the "**deep Koebner phenomenon**." You already know the cutaneous Koebner effect: scratch psoriatic skin and a plaque appears along the scratch. The same principle applies at depth. Repetitive mechanical stress at an enthesis creates micro-injury, which recruits inflammation, which becomes enthesitis. This is a beautiful unifying idea — it explains why the Achilles tendon and plantar fascia (the two most mechanically abused entheses in the body) are the favourite sites.
* **Smoking** and **alcohol** — both promote onset and worsen progression, and both reduce treatment response.

### Step 2 — The site of initiation: the enthesis and synovium

An **enthesis** is the anatomical zone where a tendon or ligament inserts into bone. Think of it as a **rivet** joining soft rope to hard steel — a region of enormous mechanical stress that also happens to be richly supplied with innate immune cells. In spondyloarthropathies, **the enthesis, not the synovium, is the primary lesion.** The synovitis is, in a sense, collateral damage spreading inward from the rivet.

### Step 3 — Immune amplification

* **Innate immunity** fires first: resident macrophages, mast cells, innate lymphoid cells, γδ T cells and neutrophils at the stressed enthesis release **IL-23**.
* **IL-23** drives **Th17 cells** (adaptive immunity) to produce **IL-17**.
* **IL-17 + TNF-α** together then do the damage: they activate synovial fibroblasts, recruit neutrophils, and — critically — dysregulate bone.

### Step 4 — Angiogenesis

Inflamed synovium demands oxygen and delivers more inflammatory cells, so **new blood vessels sprout** (VEGF-driven). Under the arthroscope, PsA synovium shows characteristically **tortuous, bushy, elongated vessels** — quite different from the straight, branching vessels of RA. This is the joint's version of the dilated, tortuous capillaries in a psoriatic dermal papilla (the same vessels that give you the Auspitz sign). *Same disease, same vascular signature, different tissue.*

### Step 5 — The bone paradox (this is the key to the X-rays)

Here is where PsA diverges most sharply from RA. In PsA, the cytokine milieu simultaneously:

* **Activates osteoclasts** (via RANKL, driven by TNF-α and IL-17) → **erosion and osteolysis**
* **Activates osteoblasts and periosteal bone formation** (via IL-17/BMP/Wnt pathways at the enthesis) → **new bone, periostitis, syndesmophytes, ankylosis**

> **Analogy:** RA is a **demolition crew** — it tears the building down and leaves a clean hole. PsA is a **demolition crew working alongside a drunk construction crew** — parts of the building are ripped away while lumpy, misplaced concrete is poured onto the outside walls. That is why a PsA X-ray shows *erosions with fluffy, whiskery new bone at their margins*, whereas an RA X-ray shows *crisp, clean-edged erosions with juxta-articular osteopenia and nothing being built.*

Burn that analogy in. Every radiographic sign below is either the demolition crew, the construction crew, or both working on the same bone.

## Part 4 — Clinical Features: The Five Faces of PsA (Moll and Wright, 1973)

PsA is **clinically heterogeneous** — it does not have one look. Moll and Wright described five patterns, and although CASPAR has replaced them for formal *classification*, these five remain the language we use to *describe* a patient.

| # | Subtype | What it is | Why it matters / how to spot it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Asymmetric oligoarthritis** | **Fewer than 5 joints**, scattered asymmetrically — e.g. right knee, left 3rd PIP, left ankle | **The most common pattern (~70% at presentation).** The asymmetry and "random" distribution is the giveaway — RA is a mirror-image disease, PsA is not |
| **2** | **Symmetric polyarthritis** | ≥5 joints, symmetric — looks *exactly* like RA | The great mimic. Distinguished by **RF and anti-CCP negativity**, plus the presence of enthesitis, dactylitis, DIP disease, nail change, or skin psoriasis |
| **3** | **Distal interphalangeal (DIP) arthritis** | Involves the DIP joints of fingers and toes | **The most characteristic pattern of PsA.** RA essentially *spares* the DIPs. Almost always accompanied by **nail dystrophy of the same digit** — because the nail matrix and the DIP joint are separated by only ~1 mm and share the extensor tendon insertion (a shared enthesis). Nail disease and DIP arthritis are literally the same lesion seen from two angles |
| **4** | **Arthritis mutilans** | **Rare (<5%), severe, grossly deforming.** Massive osteolysis dissolves phalanges, producing **"telescoping" fingers** — the digit can be pulled out and pushed in like an opera glass, with redundant folded skin | The extreme end of the demolition crew. Correlates with long disease duration, HLA-B27, and delayed treatment. This is what you are trying to prevent by diagnosing early |
| **5** | **Axial disease** | **Spine and sacroiliac joints** — inflammatory back pain, reduced spinal mobility, sacroiliitis | More common in **males** and in **HLA-B27**-positive patients. Often **asymmetric or unilateral** sacroiliitis, and frequently *less symptomatic* than AS — patients may have radiographic sacroiliitis with modest pain |

**A crucial caveat students always miss:** these subtypes are **not fixed compartments.** A patient who presents with asymmetric oligoarthritis may evolve into symmetric polyarthritis over years, or acquire axial disease. Also, **patterns overlap** — a single patient can simultaneously have DIP arthritis, dactylitis, enthesitis and sacroiliitis. Treat the classification as a descriptive vocabulary, not a set of boxes.

## Part 5 — Symptoms: The Two Hallmarks and the Two Clues

### Hallmark 1 — Enthesitis

**What it is:** inflammation at the insertion of a tendon, ligament, or joint capsule into bone.

**Where:** most commonly the **Achilles tendon insertion** (posterior heel pain) and the **plantar fascia** origin at the calcaneum (inferior heel pain). Also the patellar tendon, epicondyles, iliac crest, and costochondral junctions.

**Why it matters:** Enthesitis is **virtually pathognomonic of the spondyloarthropathies** and is **uncommon in RA**. A psoriasis patient complaining of heel pain that is worst in the morning and eases with movement is telling you they have PsA, even if every joint looks normal. It is also the most under-recognised feature in dermatology clinics — the patient often blames their shoes.

### Hallmark 2 — Dactylitis ("sausage digit")

**What it is:** diffuse, uniform, sausage-like swelling of an **entire** finger or toe.

**The mechanism — and this is the examination point:** dactylitis is **not** joint swelling. It is the *simultaneous* inflammation of the flexor tendon sheath (tenosynovitis), the joints, the entheses and the soft tissue of the whole digit. That is why the swelling is **uniform along the whole length** of the digit, obliterating the normal waisting at the joints.

> **Analogy:** RA swelling looks like **knots on a rope** — fusiform swelling *at* the joints with normal segments between. Dactylitis looks like **a sausage** — the whole digit inflated evenly, no knots, no waist.

Dactylitis occurs in **~40–50%** of PsA patients, is uncommon in RA, and is a marker of **more erosive disease** in that digit. It is so specific that CASPAR awards it a point.

### Clue 1 — Early morning stiffness

**Duration matters.** Stiffness lasting **>30 minutes** (often 45–60 min) that **improves with movement** is *inflammatory*. Osteoarthritis produces brief stiffness (<15 min) that **worsens** with use. This single question — "how long does the stiffness last, and does moving help or hurt?" — separates inflammatory from mechanical joint disease at the bedside, free of charge.

### Clue 2 — Joint swelling

Warm, boggy, soft-tissue swelling with effusion — the visible sign of synovitis. Contrast with the **hard, bony** enlargement of OA (Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes).

*(And never forget the nails: nail pitting, onycholysis, subungual hyperkeratosis and oil-drop discolouration occur in ~80–90% of PsA patients versus ~40% of psoriasis patients without arthritis. **Nail disease is the single best cutaneous predictor of PsA** — which is why CASPAR scores it.)*

## Part 6 — Differential Diagnosis: Five Rivals and How to Beat Each

| Mimic | How it resembles PsA | How you separate them |
|---|---|---|
| **Early rheumatoid arthritis** | Symmetric polyarthritis, morning stiffness, small-joint swelling | **RF and anti-CCP positive** (RF+ in ~80% of RA); **symmetric**; **spares DIPs**; **no enthesitis, no dactylitis**; erosions are clean with **no new bone formation**; juxta-articular osteopenia |
| **Ankylosing spondylitis** | Axial pain, sacroiliitis, enthesitis, HLA-B27, syndesmophytes | AS is **predominantly axial**, **bilateral symmetric sacroiliitis**, **thin marginal symmetric syndesmophytes**; near-universal HLA-B27 (>90% vs ~40% in axial PsA); **no skin/nail psoriasis** |
| **Gout** | Acutely hot, red, swollen digit — can mimic dactylitis | Typically **monoarticular** and explosively acute (peaks in 12–24 h); **tophi**; **negatively birefringent needle-shaped urate crystals** on polarised microscopy; raised uric acid. ⚠️ **Trap:** psoriasis has high epidermal turnover → increased purine load → **hyperuricaemia is common in psoriasis**, so a raised urate does *not* diagnose gout. Aspirate the joint |
| **Osteoarthritis (esp. erosive/inflammatory hand OA)** | DIP involvement, deformity, X-ray erosions | **Heberden's (DIP) and Bouchard's (PIP) nodes** — *hard, bony*; **no true inflammation**, normal CRP/ESR; **osteophytes** (which CASPAR explicitly excludes); **no enthesitis or dactylitis**; stiffness <15 min |
| **Fibromyalgia** | Widespread pain, stiffness, fatigue | **No objective synovitis**, **no erosions**, **normal CRP/ESR**; characteristic tender points; non-restorative sleep. ⚠️ Beware: fibromyalgia frequently **coexists** with PsA and inflates disease-activity scores — don't escalate biologics for fibromyalgia pain |

## Part 7 — Investigations: What to Order and What You Expect to Find

**1. Routine psoriasis workup** — as detailed under the management of psoriasis *(cross-referenced in the source as "Q.4" — this is a pointer to the baseline psoriasis investigation panel, not a numbering error)*. This covers baseline haematology, liver and renal function, metabolic screening, and infection screening — essential before you start MTX or biologics.

**2. Laboratory tests**

* **CRP and ESR** — inflammatory markers. **Important caveat:** they are **normal in up to 50%** of PsA patients, especially oligoarticular disease. A normal CRP does **not** exclude PsA. (In RA they are more consistently raised.)
* **Uric acid** — often raised in psoriasis from epidermal turnover; used to assess gout as a differential and as a comorbidity/cardiovascular marker.
* **RF and anti-CCP** — expected **negative**. Their purpose is *exclusion of RA*, and a negative RF actively earns a CASPAR point. (~5–10% of PsA patients can have a low-titre RF, and the general population has background positivity, so a weak positive doesn't overturn a clinically obvious PsA.)

**3. Plain radiographs** — of the affected hands, feet, pelvis (SI joints) and spine. Cheap, widely available, and the source of all the classic signs below. **Limitation:** X-rays show *structural damage that has already occurred* — they are a record of the past, not a detector of the present.

**4. Ultrasound or MRI — for enthesitis**
This is the modern addition and worth understanding *why*:

* **Ultrasound** shows enthesis thickening, hypoechogenicity, power-Doppler signal (active neovascularisation), bursitis and tendon sheath effusion — at the bedside, in real time, with no radiation.
* **MRI** shows **bone marrow oedema** — the earliest detectable inflammatory change, visible **months to years before erosions appear on X-ray**. MRI of the SI joints is how "non-radiographic axial disease" is diagnosed.

> **The principle:** X-ray answers "how much damage has been done?" USG/MRI answer "is there inflammation *right now*?" You need both — one to stage, one to treat.

## Part 8 — Radiographic Findings: Reading the Demolition-and-Construction Site

Now we cash in the analogy from Part 3. Every sign below is either **demolition (osteolysis)**, **construction (new bone)**, or the uniquely psoriatic combination of both in the same lesion.

### A. Peripheral joints

**1. Dactylitis — soft-tissue swelling of the entire digit.**
Radiographically you see a uniformly widened soft-tissue shadow along the whole digit, not localised periarticular swelling. *(Demolition hasn't started yet — this is the soft-tissue phase.)*

**2. Deformity of the hands and fingers.**
The end result of asymmetric destruction plus ankylosis — a hand where different digits are deformed in different directions, unlike the uniform ulnar deviation of RA.

**3. Destructive changes — subchondral cysts and erosions with distinctive "spike-like" or "fluffy" proliferative alterations at joint surface margins and the phalangeal tuft.**
This is **the** signature of PsA. Look carefully at the *edge* of the erosion: in RA the edge is sharp and clean; in PsA, whiskery, fluffy, spiculated new bone grows out from the erosion margin. Demolition and construction, side by side, in the same millimetre of bone.
Erosion of the **phalangeal tuft** (the distal terminal expansion of the distal phalanx) produces **acro-osteolysis** — a pointed, whittled fingertip, often with nail disease overlying it.

**4. Osteolytic lesions of the phalanx → the "pencil-in-cup" deformity.**
**Learn to describe this, not just name it.** Progressive osteolysis whittles the **head of the proximal bone** down to a tapered point — the **pencil**. Simultaneously, erosion of the **base of the distal bone** hollows and widens it into a concave saucer — the **cup**. The result: a sharpened pencil sitting inside a cup, with a visible gap between them.
It is **pathognomonic of PsA** and is the radiographic hallmark of **arthritis mutilans**. Clinically, this is the joint that lets a finger "telescope."

**5. Bony ankylosis.**
Complete bony fusion across a joint — the construction crew wins outright. **This is a critical distinction: PsA ankyloses joints; RA almost never does** (RA gives you fibrous ankylosis and instability, not bony fusion). Bony ankylosis of a DIP or PIP joint on a hand film is a strong pointer to PsA.

**6. Periostitis along the metaphyses and shafts of digits.**
Fluffy, irregular new bone laid down along the *shaft* — **away from the joint**. This is pure enthesis-driven construction. RA does not do this. Seeing periostitis on a hand film should make you think spondyloarthropathy immediately.

**7. Periosteal and endosteal new bone formation → increased bone density → the "ivory phalanx."**
When new bone is laid down both **outside** (periosteal) and **inside** (endosteal) a phalanx, the whole bone becomes **diffusely, uniformly sclerotic and dense white** on the radiograph — like a piece of carved ivory. Classically affects the **distal phalanx of the great toe**. Characteristic of PsA, though uncommon.

**8. Juxta-articular and gross osteoporosis.**
Localised osteopenia adjacent to inflamed joints (from local cytokine-driven bone loss and disuse), and generalised osteoporosis in long-standing disease (chronic inflammation, immobility, corticosteroid exposure).

> **One-line summary of the peripheral hand film:** *asymmetric, DIP-predominant, erosions **with** fluffy marginal new bone, periostitis away from the joint, pencil-in-cup, bony ankylosis, ivory phalanx.* If you see erosions **plus** new bone, you are looking at PsA.

### B. Spine

**1. Psoriatic syndesmophytes — asymmetric, linear/curvilinear, fluffy.**
A **syndesmophyte** is ossification bridging adjacent vertebrae. This is the single most examined comparison in spondyloarthropathy radiology:

| | **Psoriatic arthritis** | **Ankylosing spondylitis** |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetry | **Asymmetric**, patchy, skip lesions | **Symmetric**, continuous |
| Morphology | **Bulky, coarse, fluffy**, curvilinear | **Thin, delicate, vertical** |
| Attachment | **Non-marginal** — arises from the vertebral body *away* from the corner | **Marginal** — corner-to-corner |
| End result | Irregular, patchy bridging | Smooth **"bamboo spine"** |

*(Note: the coarse non-marginal syndesmophyte of PsA overlaps morphologically with DISH and reactive arthritis — context and clinical setting decide.)*

**2. Squaring of the vertebral bodies.**
The normally slightly concave anterior vertebral border becomes straight or flat. **Mechanism:** inflammation at the enthesis of the outer annulus fibrosus at the vertebral corner → erosion of the corner ("Romanus lesion," seen as a shiny corner) → reactive new bone fills the concavity → the vertebra loses its waist and looks square. Demolition followed by construction, again.

**3. Cervical spine involvement:** intervertebral/facet joint erosions, syndesmophytes, **ligamentous calcification** (including the anterior atlantoaxial and paraspinal ligaments), and — **rarely but dangerously — atlantoaxial subluxation.**

> **Clinical alarm:** atlantoaxial subluxation means the C1–C2 articulation has lost its stabilising ligamentous restraint, allowing the odontoid peg to encroach on the cervical cord. Risks include myelopathy, sudden neurological catastrophe on neck flexion, and disaster during **intubation for general anaesthesia**. It is commoner in severe RA but genuinely occurs in PsA, and it is an indication for surgical stabilisation. **Practical rule: any PsA or RA patient with neck pain, occipital headache, or new limb paraesthesiae needs cervical flexion–extension imaging before elective surgery.**

### C. Sacroiliac joints

Sacroiliitis in PsA is often **asymmetric or unilateral** (in AS it is characteristically **bilateral and symmetric**) and is frequently **clinically silent** relative to its radiographic severity. The findings follow the same demolition-then-construction sequence:

1. **Ill-defined cortical bone layer** — the earliest sign: the crisp white cortical line along the joint blurs as subchondral inflammation begins.
2. **Shallow erosions** — the cortex breaks down, producing a ragged, "postage-stamp" joint margin and apparent pseudo-widening of the joint space.
3. **Subchondral osteosclerosis** — reactive dense bone beneath the erosions; the body's attempt at repair.
4. **Bony proliferations** — new bone bridging the joint, progressing eventually to partial or complete ankylosis (and with it, loss of the joint space).

### D. Enthesis — enthesopathy of the Achilles tendon, plantar aponeurosis and pelvic attachments (ischial tuberosity, iliac crest, greater trochanter)

1. **Mineralised scars** — calcification *within* the substance of the chronically inflamed tendon or fascia.
2. **Bony proliferations** — **enthesophytes**: the fluffy, irregular, ill-defined calcaneal spurs of PsA. ⚠️ **Distinguish these from the smooth, well-defined, degenerative spurs of mechanical plantar fasciitis** — inflammatory enthesophytes are **fluffy and poorly marginated**; degenerative ones are **smooth and sharply corticated.**
3. **Erosions** — at the posterosuperior calcaneum where the Achilles inserts, and at the plantar surface.
4. **Cysts** — subcortical cystic change at the insertion site.

> **Unifying idea for the whole radiology section:** every single one of these findings — hand, spine, SI joint, heel — is the same pathology in a different postcode: **entheseal inflammation producing simultaneous osteolysis and new bone formation.** If you understand that one sentence you never have to memorise the list.

## Part 9 — Diagnosis: The CASPAR Criteria

**CASPAR** = **C**lass**i**fication criteria for **P**soriatic **AR**thritis, from the CASPAR Study Group (Taylor et al., *Arthritis Rheum* 2006). It has replaced Moll and Wright for formal classification because it is both sensitive and extraordinarily specific: **~91% sensitivity, ~99% specificity.**

### ⚠️ The prerequisite — do not skip this

> **CASPAR can only be applied to a patient who already has established inflammatory articular disease — of a joint, the spine, or an enthesis.**

This is the gate. The source text lists the scoring table without stating the gate, and that omission causes real errors. **CASPAR is not a screening tool for psoriasis patients.** If you apply the score to a psoriasis patient with no inflammatory joint disease, they will "score" 2 points for skin psoriasis and perhaps 1 for nail dystrophy and 1 for negative RF — a total of 4 — and you will "diagnose" PsA in someone with entirely healthy joints. First establish inflammatory articular disease clinically; *then* score.

### The scoring system — ≥3 points confirms PsA

| Criterion | Points |
|---|---|
| Evidence of **current skin psoriasis** (seen by a clinician) | **2** |
| **Personal or family history** of psoriasis (1st/2nd-degree relative), in the absence of current psoriasis | 1 |
| Typical **psoriatic nail dystrophy** — pitting, onycholysis, subungual hyperkeratosis | 1 |
| **Negative rheumatoid factor** | 1 |
| **Dactylitis** — current, or documented historically by a clinician | 1 |
| **Radiological juxta-articular new bone formation**, **excluding osteophytes**, on plain films of hand or foot | 1 |

### Why each item is there — the reasoning behind the score

* **Current psoriasis scores double (2 points).** It is the strongest single discriminator; visible psoriasis in a patient with inflammatory arthritis makes PsA overwhelmingly likely. Note the hierarchy: you score **either** 2 for current psoriasis **or** 1 for history/family history — never both.
* **History/family history (1 point)** exists to rescue the **~15% whose joints come first** and the patients whose psoriasis has cleared or is hidden (scalp, natal cleft, umbilicus, retroauricular — *always look in these sites*).
* **Nail dystrophy (1 point)** is included because of the DIP–nail–enthesis anatomical unity described earlier, and because nail disease is the strongest cutaneous predictor of PsA.
* **Negative RF (1 point)** — the "seronegative" identity card. Its job is to exclude RA, in which RF is positive in ~80%.
* **Dactylitis (1 point)** — the whole-digit inflammation that RA does not produce.
* **Juxta-articular new bone, excluding osteophytes (1 point)** — this is the radiologist's version of the construction crew. The exclusion of osteophytes is deliberate and clever: osteophytes are the new bone of **osteoarthritis**, and without that exclusion every elderly patient with hand OA would score a point.

**A worked example:** a 42-year-old with asymmetric swelling of the right knee and left 3rd toe (→ gate passed: inflammatory articular disease), plaques on both elbows (**2**), nail pitting (**1**), and a negative RF (**1**) = **4 points → PsA confirmed.**

## Part 10 — Treatment: The EULAR / GRAPPA Ladder

The logic is **step-up therapy**: start simple, reassess at defined intervals, escalate if the target (minimal disease activity / remission) isn't met.

### Phase 1 — NSAIDs ± local glucocorticoid injections

* **NSAIDs** for symptomatic relief of pain and stiffness — appropriate as monotherapy only in mild, non-erosive, oligoarticular disease.
* **Intra-articular or peri-entheseal corticosteroid injections** for a dominant single inflamed joint or enthesis — high local potency, minimal systemic exposure.
* ⚠️ **Important cautions:** (a) **systemic corticosteroids are used sparingly in PsA**, because withdrawal can precipitate a **pustular flare** of the skin disease; (b) avoid injecting *directly into* the Achilles tendon — risk of rupture; (c) NSAIDs may aggravate psoriasis in some patients.
* **Note the contrast with RA**, where guidelines push you to a DMARD within weeks of diagnosis. The NSAID-first step in PsA reflects the fact that many patients have mild oligoarticular disease — but if there is polyarthritis, erosions, dactylitis or poor prognostic features, **do not linger here.**

### Phase 2 — Conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs)

* **Methotrexate (MTX)** — the workhorse, and the natural first choice in dermatology because it treats **skin and peripheral joints together**.
* **Leflunomide** — effective for peripheral arthritis and dactylitis.
* **Sulfasalazine** — an option for peripheral arthritis; no benefit for skin.
* ⚠️ **Key limitation:** csDMARDs have **little or no proven efficacy for axial disease or enthesitis.** So a patient with predominantly spinal disease or heel enthesitis should **skip straight from NSAIDs to a biologic** — putting them on methotrexate wastes months. This is a genuinely high-yield point.
* *(Ciclosporin, azathioprine and apremilast may appear in this space depending on guideline version.)*

### Phase 3 — Biologic and targeted synthetic DMARDs

**⚠️ Important correction to the source text:** the source groups PDE4 inhibitors and JAK inhibitors under the heading "Biologics." **This is incorrect, and the distinction is commonly examined.** The correct classification:

**Biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs)** — large-molecule proteins (monoclonal antibodies, fusion proteins) manufactured in living cells; given by injection/infusion; target cytokines or receptors **outside** the cell:

* **TNF-α inhibitors** — infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept, golimumab, certolizumab
* **IL-12/23 inhibitors** — ustekinumab (targets the shared p40 subunit)
* **IL-23 (p19) inhibitors** — guselkumab, risankizumab
* **IL-17 inhibitors** — secukinumab, ixekizumab (IL-17A); bimekizumab (IL-17A **and** F)
* *(Also T-cell co-stimulation modulator abatacept)*

**Targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs)** — **small molecules**, taken **orally**, targeting specific **intracellular** signalling pathways:

* **PDE4 inhibitor** — **apremilast** (raises intracellular cAMP → dampens TNF-α/IL-17 production)
* **JAK inhibitors** — **tofacitinib, upadacitinib** (block Janus kinase, interrupting cytokine signal transduction at the receptor's intracellular tail)

> **Mnemonic for the difference:** **b**DMARDs are **b**ig proteins made by cells, injected, and act **outside** the cell. **ts**DMARDs are **t**iny **s**ynthetic molecules, swallowed, and act **inside** the cell.

**GRAPPA's central contribution — treat by domain.** GRAPPA stratifies therapy across **six domains**: peripheral arthritis, axial disease, enthesitis, dactylitis, skin, and nails. Different agents excel in different domains — for example, IL-17 inhibitors are outstanding for skin and enthesitis; TNF inhibitors have the longest track record for peripheral arthritis and axial disease; IL-17 inhibitors are also strong axially; IL-23 inhibitors are excellent for skin. **The clinical skill is matching the drug to the patient's dominant domain(s)**, plus comorbidities (e.g. avoid IL-17 blockers in coexisting IBD, which they can worsen; favour TNF or IL-12/23 inhibitors instead; avoid JAK inhibitors in high cardiovascular/thrombotic risk).

### Phase 4 — Switch after 3–6 months of inadequate response

* Assess response at **3 months**, with the definitive decision by **6 months**.
* If the target is not met, **switch to an agent with a different mechanism of action** (e.g. TNF inhibitor → IL-17 inhibitor) rather than cycling endlessly within one class.
* **Why this window?** It is long enough for a biologic to demonstrate full effect, but short enough to prevent irreversible erosion. Remember, PsA damages bone within the first two years.

## Part 11 — Common Misunderstandings (Clear These Up Now)

1. **"Seronegative means we can't test for it."** No — it means the *rheumatoid* serology is negative. The negative RF is itself a diagnostic criterion.
2. **"Normal CRP/ESR rules out PsA."** Wrong. Up to half of PsA patients have normal inflammatory markers. PsA is a **clinical and radiological** diagnosis.
3. **"Only severe psoriasis gets PsA."** PsA correlates with severity, but it can arise in limited disease — especially with **nail, scalp and intergluteal/perianal** involvement, which are independent predictors.
4. **"Dactylitis is just a swollen joint."** No — it is whole-digit inflammation involving tendon sheath, joints, entheses and soft tissue. Uniform sausage, not knots on a rope.
5. **"PsA and RA look the same on X-ray."** They look opposite. RA = **erosions + osteopenia, no new bone, no ankylosis, DIPs spared.** PsA = **erosions + fluffy new bone + periostitis + bony ankylosis, DIPs favoured.**
6. **"Raised uric acid in a psoriatic = gout."** Not necessarily — high epidermal turnover raises urate in psoriasis anyway. Aspirate and look for crystals.
7. **"Pencil-in-cup is just a fancy name for an erosion."** It is a *two-bone* deformity: tapered proximal bone (pencil) + hollowed, widened distal base (cup).
8. **"Apremilast and tofacitinib are biologics."** They are **tsDMARDs** — oral small molecules acting intracellularly.
9. **"CASPAR screens psoriasis patients for arthritis."** No — it requires **pre-existing inflammatory articular disease** as a prerequisite. For screening, use questionnaires (PEST, ToPAS) and a targeted history.
10. **"Skin severity predicts joint severity."** They often run independently. A patient can have trivial skin and destructive arthritis, or vice versa — which is exactly why you must ask about joints at every visit regardless of PASI.
11. **"Moll and Wright is obsolete."** It is superseded for *classification* but remains the standard descriptive vocabulary — and it's still examined.
12. **"Steroids are fine for a PsA flare."** Systemic steroids risk **rebound pustular psoriasis** on withdrawal. Prefer local injections and escalate DMARD/biologic therapy instead.

## Part 12 — The Core Idea in One Picture

Picture a single **finger**.

* In its **skin**, TNF-α, IL-17 and IL-23 are driving keratinocyte hyperproliferation → a **psoriatic plaque**.
* In its **nail matrix** — only a millimetre from the DIP joint and sharing the extensor tendon's entheseal anchor — the same cytokines produce **pitting and onycholysis**.
* At that **enthesis**, mechanical stress plus the same cytokines produce **enthesitis** — the primary lesion of the disease.
* Spreading inward, the **synovium** becomes inflamed and grows tortuous new vessels → **synovitis**, and when tendon sheath, joints and soft tissue all inflame together, the whole digit swells into a **sausage** → **dactylitis**.
* In the **bone**, the cytokines simultaneously unleash **osteoclasts** (→ erosions, osteolysis, **pencil-in-cup**, acro-osteolysis) and **osteoblasts** (→ fluffy marginal new bone, **periostitis**, **ivory phalanx**, **bony ankylosis**).
* Scale that same process up to the **spine and sacroiliac joints** and you get **squared vertebrae**, **asymmetric fluffy non-marginal syndesmophytes**, and **asymmetric sacroiliitis**.
* Because RF and anti-CCP are absent, the blood is **seronegative** — earning a CASPAR point.
* And because one cytokine axis drives every one of these lesions, **a single biologic can clear the plaque, restore the nail, and quiet the joint at the same time.**

> **One sentence to carry away:**
> **Psoriatic arthritis is an HLA-linked, TNF-α/IL-17/IL-23-driven, seronegative, enthesis-centred spondyloarthropathy in which the same cytokines that scale the skin simultaneously destroy and rebuild bone — which is why it is asymmetric, loves the DIP joints, produces dactylitis and enthesitis, and shows the unique radiographic signature of *erosions accompanied by fluffy new bone* culminating in the pencil-in-cup deformity; it is classified clinically by Moll and Wright's five patterns, confirmed by ≥3 CASPAR points in a patient with established inflammatory articular disease, and treated by stepping up from NSAIDs → csDMARDs → bDMARDs/tsDMARDs, choosing the agent according to the dominant clinical domain.**
