# Psoriasis: A Complete Guide to Management

## *A Mini-Lecture — from the bedside scrape to the biologic*

---

## Part 0 — The Big Picture: Why We Manage Psoriasis the Way We Do

Before we touch a single drug, let me give you the mental map, because everything that follows hangs off it.

Psoriasis is **not** a disease of "dry skin." It is a **chronic, immune-mediated, systemic inflammatory disease** in which a self-sustaining conversation between dendritic cells, T cells and keratinocytes goes into a feedback loop. The core axis — and you must hold this in your head, because it explains both the biopsy and the biologics — is:

> **Trigger → dendritic cell activation → IL-23 release → Th17 cell differentiation → IL-17A / IL-22 / TNF-α → keratinocyte hyperproliferation + neutrophil chemotaxis + angiogenesis → plaque.**

Every single thing we are about to discuss is a downstream consequence of that loop:

| Consequence of the loop | What you see clinically | What you see on biopsy | What you see on dermoscopy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keratinocyte turnover accelerates from ~28 days to **3–4 days** | Thick plaque, silvery scale | Acanthosis, parakeratosis, loss of granular layer | White-yellow scales |
| Neutrophils are recruited into the epidermis by IL-17-driven chemokines (CXCL8/IL-8) | Pustules (in pustular variants) | Munro's microabscess, Kogoj's spongiform pustule | — |
| VEGF-driven angiogenesis with elongation of dermal papillae | Bright red erythema, bleeds easily | Dilated tortuous capillaries in elongated papillae | Regularly distributed **red dots** |
| Epidermis over the papillary tips becomes paper-thin | **Auspitz sign** | **Suprapapillary thinning** | Pinpoint bleeding |

So when a student asks, "Why does psoriasis bleed when you scrape it?" the answer is not a rule to memorise — it is a direct read-out of the pathology. Hold on to that; it will make the whole answer coherent.

**Management therefore has five sequential tasks**, and a good exam answer (or a good clinic visit) follows them in order:

1. **Confirm the diagnosis** — bedside signs, then histopathology/dermoscopy if doubtful.
2. **Assess severity and impact** — body surface area, PASI, quality of life, special sites.
3. **Screen for comorbidities and triggers** — baseline bloods, arthritis, metabolic disease, infection screen before immunosuppression.
4. **Treat, climbing a rational therapeutic ladder** — topicals → phototherapy → conventional systemics → biologics/small molecules.
5. **Educate, maintain, and monitor** — because we control, we do not cure.

Let's take them one at a time.

## Part 1 — Confirming the Diagnosis: The Bedside Investigations

### 1.1 The Grattage Test (the "scraping test")

**What it is:** *Grattage* is simply French for "scratching." You take a **glass slide** (or the edge of a wooden spatula) and gently, methodically scrape the surface of the plaque. What makes this beautiful is that it produces **three signs in a strict sequence**, each corresponding to a deeper histological layer. Think of it as an archaeological dig through the plaque.

**Stage 1 — The Candle-Grease Sign (*signe de la tache de bougie*)**

As you scrape, the scale becomes **whiter, more abundant and more silvery**, exactly like scraping the surface of a wax candle with a knife.

*Why does this happen?* In normal skin the corneocytes are packed tightly like bricks in mortar. In psoriasis, the parakeratotic corneocytes are stacked **loosely and irregularly**, with **air trapped between the layers**. Air–keratin interfaces scatter light. So scraping fractures those layers, traps even more air, and the scale goes from grey-brown to brilliant silvery-white. It is an optical phenomenon — the same reason crushed ice looks white while a solid ice cube looks clear.

**Stage 2 — The Membrane of Bulkley**

Keep scraping. Once *all* the scale is removed, you are left with a **thin, moist, glistening, translucent red surface** — a last delicate film before you reach raw dermis. This is the **Membrane of Bulkley** (named after L. Duncan Bulkley, the American dermatologist, 1845–1928).

> ⚠️ **Correction to note:** Many handwritten notes and OCR-scanned texts render this as "Membrane of **Berkeley**." That is an error. The correct eponym is **Bulkley**.

*What is this membrane histologically?* It represents the **last few viable suprapapillary epidermal cell layers** overlying the papillary tips — clinically often described as "the exposed basement membrane region." Because only 2–3 cells separate you from the capillary loops, it looks wet and red (you are essentially looking *through* the epidermis at blood).

**Stage 3 — The Auspitz Sign**

Remove the Bulkley membrane and you tear the roof off the capillary loops. Blood appears as **multiple discrete pinpoint droplets** — not a sheet of bleeding, but a dotted pattern, like dew on grass. This is the **Auspitz sign**, described by Heinrich Auspitz.

**The mechanism — three factors must coexist:**

1. **Suprapapillary thinning of the stratum Malpighii** — the living epidermis over each papillary tip is thinned to only a few cells. The "roof" is flimsy.
2. **Elongation and tortuosity of the dermal papillary capillaries** — the vessels have grown *up* into elongated papillae and sit abnormally superficially. The target is raised toward you.
3. **Parakeratosis** — the overlying stratum corneum is loose and shears off easily rather than resisting the blade. The lid comes off easily.

> **Analogy:** Imagine rows of water balloons (the capillary-filled papillae) laid on a table, covered by a single sheet of wet tissue paper (the suprapapillary epidermis), and over that a layer of loose cornflakes (the parakeratotic scale). Brush off the cornflakes (candle-grease sign), and you see the glistening wet tissue paper (Bulkley's membrane). Rub the tissue paper away and the balloon tops rupture — one drop of blood per balloon. That "one drop per papilla" geometry is exactly why the bleeding is *pinpoint* and *evenly spaced*, not confluent.

**Clinical value — sensitive but NOT specific:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Positive in** | Psoriasis vulgaris (chronic plaque) — highly characteristic; also **Darier's disease** and **actinic keratosis** |
| **Negative/absent in** | **Pustular** psoriasis, **erythrodermic** psoriasis, **guttate** psoriasis, and inverse/flexural psoriasis |

*Why absent in those variants?* Because the sign requires the **mature architecture of a chronic plaque**. In **guttate** psoriasis the lesions are days-to-weeks old — there is heavy inflammation but the acanthosis and papillary elongation haven't had time to develop. In **erythrodermic** psoriasis the epidermis is diffusely inflamed and thinned without the regular elongated-papillae pattern. In **pustular** psoriasis the dominant pathology is intraepidermal neutrophil collection, not papillary vascular elongation. This is a lovely example of a sign being a *structural* read-out: no structure, no sign.

> 🩺 **Practical caution rarely taught but important:** Do not vigorously elicit the Auspitz sign as a routine ritual. Psoriasis exhibits the **Koebner phenomenon** — trauma induces new lesions. Aggressive scraping can seed a fresh plaque at the test site. Use it once, gently, when the diagnosis is genuinely in question.

### 1.2 Histopathology — Reading the Biopsy as a Story

Biopsy is **not routine** in classic plaque psoriasis (the clinical diagnosis is usually confident). Reserve it for: atypical morphology, suspected pustular/erythrodermic variants, palmoplantar disease vs. eczema, psoriasiform drug eruptions, or before committing to long-term systemic therapy in a doubtful case.

Here is the essential principle that unifies all the findings:

> **Psoriasis histology = (a) accelerated, incomplete keratinocyte maturation + (b) neutrophils in the wrong place + (c) an overgrown, over-vascularised papillary dermis.**

#### A. Psoriasis Vulgaris — EARLY (active/evolving) lesion

| Finding | What it means mechanistically |
|---|---|
| **Mounds of parakeratosis** alternating with **orthokeratosis** | Parakeratosis = corneocytes retaining their **nuclei**. The cell was pushed to the surface so fast (3–4 days instead of 28) it never had time to extrude its nucleus. "Mounds" alternating with normal orthokeratosis shows the process is *focal and episodic* early on. |
| **Munro's microabscesses** | Collections of **neutrophils within the parakeratotic stratum corneum**. Driven by IL-17-induced CXCL8/IL-8 from keratinocytes. **Sterile** — the word "abscess" is misleading. |
| **Vacuolation and disappearance of granular cells** (hypogranulosis/agranulosis) | The granular layer is where keratohyalin granules and profilaggrin are made — a *maturation step*. Skip the step, lose the layer. Hypogranulosis and parakeratosis are two faces of the same coin. |
| **Lymphocytes extending into the lower epidermis with slight spongiosis** | T cells trafficking upward; the intercellular oedema they cause is *slight* — an important discriminator from eczema, where spongiosis dominates. |
| **Capillary dilatation and papillary dermal oedema** | VEGF-driven; produces the clinical bright erythema. |
| **Perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate in dermis** | The immunological engine room. |
| **Spongiform pustule of Kogoj** in the **spinous (Malpighian) layer** | Neutrophils accumulating *between* keratinocytes, whose degenerating cell membranes form a sponge-like meshwork. Essentially the same process as Munro's, but one storey lower. **Kogoj = spinous layer; Munro = stratum corneum.** Remember it as "**K** for **K**eratinocyte layer, **M** for the **M**ost superficial layer." |

#### B. Psoriasis Vulgaris — ADVANCED (chronic, established plaque)

This is the "textbook" picture, and notice how it is simply the early picture *matured and made confluent*:

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Acanthosis with regular/irregular elongation of the rete ridges, thickened in their lower portion** | Sustained hyperproliferation. The classic description is "test-tubes in a rack" — long, downward, club-shaped rete pegs of roughly equal length. |
| **Suprapapillary thinning**, with occasional spongiform pustules | The epidermis is *thick between* the papillae and *thin over* them. **This is the anatomical basis of the Auspitz sign.** |
| **Pallor of the upper epidermal layers** | Glycogen accumulation in rapidly dividing, metabolically abnormal keratinocytes; also intracellular oedema. |
| **Diminished or absent granular layer** | Persistent maturation skip. |
| **Confluent parakeratosis** | The focal "mounds" of the early lesion have merged into a continuous sheet — this is the histological correlate of the thick, adherent silvery scale. |
| **Munro's microabscesses present** | Ongoing neutrophil recruitment. |
| **Elongation and oedema of dermal papillae** | Papillae stretch upward like fingers, carrying their capillaries with them. |
| **Dilated, tortuous capillaries** | The second anatomical basis of the Auspitz sign — and of the dermoscopic red dots. |

> **One-sentence synthesis:** In advanced plaque psoriasis the epidermis is thick where it shouldn't be (the rete pegs) and thin where it shouldn't be (over the papillae), full of nucleated corneocytes and neutrophils, sitting on a bed of elongated, over-vascularised papillae.

#### C. Guttate Psoriasis

> "Like an active psoriasis lesion, but **more inflammation and less architecture**."

- Similar to an active (early) psoriasis lesion, but with a **more pronounced inflammatory infiltrate** and **less acanthosis**.
- **Neutrophils located above the parakeratosis** (i.e., very superficial collections).

*Why?* Guttate psoriasis is typically a **post-streptococcal**, acute, explosive eruption (classically 1–3 weeks after a streptococcal pharyngitis, via molecular mimicry between streptococcal M protein and keratin 17). It is inflammation without time. The immune infiltrate is florid; the epidermal remodelling hasn't caught up. **And this is exactly why the Auspitz sign is negative in guttate disease** — no acanthosis, no elongated papillae, no suprapapillary thinning. Beautifully internally consistent.

#### D. Erythrodermic Psoriasis

- May resemble an active psoriasis lesion (**rarely**), or be **indistinguishable from chronic eczematous dermatitis**.

*Why is it so unhelpful?* When >90% of the skin is involved, the specific architecture is washed out by diffuse, nonspecific inflammation. **Teaching point:** In erythroderma, the biopsy frequently *cannot* give you the answer. You diagnose erythrodermic psoriasis from **history** (pre-existing plaques, a known trigger such as abrupt withdrawal of systemic steroids), **nail changes** (which persist and are a superb clue), **scalp involvement**, and sometimes by repeated biopsies over time. Never promise a patient that "the biopsy will tell us."

#### E. Psoriasis in HIV/AIDS

| Finding | Significance |
|---|---|
| **Acanthosis WITHOUT suprapapillary thinning** | Altered epidermal kinetics → helps explain why the Auspitz sign may be unreliable. |
| Slight **spongiosis** | Adds an eczematous flavour. |
| Rare **necrotic keratinocytes** | Suggests a more cytotoxic, "interface-like" reaction. |
| Superficial perivascular infiltrate of **lymphocytes and histiocytes, occasionally with plasma cells** | Plasma cells are *not* expected in ordinary psoriasis — their presence should prompt you to consider HIV, syphilis, or a psoriasiform reaction pattern. |
| **Eosinophils ±** | Again atypical for classic psoriasis. |

> 🔑 **High-yield clinical link:** Psoriasis in HIV is often **severe, atypical, treatment-resistant, and may be the presenting illness**. Paradoxically it worsens as CD4 counts *fall*, despite psoriasis being T-cell driven — a reminder that loss of regulatory T cells and unopposed innate/Th17 activity matter as much as absolute CD4 number. Management implication: **antiretroviral therapy is itself a psoriasis treatment**, and among systemics, **acitretin** (a retinoid, not an immunosuppressant) and **phototherapy** are preferred first. Reserve methotrexate/cyclosporine/biologics for refractory disease with specialist input.

### 1.3 Blood and Ancillary Investigations — and *Why* Each One

This is where students lose marks: they list tests without justification. Every test below answers a specific question.

**Baseline / diagnostic:**

| Test | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Complete Blood Count (CBC)** | Is there an underlying **infection** or systemic inflammation driving the flare (e.g., neutrophilia in pustular psoriasis, which is a marker of disease activity, not necessarily sepsis)? Also gives me my **baseline** before methotrexate, which is myelosuppressive. Anaemia of chronic disease is common in extensive disease. |
| **ESR and CRP** | Objective measure of **systemic inflammatory burden**. Markedly raised in generalised pustular and erythrodermic psoriasis — a severity marker. Also helps flag psoriatic arthritis. |
| **Liver function tests (LFTs) and Renal function tests (RFTs)** | Mandatory **before and during systemic therapy**. Methotrexate and acitretin are hepatotoxic; cyclosporine and methotrexate (renally excreted) require intact renal function. Psoriasis is also independently associated with **non-alcoholic fatty liver disease**, so abnormal LFTs may reflect the disease, not just the drug. |
| **Random blood sugar (RBS) / fasting glucose / HbA1c** | Psoriasis is strongly associated with **metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes**. Also: acitretin worsens dyslipidaemia and glycaemia; corticosteroids raise glucose. Screening here is *comorbidity management*, not a formality. |
| **Urine routine** | Screens for occult **urinary tract infection** (a potential trigger/focus of infection before immunosuppression) and for **proteinuria/haematuria** (baseline before nephrotoxic cyclosporine). |
| **Fasting lipid profile** *(add this)* | Baseline for acitretin (which raises triglycerides significantly) and for cardiovascular risk stratification. |

**Infection screen before immunosuppression or biologics:**

| Test | Why |
|---|---|
| **HIV serology** | May be the cause of atypical/refractory psoriasis; also changes drug choice (avoid potent immunosuppressants first-line). |
| **HBsAg, anti-HCV** | Immunosuppressants and biologics (especially TNF-α inhibitors) can cause **reactivation of hepatitis B** — potentially fatal. Check *before*, not after. |
| **VDRL/RPR** | **Secondary syphilis** is a great mimic of guttate/psoriasiform eruptions (especially palmoplantar). Do not put a syphilitic on methotrexate. |
| **Mantoux test (TST) and/or IGRA + chest X-ray** | **Latent tuberculosis reactivation** is the signature hazard of TNF-α inhibitors and, to a lesser degree, other biologics and immunosuppressants. Screening is mandatory before initiation and periodically thereafter. |

**Additional, situation-specific:**
- **Throat swab / ASO titre** — in guttate psoriasis, to identify streptococcal trigger.
- **Serum uric acid** — may be raised in extensive disease from high cell turnover; also before starting drugs affecting it.
- **Pregnancy test (β-hCG)** — *absolutely essential* before methotrexate, acitretin, or tazarotene. All are teratogenic.
- **Skin biopsy / pus culture** — in pustular psoriasis, to prove the pustules are sterile and exclude infection.
- **Serum calcium** — if very large quantities of topical calcipotriol are being used.
- **Joint imaging / rheumatology referral** — if the PEST/PsA screening questionnaire is positive.

## Part 2 — Dermoscopy: A Window onto the Papillae

Dermoscopy in psoriasis is essentially **live histopathology**. Each finding maps directly onto a microscopic feature. Learn the mapping and you never have to memorise the list.

| Dermoscopic finding | Histological correlate |
|---|---|
| **Evenly/regularly distributed red dots (dotted vessels)** on a light-red background | The **tips of the dilated, tortuous capillary loops** inside the elongated dermal papillae, seen end-on. Because psoriatic papillae are uniformly elongated, the dots are **strikingly regular in spacing** — this regularity is the single most useful diagnostic clue, and it is what distinguishes psoriasis from eczema (where vessels are patchy/irregular) and from lichen planus (Wickham striae, no dotted vessels). |
| **White-yellow scales** (diffuse white scaling; yellowish where serous crust/pustules are present) | Confluent parakeratosis. White = pure hyperkeratosis/parakeratosis. Yellow = admixed serum or neutrophils (Munro's microabscesses reaching the surface). |
| **Auspitz sign** — pinpoint bleeding after scale removal | Suprapapillary thinning + superficial capillaries, as explained above. |
| **Evenly spaced papillae — "squirting papillae"** | The papillae appear as tiny pinpoint dots that "squirt" or bleed when manipulated, because the capillary inside each papilla is dilated and covered by almost nothing. The name is descriptive: manipulate, and each papilla squirts. |
| **Perifollicular involvement** | Follicular ostia ringed by scale/erythema — common in scalp psoriasis. |
| **"Holiday tree"/"Christmas tree" pattern** (as described in the source) | An arboriform/branching arrangement noted in some plaques. |

> 📌 **Two honest clarifications for the careful student:**
>
> 1. **"Squirting papillae" are not specific.** The same appearance is described in **seborrhoeic dermatitis, eczema (including contact dermatitis), and some skin cancers**. Dermoscopy in inflammatory disease works on **pattern + regularity + background colour**, never on one feature alone. The diagnostic signature of psoriasis is the *combination*: **uniform dotted vessels, regularly distributed, on a light-red background, with diffuse white scaling.**
> 2. The term **"Christmas/holiday tree"** is used inconsistently in the literature — it is far more classically applied to the *clinical* truncal distribution of **pityriasis rosea** along cleavage lines. Learn it as written in your source if your examiner expects it, but know that the internationally standardised dermoscopic descriptor for psoriasis is **"regularly distributed dotted vessels with white scales."** Knowing both protects you in both a viva and in practice.

## Part 3 — Before You Prescribe: Severity Assessment and Comorbidity Screening

Your source jumps from investigations to treatment. But **you cannot rationally choose a therapy without grading severity**, because the entire ladder is defined by it. Let me fill that gap — it is the hinge of the whole answer.

### 3.1 How we grade severity

| Tool | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **BSA (Body Surface Area)** | One palm + digits ≈ 1% BSA. **Mild <3%, Moderate 3–10%, Severe >10%.** |
| **PASI (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index)** | Scores erythema, induration (thickness) and desquamation (scaling) in 4 body regions, weighted by area. Range 0–72. **PASI >10 = severe.** The research/registry standard. |
| **DLQI (Dermatology Life Quality Index)** | 10 questions on quality of life, 0–30. **DLQI >10 = major impairment.** |
| **"Rule of Tens"** | Severe disease = **BSA >10% OR PASI >10 OR DLQI >10**. A wonderfully practical triad. |
| **Special-site involvement** | **Face, scalp, palms, soles, genitalia, nails** — even 1% BSA here may qualify as severe because of disproportionate functional and psychological impact. A banker with severe palmar psoriasis who cannot shake hands is "severe," whatever the PASI says. |

**Treat-to-target:** Modern guidelines set explicit goals — aim for **PASI 90** (90% improvement) or **absolute PASI ≤2 / BSA ≤1%** by 3–6 months. If not reached, escalate. This is the same philosophy as treating hypertension to a BP target rather than "some improvement."

### 3.2 Identify and remove triggers (cheap, and often decisive)

| Trigger | Action |
|---|---|
| **Streptococcal pharyngitis/tonsillitis** | Treat; consider tonsillectomy in recurrent, strep-driven guttate disease. |
| **Drugs**: β-blockers, **lithium**, antimalarials (chloroquine), NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, interferons, terbinafine, and — critically — **abrupt withdrawal of systemic corticosteroids** | Review and substitute where possible. |
| **Physical trauma** (Koebner phenomenon) | Avoid scratching, harsh scrubbing, unnecessary scraping. |
| **Smoking** (strongly linked to palmoplantar pustulosis) and **alcohol** | Cessation counselling — also improves methotrexate safety. |
| **Obesity** | Weight loss measurably improves PASI *and* improves response to biologics (which are often weight-dosed or weight-sensitive). |
| **Psychological stress** | Address it; the skin–stress loop is bidirectional and real. |
| **Infection (HIV, focal sepsis)** | Screen and treat. |

### 3.3 Screen for comorbidities — psoriasis is a systemic disease

- **Psoriatic arthritis (PsA)** — occurs in up to ~30%. Screen *every visit* with a tool like **PEST**; ask about morning stiffness >30 min, dactylitis, enthesitis, inflammatory back pain. **Nail pitting/onycholysis is a red flag for PsA.** Early recognition prevents irreversible joint erosion — and it changes drug choice entirely (you pick a drug that treats both).
- **Cardiometabolic**: hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, obesity, NAFLD, increased myocardial infarction and stroke risk (severe psoriasis shortens life expectancy by several years). Measure **BP, waist circumference, BMI, lipids, HbA1c**.
- **Psychiatric**: depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, alcohol misuse. Ask directly.
- **Inflammatory bowel disease** and **uveitis** — shared IL-23 biology; and IBD history changes biologic selection.

## Part 4 — The Therapeutic Ladder

Here is the organising principle. **Match the intensity of therapy to the burden of disease, and match the *mechanism* to the *patient's comorbidities*.**

```
MILD / LOCALISED  ──────────────►  TOPICAL THERAPY
                                        │ (inadequate, or >5–10% BSA)
MODERATE  ─────────────────────►  PHOTOTHERAPY (NB-UVB / PUVA)
                                        │ (inadequate, impractical, or unstable)
MODERATE–SEVERE  ──────────────►  CONVENTIONAL SYSTEMICS
                                   (MTX, acitretin, cyclosporine)
                                        │ (failure / intolerance /
                                        │  contraindication)
SEVERE / REFRACTORY / PsA  ────►  BIOLOGICS  &  ORAL SMALL MOLECULES
```

### 4.1 Topical Therapy — for mild-to-moderate psoriasis with localised plaques

The strategy is always **combination and rotation**, not monotherapy. You typically need: *something to remove scale* + *something to suppress inflammation* + *something to normalise differentiation* + *emollient for the barrier*.

| Agent | Mechanism (the "why") | Practical points & pitfalls |
|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin D₃ analogues — Calcipotriol** (also calcitriol, tacalcitol) | Binds the **vitamin D receptor** on keratinocytes → **inhibits proliferation, promotes normal differentiation** (restores the granular layer), and **suppresses T-cell cytokines**. In other words: it directly reverses the two core defects. | First-line steroid-sparing agent. **Max ~100 g/week** in adults — exceeding this risks **hypercalcaemia**. Mildly irritant on the face/flexures. **Inactivated by low pH — do NOT mix with salicylic acid** in the same application, and it is degraded by UVA, so apply *after* phototherapy, not before. The **fixed combination with betamethasone dipropionate** is elegantly synergistic: the steroid suppresses calcipotriol's irritation, and calcipotriol offsets steroid-induced atrophy. |
| **Topical corticosteroids** *(the practical mainstay — add this to your list)* | Bind glucocorticoid receptors → broad anti-inflammatory, antiproliferative, vasoconstrictive effects. | Potency matched to site: **mild** on face/flexures, **potent/very potent** for trunk, limbs, palms, scalp. Risks: **atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, tachyphylaxis, rebound flare (including pustular conversion) on abrupt withdrawal**. Use short bursts, then taper to weekend/intermittent maintenance. |
| **Topical retinoid — Tazarotene** | Binds **RAR-β and RAR-γ** → normalises keratinocyte differentiation, reduces proliferation, is anti-inflammatory. | Steroid-sparing, and the benefit outlasts treatment (long remissions). Main problem: **irritation** — mitigate with alternate-day use, short contact, or pairing with a steroid. **Teratogenic — contraindicated in pregnancy.** |
| **Coal tar** | An **aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) agonist** → restores filaggrin expression and normalises differentiation; also antimitotic and antipruritic. The oldest effective treatment we have. | Cheap, safe in pregnancy, excellent for scalp (tar shampoos) and chronic stable plaques. Downsides: **smell, staining of clothes, folliculitis, photosensitivity**. Classic **Goeckerman regimen** = coal tar + UVB — still remarkably effective. |
| **Anthralin (Dithranol)** | Generates **free radicals**, inhibits mitochondrial respiration and DNA replication → potently antiproliferative; also anti-neutrophil. | Given as **short-contact therapy** (10–30 min, then wash off), with concentration escalated gradually. Causes **staining (skin, clothes, bath — a brownish-purple stain) and irritation of surrounding normal skin**, so apply carefully to the plaque only. The **Ingram regimen** = dithranol + coal tar bath + UVB. Can produce long remissions. |
| **Salicylic acid** (2–6%) | A **keratolytic** — reduces corneocyte cohesion and dissolves intercellular cement, so thick scale lifts off. | Use for **thick, hyperkeratotic, heavily scaling plaques** and for scalp/palmoplantar disease. Its real purpose is **to let the other drugs get in** — a locksmith, not a treatment. Pitfalls: **do not combine with calcipotriol** (inactivates it); **do not apply before phototherapy** (it absorbs/blocks UV); **avoid over large areas or in children** (risk of **salicylism**: tinnitus, nausea, metabolic acidosis). |
| **Emollients** | Restore barrier lipids, reduce transepidermal water loss, soften scale, reduce fissuring and itch, and **enhance penetration** of active agents. | Never an afterthought. Bland, generous, frequent. Reduce Koebnerising scratch trauma. |
| **Topical calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) *(add)* | Inhibit calcineurin → block T-cell IL-2 transcription. | Off-label but invaluable for **face, eyelids, genital and flexural psoriasis**, where steroids cause atrophy. Burning on first applications; warn the patient. |

**Site-specific tips that examiners love:**
- **Scalp:** salicylic acid/coconut oil to descale → steroid lotion/gel/foam or calcipotriol-betamethasone gel; tar shampoo for maintenance.
- **Face/flexures:** mild steroid short-term + tacrolimus for maintenance. Avoid tar, dithranol, tazarotene here.
- **Palms/soles:** very potent steroid under **occlusion**, keratolytics, acitretin, or local PUVA.
- **Nails:** intralesional triamcinolone into the nail matrix/bed, potent steroid + calcipotriol; but truly significant nail disease usually needs **systemic/biologic** therapy. Warn that nails take **6–12 months** to improve — this is the single commonest cause of patients thinking treatment failed.

### 4.2 Phototherapy — for moderate disease

The principle: ultraviolet light is a **locally delivered immunosuppressant**. It induces **apoptosis of activated T cells** in skin, depletes Langerhans cells, shifts the cytokine milieu away from Th1/Th17, and is antiproliferative.

| Modality | Details |
|---|---|
| **Narrowband UVB (NB-UVB, 311–313 nm)** | **The workhorse for stable, widespread plaque or guttate psoriasis.** Given 2–3× weekly, dose escalated by erythema response/MED. Advantages: **no systemic drug, no photosensitiser, safe in pregnancy and children, safe in HIV**, minimal monitoring. The 311 nm peak was chosen because it maximises therapeutic effect while minimising the erythemogenic/burning wavelengths. |
| **PUVA (Psoralen + UVA)** | Oral (or topical/bath) **8-methoxypsoralen** + UVA. Psoralen intercalates into DNA and, on UVA absorption, forms **cross-links** → potent antiproliferative and immunosuppressive effect. Used for **widespread, thick, or NB-UVB-refractory disease**, and for palmoplantar disease (topical/bath PUVA). Requires **protective eyewear for 12–24 h** (cataract risk) and photoprotection. Higher **long-term cutaneous carcinogenic risk** (squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma with high cumulative doses) — so lifetime session counts are tracked. Contraindicated in pregnancy, lactation, hepatic impairment, and children. |

**When NOT to use phototherapy:** photosensitive disorders (lupus, xeroderma pigmentosum), photosensitising drugs, history of melanoma/multiple NMSC, **unstable or erythrodermic psoriasis** (UV can precipitate a flare), and poor practical access (3 hospital visits a week for 8 weeks defeats many patients).

> **Elegant combination:** Phototherapy + acitretin ("**Re-PUVA**" or Re-UVB) is markedly more effective than either alone and lets you reduce the cumulative UV dose. **Avoid** combining phototherapy with cyclosporine (skin-cancer risk stacks up) or with methotrexate long-term.

### 4.3 Conventional Systemic Therapy — moderate-to-severe disease unresponsive to topicals/phototherapy

### 4.4 Biologic Therapies — for moderate-to-severe psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, and failure of other therapies

Here is where the pathogenesis pays you back. Look at the axis again: **IL-23 → Th17 → IL-17A / TNF-α**. Biologics are simply **precision scissors cutting that chain at a chosen link.** The further downstream in the psoriasis-specific pathway you cut, the more selective (and generally the safer for the skin indication) the drug becomes.

> **Universal pre-biologic checklist:** screen for **latent TB (Mantoux/IGRA + CXR)**, **hepatitis B and C**, **HIV**; exclude active infection and malignancy; update vaccinations and give **no live vaccines** once on therapy; assess cardiac failure and demyelinating disease (for TNF inhibitors) and IBD history (for IL-17 inhibitors); check pregnancy status.

#### A. TNF-α inhibitors (cut the shared inflammatory amplifier)

**Adalimumab** — a fully human monoclonal anti-TNF-α antibody.
- **Dose: 80 mg subcutaneously initially, then 40 mg every 2 weeks** (commonly starting one week after the loading dose).
- **Strengths:** excellent for **psoriatic arthritis, enthesitis and axial disease**, hidradenitis suppurativa, uveitis, and IBD — the ideal choice when psoriasis travels with joints or gut.
- **Watch for:** **reactivation of latent TB** and hepatitis B, serious infections, **worsening of congestive heart failure**, demyelinating disease, drug-induced lupus, injection-site reactions, and the curious **paradoxical psoriasis** (especially palmoplantar pustulosis) that TNF blockade can itself induce.
- *Other agents in the class:* etanercept (fusion protein; also licensed in children), infliximab (IV, the fastest-acting — valuable in erythrodermic and generalised pustular psoriasis), certolizumab pegol (lacks an Fc region → minimal placental transfer → the **preferred biologic in pregnancy**).

#### B. IL-17 inhibitors (cut the final effector cytokine)

These block the cytokine that *directly* drives keratinocyte hyperproliferation and neutrophil chemotaxis — hence their **speed and depth of skin clearance** (high PASI 90/100 rates, often within 4 weeks).

1. **Secukinumab** (anti-IL-17A) — **300 mg at weeks 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4, then 300 mg every 4 weeks.** *(Your source's "W = 0, 1, 2, 3, then every 4 weeks" is the standard weekly loading schedule; note that week 4 is both the last loading dose and the first maintenance dose — hence the two notations you will see in different books.)*
2. **Ixekizumab** (anti-IL-17A) — **160 mg (two 80 mg injections) initially, then 80 mg every 2 weeks to week 12, then 80 mg every 4 weeks.** *(Your source's "then 80 mg every 4 weeks" describes the maintenance phase; the every-2-week induction phase is part of the licensed regimen.)*
3. *Also:* **brodalumab** (blocks the IL-17 **receptor A**, thus blocking IL-17A/F/E — carries a boxed warning regarding suicidal ideation) and **bimekizumab** (blocks **both IL-17A and IL-17F** — currently the most potent for skin clearance, with a higher rate of oral candidiasis).

**Class-specific adverse effects — and the logic behind them:** IL-17 is a key defence cytokine at mucosal surfaces against *Candida*. Block it and you get **mucocutaneous candidiasis** (usually mild, oral/genital). IL-17 also has a protective role in the gut epithelium, so IL-17 blockade can **precipitate or worsen inflammatory bowel disease** — therefore **avoid IL-17 inhibitors in patients with Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis**. Also: upper respiratory infections, neutropenia, injection-site reactions.

#### C. IL-23 inhibitors (cut the master upstream switch)

Blocking IL-23 removes the signal that *sustains* the pathogenic Th17 population, rather than merely mopping up its product. The consequence is the most attractive feature of this class: **remarkably durable responses with very infrequent dosing**, and the cleanest safety profile currently available.

1. **Guselkumab** (anti-IL-23 **p19** subunit) — **100 mg at week 0, 100 mg at week 4, then 100 mg every 8 weeks.**
   > ⚠️ **Clarification:** The notation "100 mg at weeks 0, 4, 8" that appears in some notes is shorthand and is easily misread. The licensed schedule is **weeks 0 and 4, then every 8 weeks** — i.e., the third dose *falls* at week 12, not week 8. Learn it as "**0, 4, then q8w**."
2. **Ustekinumab** — **45 mg (≤100 kg) or 90 mg (>100 kg) at weeks 0 and 4, then every 12 weeks.**
   > ⚠️ **Important classification correction:** Ustekinumab binds the **shared p40 subunit**, and therefore blocks **both IL-12 and IL-23**. It is properly an **anti-IL-12/23** agent, *not* a selective IL-23 inhibitor. It is frequently grouped with the IL-23 blockers for convenience (and your source does so), but if you are asked to classify precisely, say **IL-12/23 (p40) inhibitor**. The selective **p19** (IL-23-only) agents are **guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab**.
3. *Also:* **risankizumab** (150 mg at weeks 0, 4, then q12w) and **tildrakizumab** (100 mg at weeks 0, 4, then q12w).

**Class notes:** Excellent safety; nasopharyngitis and headache are the commonest events; lower infection risk than TNF inhibitors; **safe in patients with IBD** (indeed, ustekinumab and risankizumab *treat* Crohn's disease). Slightly **slower onset** than IL-17 inhibitors but superior long-term drug survival.

#### D. How to *choose* a biologic — the decision that earns marks

| Patient scenario | Preferred class |
|---|---|
| Psoriasis **+ significant psoriatic arthritis / axial disease** | TNF-α inhibitor or IL-17 inhibitor |
| Psoriasis **+ inflammatory bowel disease** | **IL-23/IL-12-23 inhibitor** (ustekinumab, risankizumab); **avoid IL-17 inhibitors** |
| Psoriasis **+ heart failure or demyelinating disease** | Avoid TNF inhibitors → use IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitor |
| **Pregnancy / planning pregnancy** | **Certolizumab pegol** (negligible placental transfer) |
| **Need for very rapid, deep clearance** (e.g., wedding, severe distress) | IL-17 inhibitor (or IV infliximab) |
| **Priority on infrequent injections / long-term durability** | IL-23 inhibitor (q8–12 weekly) |
| **Latent TB not yet treated / high TB burden setting** | Prefer IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitors over TNF inhibitors, after treating latent TB |
| **Generalised pustular psoriasis flare** | **Spesolimab** (anti-IL-36 receptor — a newer, targeted rescue) or infliximab/cyclosporine |

### 4.5 Oral Small-Molecule Inhibitors — for moderate psoriasis intolerant of biologics or other systemics

These give you **targeted immunomodulation in a tablet** — attractive for needle-phobic patients and for those who cannot take injections.

| Drug | Mechanism | Dosing | Key points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Apremilast** (**PDE-4 inhibitor**) | Inhibits phosphodiesterase-4 → **intracellular cAMP rises** → PKA activation → **down-regulation of TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23, IFN-γ** and up-regulation of IL-10. It rebalances rather than suppresses. | **30 mg twice daily**, after a 5-day dose-titration schedule to limit GI upset. | Modest efficacy (PASI 75 in ~30%), but **no laboratory monitoring required** and **no TB screening needed** — a big practical advantage. Especially useful for **scalp, nail and palmoplantar** disease, and in patients with malignancy history or infection risk. Side effects: **diarrhoea, nausea, headache, weight loss**, and **depression/suicidal ideation (ask about mood)**. Reduce dose in severe renal impairment. |
| **Tofacitinib** (**JAK inhibitor**, JAK1/3) | Blocks **JAK–STAT signalling**, the intracellular relay used by IL-23, IL-12, IL-6, IFNs and many other cytokines → broad cytokine blockade from inside the cell. | Source states **5–15 mg once daily**. | ⚠️ **Flagged for accuracy:** In practice, tofacitinib for psoriatic disease is typically **5 mg twice daily** (or 11 mg extended-release once daily). It is **approved for psoriatic arthritis but not for plaque psoriasis in the US/EU**, and use in skin psoriasis is off-label and region-dependent. The "5–15 mg OD" range in the source likely reflects variation across indications and trials. **Class warnings (boxed):** serious infections, **herpes zoster**, TB reactivation, **venous thromboembolism**, major adverse cardiovascular events, malignancy, and dose-dependent **dyslipidaemia, cytopenias and raised creatine kinase**. Worth knowing — it is the direction the field has taken. |

### 4.6 Special Situations — where the ladder bends

| Situation | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Generalised pustular psoriasis (von Zumbusch)** | A **dermatological emergency**. Admit; correct fluid, electrolyte, protein and thermoregulatory derangement; treat infection. First-line systemics: **acitretin, cyclosporine, or infliximab**; methotrexate as an alternative. **Spesolimab (anti-IL-36R)** is the targeted rescue therapy for flares. |
| **Erythrodermic psoriasis** | Also an emergency — risks of **hypothermia, high-output cardiac failure, dehydration, hypoalbuminaemia, sepsis**. Nurse warm, bland emollients, fluids, nutrition. Systemics: **cyclosporine or infliximab (fastest)**, then acitretin/methotrexate. **Avoid phototherapy and abrupt drug changes.** |
| **Guttate psoriasis** | Often self-limiting over 3–4 months. Treat streptococcal infection; emollients, topical steroid/calcipotriol; **NB-UVB** works beautifully for widespread small lesions. |
| **Psoriasis in pregnancy** | Preferred: **emollients, topical corticosteroids (mild–moderate, limited area), NB-UVB**. Acceptable if needed: **cyclosporine**; among biologics, **certolizumab pegol**. **Absolutely contraindicated: methotrexate, acitretin, tazarotene, PUVA.** Note that psoriasis often *improves* in pregnancy and may flare postpartum. Also remember **impetigo herpetiformis** (pustular psoriasis of pregnancy). |
| **Children** | Topicals and **NB-UVB** first. Licensed biologics include **etanercept, ustekinumab, secukinumab, ixekizumab**. Methotrexate is used with care. Always look for the streptococcal/perianal trigger. |
| **HIV-associated psoriasis** | Start/optimise **ART** — it is therapeutic. Then **topicals, NB-UVB, acitretin** (non-immunosuppressive). Reserve immunosuppressants and biologics for refractory cases with specialist input. |
| **Nail psoriasis** | Intralesional triamcinolone, potent steroid + calcipotriol, topical tazarotene; systemics/biologics for severe disease. **Counsel that improvement takes 6–12 months.** Strong association with PsA. |
| **Psoriatic arthritis** | Requires a **disease-modifying strategy**: methotrexate; then TNF-α inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, ustekinumab/IL-23 inhibitors, or tofacitinib/apremilast. **Topicals and phototherapy do nothing for joints** — this is why screening matters. |

### 4.7 The Non-Pharmacological Half of Management (frequently forgotten, frequently marked)

- **Education:** Explain that psoriasis is **chronic, relapsing, controllable but not curable**, and above all **not contagious**. This single conversation transforms adherence.
- **Set expectations:** "Your skin will look better in 4–8 weeks, not 4 days." Non-adherence in psoriasis is enormous — largely because patients weren't told what to expect.
- **Emollients daily, forever.**
- **Lifestyle:** smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, weight loss, exercise, Mediterranean-style diet — each independently improves disease and cardiovascular risk.
- **Psychological support:** screen for depression; offer counselling, cognitive behavioural therapy, patient support groups.
- **Drug rotation and "drug holidays":** to limit cumulative toxicity (e.g., cyclosporine's renal toxicity, PUVA's carcinogenicity), rotate modalities rather than indefinitely continuing one.
- **Combination and sequential therapy:** e.g., start with cyclosporine for rapid control, then transition to methotrexate or a biologic for maintenance ("induction–transition–maintenance"). This is how experienced clinicians actually practise.
- **Vaccination and infection vigilance** on immunosuppressants.

## Part 5 — Common Misunderstandings (clear these and you will stand out)

1. **"The Auspitz sign is pathognomonic of psoriasis."** No. It is **sensitive but not specific** — it also occurs in **Darier's disease** and **actinic keratosis** — and it is **absent** in pustular, erythrodermic and guttate psoriasis. It supports, never proves.
2. **"Munro's microabscess means bacterial infection."** No — the neutrophils are **sterile**, recruited by IL-17-driven chemokines. Don't order antibiotics because of this word.
3. **"Munro and Kogoj are the same."** **Kogoj = spongiform pustule in the *spinous* layer; Munro = microabscess in the *stratum corneum*.** Different storeys of the same building.
4. **"Systemic steroids are the quickest way to clear psoriasis."** They are the quickest way to cause a catastrophe. **Withdrawal precipitates generalised pustular or erythrodermic psoriasis.** Avoid.
5. **"Methotrexate is a daily drug."** It is **weekly**. Daily dosing causes fatal pancytopenia and mucositis. Write it as "once weekly, on [named day]" and say it aloud to the patient.
6. **"Mix salicylic acid with calcipotriol for a two-in-one effect."** Salicylic acid's acidity **inactivates calcipotriol**. Separate them in time or site. Similarly, don't apply salicylic acid before phototherapy — it blocks UV.
7. **"Scrape every plaque to demonstrate the Auspitz sign."** Trauma **Koebnerises** psoriasis. Use the test sparingly.
8. **"Ustekinumab is an IL-23 inhibitor."** It targets the **p40 subunit shared by IL-12 and IL-23** — it is an **IL-12/23** inhibitor. The selective p19/IL-23 agents are guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab.
9. **"Biologics are a last resort."** Modern thinking favours **early effective control** in moderate-to-severe disease, both to improve quality of life and potentially to reduce the systemic inflammatory burden driving cardiovascular risk.
10. **"Clear skin means cured."** It means **controlled**. Stopping therapy abruptly usually means relapse; plan maintenance.
11. **"Coal tar and dithranol are obsolete."** They are inexpensive, effective, safe in pregnancy, and produce genuinely long remissions. In resource-limited settings they remain frontline.
12. **"Dermoscopy alone can diagnose psoriasis."** It is a **pattern-recognition adjunct**. "Squirting papillae" and dotted vessels also occur in seborrhoeic dermatitis, eczema and contact dermatitis. It is the **regularity and uniformity** of the dotted vessels plus white scale that points to psoriasis.
13. **"Nail and joint disease are cosmetic side issues."** Nail pitting predicts psoriatic arthritis; untreated PsA causes **irreversible joint destruction**. Ask about joints at *every* visit.

## Part 6 — The Summary Anchor: The Core Idea in One Picture

Picture a single psoriatic plaque in cross-section, and let it organise your entire answer:

> **At the bottom** sit dendritic cells pumping out **IL-23**, which drives **Th17** cells to release **IL-17 and TNF-α**.
> **Because of that**, the papillae grow long and stuff themselves with dilated, tortuous capillaries → *bright erythema, dermoscopic regularly-spaced red dots.*
> **Because of that**, keratinocytes divide every 3–4 days instead of every 28 → *acanthosis with elongated rete pegs, lost granular layer, confluent parakeratosis → thick silvery scale and the candle-grease sign.*
> **Because of that**, the epidermis over each papillary tip is paper-thin (**suprapapillary thinning**) → scrape off the scale and you reach the **Membrane of Bulkley**; scrape once more and each capillary loop yields one drop of blood → **the Auspitz sign.**
> **Because of that**, neutrophils swarm up through the epidermis → **Kogoj's spongiform pustule** in the spinous layer and **Munro's microabscess** in the horny layer.
>
> **And therefore, management is simply the act of interrupting this chain at the level your patient's disease demands:**
> — **Scale and surface** → keratolytics, emollients, tar, dithranol.
> — **Keratinocyte differentiation** → vitamin D analogues, retinoids.
> — **Local T cells** → topical steroids, calcineurin inhibitors, **NB-UVB/PUVA**.
> — **Systemic lymphocyte activation** → **methotrexate, cyclosporine, acitretin**.
> — **The exact cytokine** → **anti-TNF (adalimumab), anti-IL-17 (secukinumab, ixekizumab), anti-IL-23/IL-12-23 (guselkumab, ustekinumab)**.
> — **The intracellular signal** → **apremilast (PDE-4), tofacitinib/deucravacitinib (JAK/TYK2)**.
>
> **And wrapped around all of it:** grade the severity (**rule of tens**), hunt the trigger (**strep, drugs, trauma, smoking, stress**), screen the comorbidities (**joints, metabolic, mood, TB/hepatitis/HIV before immunosuppression**), and never forget that you are treating **a person with a lifelong systemic inflammatory disease**, not a patch of scale.

That is the whole answer — one mechanism, read in six different ways.
