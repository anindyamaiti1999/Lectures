# Lichen Planus Pigmentosus (LPPig) — A Mini-Lecture

---

## 1. Setting the Stage: Where Does This Condition Live?

Let me start by orienting you, because LPPig makes no sense in isolation — it only makes sense as a *member of a family*.

Think of **lichen planus (LP)** as a single immunological event with many costumes. The event is always the same: **cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) attack the basal layer of the epidermis.** That's it. That's the whole disease. Everything else — the shape of the lesion, its colour, its site — depends on *how intense* the attack is, *how long* it lasts, and *where* it happens.

Now, when that attack is **loud and sustained**, you get the textbook picture: violaceous, flat-topped, polygonal, pruritic papules with Wickham's striae. The epidermis reacts vigorously — it thickens (acanthosis), the granular layer wedges up (hypergranulosis), and the roof gets hyperkeratotic. That's *classical LP*.

But what if the attack is **brief, quiet, and then burns itself out?** The epidermis never has time to mount its hypertrophic response. Instead, the only lasting evidence of the battle is **pigment that has spilled out of dying basal cells into the dermis** — like a scorch mark left after a fire that no one saw.

**That is lichen planus pigmentosus.** It is often called an **abortive variant** or *forme fruste* of LP: the inflammatory phase is subtle or subclinical, and what the patient brings to you is not a rash but a **stain**.

> **Why this matters clinically:** patients with LPPig almost never come saying "I itch." They come saying *"my face is getting dark."* It is a **cosmetic and psychosocial disease**, and it disproportionately affects young to middle-aged women with brown skin — a population for whom facial dyspigmentation carries real social weight. This is why LPPig is both an exam favourite and a genuine clinic challenge.

---

## 2. Definition — Said Three Ways

Because definitions stick better when you hear them from different angles:

1. **Formal:** LPPig is a rare, chronic, acquired **macular pigmentary variant of lichen planus**, characterised by dark brown to slate-grey macules and patches, typically on the face, neck and flexures, in the *absence* of the classical violaceous papules.
2. **Mechanistic:** It is **acquired dermal macular hyperpigmentation** resulting from lichenoid interface damage with heavy pigmentary incontinence.
3. **Bedside:** It is lichen planus that *left the pigment behind and took the rash with it.*

**Historical anchor:** first characterised by **Bhutani et al. (1974) in India**, which is your clue to the epidemiology that follows.

## 3. Who Gets It, and Why That Isn't a Coincidence

| Feature | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **Skin phototype** | Fitzpatrick **III–VI** — darker phototypes |
| **Ethnicity** | **Indians/South Asians**, East Asians, Middle Easterners, Hispanics, Latin Americans |
| **Sex** | **Females > males** (except the Blaschkoid/linear variant, where **males predominate**) |
| **Age** | Young to middle-aged adults; third to fifth decades; also perimenopausal women |

**Now, *why* darker skin?** This is worth reasoning through rather than memorising.

In Fitzpatrick I–II skin, each basal melanocyte holds relatively little melanin and transfers little to keratinocytes. If those cells die, there simply isn't much pigment to spill. You get transient pinkness, and it fades.

In Fitzpatrick IV–VI skin, the same basal unit is **loaded** with melanin. Kill it, and you release a large pigment payload into the papillary dermis — where macrophages eat it and hold it for a very long time.

> **Analogy:** imagine two water balloons, one filled with clear water and one filled with black ink. Pop both over a white carpet. Identical mechanical event; radically different aftermath. The immune attack in LPPig is the pin. Melanin content is what's in the balloon.

This single idea explains almost everything about LPPig: why it is a disease of brown skin, why it is so persistent, and why it is so hard to treat.

## 4. Triggers and Suspected Aetiologies

LPPig is formally **idiopathic**, but there is a consistent cast of suspects. Notice that most of them are things that either **irritate/sensitise the skin** or **deliver UV/oxidative stress** — i.e., anything that can tip a latent lichenoid tendency into an active interface attack.

| Category | Specific agents | Proposed mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Mustard oil** | Contains **allyl isothiocyanate** | A potent **irritant and photosensitiser**; widely used for scalp/body massage and cooking in South Asia |
| **Amla oil** | *Emblica officinalis* hair oil | Contact sensitisation; commonly used on scalp/face margins |
| **Cosmetics** | Fragrances, **henna**, **hair dyes (PPD)**, kumkum, bindi adhesives | Repeated low-grade contact allergy/photocontact reaction → lichenoid tissue reaction |
| **Ultraviolet light** | Chronic sun exposure | Explains the strong face/neck/forearm predilection; also links LPPig to actinic LP |
| **Metals** | Nickel and nickel-containing foods | Systemic/contact hypersensitivity; reported association |
| **Hormonal** | **Perimenopausal women** | Overlaps with the FFA/LPP hormonal hypothesis |
| **Genetic/constitutional** | Darker phototypes, Asian ancestry | Melanin load + possible HLA predisposition |
| **Infective (reported)** | Hepatitis C (inconsistent, weaker than for classical LP) | Immune dysregulation |

> ⚠️ **Correction to note (high-yield):** the compound in mustard oil is **allyl *iso*thiocyanate** (CH₂=CH–CH₂–**N=C=S**), *not* "allyl thiocyanate" (CH₂=CH–CH₂–**S–C≡N**). The "iso-" is not pedantry — the isothiocyanate group is the reactive electrophile responsible for the pungency, irritancy and photosensitising behaviour of mustard oils. Writing it without the "iso" names a chemically distinct molecule.

> **Practical teaching point:** always take a **cosmetic and hair-oil history** in a patient with facial dermal pigmentation. Ask specifically: *"What do you apply to your hair and face? Who applies it? How often? Do you go in the sun afterwards?"* Mustard oil applied to the scalp runs onto the forehead and temples — precisely the classic LPPig distribution.

## 5. Pathogenesis — The Cause → Effect Chain

Let me walk this as a sequence. Each step causes the next.

**Step 1 — Antigenic trigger.**
A contact allergen, photoproduct, or self-antigen altered by UV appears on/near basal keratinocytes and melanocytes. In genetically predisposed people, this alters the antigenic profile of the basal layer.

**Step 2 — CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell recruitment.**
Antigen-presenting cells present the altered antigen; **CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes** home to the dermo-epidermal junction. They accumulate in a **band-like (lichenoid)** array hugging the junction. This is the defining immunological signature shared by all LP variants.

**Step 3 — Apoptosis of the basal layer.**
The T cells release perforin/granzyme and engage Fas/FasL. Basal keratinocytes **and basal melanocytes** undergo apoptosis. Histologically you see **vacuolar (hydropic) degeneration** of the basal layer and scattered apoptotic bodies (Civatte bodies).

**Step 4 — Pigmentary incontinence (the pivotal step).**
Here is the heart of the disease. Melanocytes normally hand melanosomes to basal keratinocytes; melanin then travels *upward* with the maturing epidermis and is shed. But when the basal layer is destroyed, the basement membrane is focally breached and melanin **falls downward** into the papillary dermis instead.

> **Analogy:** the basement membrane is the floor of a warehouse where ink barrels are stacked. Normally barrels travel upward on a conveyor and out of the building. Blow a hole in the floor and the barrels drop into the basement — where there is no conveyor out.

**Step 5 — Melanophage formation and pigment retention.**
Dermal macrophages phagocytose the free melanin, becoming **melanophages**. Dermal clearance of melanin is *slow* — it depends on macrophage transit to lymphatics, which takes **months to years**. This is why the pigmentation long outlives the inflammation.

**Step 6 — Abortive regression.**
The infiltrate then wanes. The epidermis, never having had a prolonged stimulus, does **not** become acanthotic or hyperkeratotic — it may even become **atrophic**. So you are left with a flat, non-palpable, slate-grey macule: inflammation gone, evidence permanent.

**The one-sentence pathogenesis:**
> *A brief CD8-mediated lichenoid assault on the basal layer dumps melanin into the dermis, where melanophages hold it hostage for years.*

## 6. Clinical Features

### Morphology
- **Macules and patches** — flat, *non-palpable*, **not** papules. If you feel a raised, violaceous, polygonal papule, you have drifted into classical LP.
- **Colour:** slate-grey, blue-grey, dark brown to brownish-black. The greyish hue is a **Tyndall/Rayleigh scattering effect** — melanin sitting deep in the dermis reflects shorter (blue) wavelengths, so deep pigment looks grey/blue while superficial epidermal pigment looks brown. *Any grey-blue pigmentation should make you think "dermal."*
- **Borders:** ill-defined, blending imperceptibly into normal skin — **no raised or erythematous rim** (this is a crucial differentiating point, see §9).
- **Size/shape:** small, round to oval macules that **coalesce** into large confluent or reticulate patches.
- **Symptoms:** usually **asymptomatic**; mild pruritus or burning in up to a third. The absence of symptoms is itself diagnostically useful.
- **Course:** **progressive**, with waves of new lesions; remissions and exacerbations.

### Distribution — Two Competing Patterns
This is the elegant part of LPPig. It has **two apparently contradictory favourite sites**, and both are explained by a "low-grade repeated insult" model:

1. **Sun-exposed skin** — **face** (forehead, temples, preauricular, malar "mask" area), **neck**, upper limbs, upper back/V-area. Driver: **UV + photosensitising topicals**.
2. **Flexures** — **axillae, inguinal/groin folds, submammary, cubital fossae**. Driver: **friction, occlusion, sweat, maceration**. This is the *inversus* pattern.

Also involved: trunk, extremities. **Mucosae, palms, soles and nails are characteristically spared** — a very useful negative (unlike classical LP, which loves mucosa and nails). However, patients may *separately* have oral LP as an association.

## 7. Clinical Variants (Know These by Name)

| Variant | Distribution | Distinguishing points |
|---|---|---|
| **LPPig classicus** | Face, neck, forearms — sun-exposed | Commonest; female predominance; brown-grey confluent patches |
| **LPPig inversus** | **Flexures only** (axillae, groins, inframammary), *sparing* sun-exposed sites | Argues against UV as the sole cause; **friction/occlusion**-driven; often smaller, more discrete, better demarcated macules |
| **Linear / Blaschkoid / zosteriform** | Follows **lines of Blaschko** or a dermatome | Attributed to **cutaneous somatic mosaicism** — a clone of keratinocytes carrying a susceptibility mutation is targeted, so the disease traces the embryonic migration lines of that clone. **Males > females**; often younger |
| **Generalised / diffuse** | Widespread trunk and limbs | Extensive, may mimic drug-induced pigmentation |
| **Reticular** | Net-like/reticulate pigmentation | Mimics Riehl's melanosis and prurigo pigmentosa pattern |
| **Perifollicular / dotted** | Brown dots around follicular ostia | Overlaps with LPP/FFA spectrum |

> **Why the Blaschkoid variant is conceptually important:** it demonstrates that the *target*, not just the *trigger*, can be spatially restricted. The immune system is behaving normally; it is the skin that is genetically patchy. Same principle as segmental vitiligo or lichen striatus.

## 8. Associations — and the Logic Behind Them

LPPig does not travel alone. Its companions cluster into two meaningful groups:

**A. Other lichenoid diseases (shared immunopathology — same T-cell attack, different target):**
- **Lichen planopilaris (LPP)** — target = follicular stem cells → scarring alopecia
- **Frontal fibrosing alopecia (FFA)** — a patterned LPP of the frontal hairline; **LPPig of the face/temples very often coexists with FFA**, sometimes preceding it
- **Oral lichen planus**
- **Classical cutaneous LP**, **actinic LP**

> ⚠️ **Correction:** source text reads *"FFP"* — there is no dermatological entity "FFP" in this context (FFP = fresh frozen plasma). The intended association is **FFA — frontal fibrosing alopecia.** This is a genuinely high-yield pairing: in a perimenopausal woman with grey-brown temporal pigmentation, **examine the hairline and eyebrows.**

**B. Autoimmune comorbidities (shared immune dysregulation):**
- **Vitiligo** (the striking paradox: pigment loss and pigment retention in the same patient — both are melanocyte-directed autoimmunity, differing in whether the melanocyte is destroyed outright or destroyed after unloading its pigment)
- **Hypothyroidism / autoimmune thyroid disease** — worth a TSH
- **Lupus erythematosus** (including LP–LE overlap)
- Reported: diabetes, dyslipidaemia, nephrotic syndrome, HCV

## 9. Differential Diagnosis — The Core of the Answer

This is where marks and clinical credibility are won. Let me first give you a **mental algorithm**, then the comparative table.

### The three questions that organise all dermal pigmentation

**Q1 — Is the pigment epidermal or dermal?**
Use **Wood's lamp**: epidermal pigment (melasma, PIH, lentigines) **accentuates** under UV; dermal pigment (LPPig, ashy dermatosis, ADM, ochronosis) **does not**. Dermoscopy and biopsy confirm.

**Q2 — Was there visible inflammation before the pigment, and where?**
- Inflammation present, face/flexures, brown-grey → **LPPig**
- Inflammation with a **raised erythematous rim**, trunk/proximal limbs → **ashy dermatosis/EDP**
- No inflammation at all, trunk, child → **IEMP**
- Inflammation at *the same spot* every time a drug is taken → **FDE**

**Q3 — Is the pigment even melanin?**
- Homogentisic acid polymer → **exogenous ochronosis**
- Amyloid → **macular amyloidosis**
- Drug–melanin/iron complexes → **drug-induced pigmentation** (minocycline, amiodarone, antimalarials, clofazimine, imipramine, chemotherapeutics)

### Comparative table

| Differential | Typical patient & site | Morphology clue | Histology/investigation clue | How to separate from LPPig |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ashy dermatosis / Erythema dyschromicum perstans (EDP)** | Latin American, Asian; **trunk, proximal limbs, neck** | Blue-grey (ashen) macules; in the **active phase a characteristic slightly raised erythematous border** | Vacuolar interface change + melanophages — **nearly identical to LPPig** | The decisive points are **clinical**: EDP has an **active erythematous advancing rim** and **tends to spare the face and flexures**; LPPig is **borderless** and **favours face + flexures**. Many authors regard them as a spectrum — say so, and you sound informed |
| **Riehl's melanosis (pigmented contact dermatitis)** | Women using cosmetics/fragrances; **forehead, temples, malar, lateral neck** | Brown-grey **reticulate/patchy** pigment with **follicular accentuation**; often preceded by mild erythema/itch | Lichenoid interface change + melanophages (again similar) | **History of a specific cosmetic/fragrance** and a **positive patch/photopatch test**; pigment often fades on strict allergen withdrawal, whereas LPPig persists. Conceptually LPPig and Riehl's overlap heavily |
| **Exogenous ochronosis** | Prolonged **hydroquinone** (or phenol/resorcinol) use, often for melasma; malar/zygomatic | **Blue-black speckled/caviar-like** pigmentation, sometimes with pinpoint papules; sharply confined to treated area | **Banana-shaped, ochre-coloured refractile deposits** in the dermis; dermoscopy shows blue-grey amorphous globules obliterating the pigment network | Drug/cosmetic history + the **pathognomonic banana bodies**. No lichenoid infiltrate |
| **Fixed drug eruption (FDE)** | Any age; lips, genitals, trunk, acral | **Round/oval, well-demarcated** violaceous-to-slate-brown patch; **recurs at the identical site** with each re-exposure; may blister | Interface dermatitis with **necrotic keratinocytes, eosinophils, and deep as well as superficial infiltrate**; abundant melanophages | **Temporality + site fidelity** to a drug (NSAIDs, cotrimoxazole, tetracyclines, fluconazole). LPPig lesions are multiple, ill-defined and not drug-locked |
| **Idiopathic eruptive macular pigmentation (IEMP)** | **Children/adolescents**; trunk, neck, proximal limbs | Asymptomatic brown macules appearing **de novo**, **no preceding inflammation**; often **self-resolving over months–years** | **Basal layer hyperpigmentation ± mild melanophages, with essentially NO interface dermatitis** | The absence of interface change is decisive; also the young age and spontaneous resolution (LPPig is chronic/progressive) |
| **Acrokeratosis paraneoplastica of Bazex** | Older men, smokers/drinkers | **Violaceous, psoriasiform, hyperkeratotic plaques** on **ears, nose, fingers, toes**, with nail dystrophy | Non-specific psoriasiform/hyperkeratotic histology; **search for upper aerodigestive tract SCC** | It is **palpable, scaly and acral** — not a flat facial/flexural macule. Included here chiefly because its **violaceous acral plaques mimic lichen planus** generally; a mandatory malignancy work-up makes it unmissable |

### Additional differentials worth adding (frequently expected)

| Entity | Discriminating feature |
|---|---|
| **Melasma** | **Epidermal/mixed**, symmetric centrofacial/malar/mandibular, **brown not grey**, accentuates on Wood's lamp, no interface dermatitis; hormonal/pregnancy link |
| **Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH)** | Conforms exactly to the footprint of an identifiable prior dermatosis (acne, eczema, lichenoid drug eruption); non-progressive; fades |
| **Acquired dermal melanocytosis / Hori's naevus (ADM)** | Bilateral blue-grey **speckled** malar macules in Asian women; histology shows **dermal melanocytes** (not melanophages) — the key distinction |
| **Macular amyloidosis** | **Rippled/ripple-patterned** brown pigment on upper back/extensors, **itchy**, history of friction/scrubbing; **Congo red / thioflavin T positive** amyloid in papillary dermis |
| **Drug-induced pigmentation** | Minocycline (blue-grey shins, scars, sclerae), amiodarone (slate-grey photo-exposed), antimalarials, clofazimine, imipramine, chemotherapy; **bilateral, diffuse, drug history**; often pigment granules/iron on stains |
| **Addison's disease / haemochromatosis** | Generalised bronze pigmentation with **mucosal, palmar crease and scar** accentuation; systemic features; endocrine/iron studies |
| **Actinic lichen planus (LP actinicus)** | Annular/dyschromic plaques on sun-exposed skin in young Middle Eastern/Indian patients — **palpable, annular, with a hypopigmented rim**; spring/summer flares. Sits on a continuum with LPPig |
| **Ochronosis (endogenous, alkaptonuria)** | Systemic — ear cartilage, sclerae, arthropathy, dark urine |
| **Prurigo pigmentosa** | Intensely **pruritic** reticulate pigmentation on trunk/back; ketosis/dieting association; neutrophilic then lichenoid histology |

> **The clean exam sentence:** *"In an adult with brown skin presenting with grey-brown, ill-defined, asymptomatic macules on the face and flexures, LPPig is the leading diagnosis; the principal mimics are ashy dermatosis (raised erythematous active border, trunk, spares face/flexures) and Riehl's melanosis (cosmetic history, follicular accentuation, positive patch test), both of which share near-identical histology — so the distinction is predominantly clinical and historical."*

## 10. Investigations

**A. Bedside**
- **Wood's lamp** — pigment does **not** accentuate → confirms a **dermal** location.
- **Dermoscopy** — brown to blue-grey **dots, globules and granules** in a diffuse, patchy or reticular/pseudonetwork pattern; may show perifollicular accentuation; obliteration of the normal pigment network. (Compare: melasma → accentuated pseudonetwork; ochronosis → blue-grey amorphous globules; ADM → deep blue-grey structureless areas.)
- **Patch and photopatch testing** — when contact/photocontact cause is suspected (essential if Riehl's melanosis is in the differential).

**B. Histopathology (the confirmatory test)**

Read the biopsy as a **narrative of a fire that has already gone out**:

| Level | Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Stratum corneum** | Mild hyperkeratosis or normal | Minimal — no time for a hypertrophic response |
| **Epidermis** | **Atrophy/thinning**; acanthosis and hypergranulosis are **absent or minimal** | Distinguishes LPPig from classical LP |
| **Basal layer** | **Vacuolar (hydropic) degeneration**, apoptotic/Civatte bodies, focal basement-membrane damage | The active lesion — the "pin in the balloon" |
| **Papillary dermis** | **Sparse-to-moderate band-like (lichenoid) / perivascular, sometimes perifollicular lymphocytic infiltrate** | Note: **sparser** than in classical LP — consistent with the "abortive" concept |
| **Papillary dermis** | **Abundant melanin-laden melanophages** — the **hallmark** | Pigmentary incontinence; the durable stain |
| **Special stains** | Fontana–Masson **positive** (melanin); **Congo red negative** (excludes amyloidosis); no banana bodies (excludes ochronosis) | Rules out mimics |
| **Direct immunofluorescence** | **Globular / cytoid-body deposits of IgM** (± IgA, C3, fibrinogen) at the **DEJ and papillary dermis** | Non-specific but supportive; represents immunoglobulin trapped in apoptotic colloid bodies — the same finding as in classical LP |

> ⚠️ **Correction:** the source line *"Immunofluorescence — epidermal atrophy and hyperkeratosis"* conflates two things. Epidermal atrophy and hyperkeratosis are **routine H&E (light-microscopic) findings**, not immunofluorescence findings. The genuine **DIF** finding is **globular/colloid-body IgM deposition** at the DEJ and papillary dermis. Keep the two columns separate.

> **Histological pearl to memorise:** *Atrophic epidermis + basal vacuolisation + sparse lichenoid infiltrate + heavy papillary-dermal melanophages = LPPig.* Contrast with classical LP, which is *acanthosis + hypergranulosis + saw-toothing + dense band-like infiltrate*. Same immunology, opposite epidermal reaction.

## 11. Management — and Why It Is Genuinely Difficult

Understand the therapeutic problem first, and the drug list becomes logical.

You are fighting on **two fronts** that respond to entirely different strategies:

- **Front 1 — the active inflammation** (ongoing basal-cell killing, producing *new* pigment). This is **treatable** and is where anti-inflammatories work. Your realistic goal is **arrest of progression**.
- **Front 2 — the pigment already deposited in the dermis** inside melanophages, behind an intact basement membrane. Topical lighteners work at the *epidermal* level and therefore barely reach it. This resolves only by **slow macrophage clearance over months to years**.

> **Therefore, counsel the patient honestly at visit one:** *"Treatment can stop this from spreading and fading of existing pigment will be slow and partial."* Failure to set this expectation is the commonest cause of treatment "failure" in LPPig.

### A. Trigger avoidance and photoprotection — non-negotiable foundation
- **Stop mustard oil, amla oil** and all hair oils applied to scalp/face margins.
- Withdraw suspect **cosmetics, fragrances, hair dyes (PPD), henna**; patch-test-guided avoidance.
- **Broad-spectrum sunscreen SPF ≥50 with high UVA (PA+++/++++) protection**, ideally **tinted** (iron oxides) to block **visible light**, which is a potent driver of pigmentation in dark skin. Reapply; add hats and shade.
- Reduce **friction/occlusion** in the inversus variant (loose clothing, control sweating).

### B. Topical therapy
| Drug class | Agents | Rationale / caveats |
|---|---|---|
| **Topical corticosteroids** | Medium-to-high potency (e.g., mometasone, betamethasone valerate; clobetasol for short flexural courses) | Suppress the T-cell infiltrate. **Limit duration on the face** — risk of atrophy, telangiectasia, steroid rosacea, and paradoxically worse dyschromia |
| **Topical calcineurin inhibitors** | **Tacrolimus 0.1%**, pimecrolimus 1% | **Preferred for face and flexures**; steroid-sparing; inhibit T-cell activation without atrophy. Mainstay of long-term maintenance |
| **Topical retinoids** | Tretinoin 0.025–0.1%, adapalene | Accelerate epidermal turnover and pigment dispersal; mildly anti-inflammatory. Caution: irritant retinoid dermatitis can itself cause PIH — start low, go slow |
| **Depigmenting agents (adjunct, limited benefit)** | Hydroquinone-containing/triple combination, azelaic acid, kojic acid, arbutin, cysteamine, niacinamide | Act mainly on **epidermal** melanin → modest effect on dermal pigment. **Beware exogenous ochronosis** with prolonged hydroquinone |

> ⚠️ **Correction:** the source phrase *"medium-to-high potency CS (tacrolimus, retinoids and sunscreens)"* wrongly nests three separate drug classes inside "corticosteroids." **CS = corticosteroids** (clobetasol, betamethasone, mometasone); **tacrolimus = a macrolide calcineurin inhibitor**; **retinoids = vitamin A derivatives**; **sunscreens = photoprotective agents.** They differ in mechanism, side-effect profile and monitoring — never conflate them in an answer.

### C. Systemic therapy — for progressive, widespread or cosmetically disabling disease
| Agent | Typical use | Mechanism / notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Corticosteroids** | **Oral mini-pulse (OMP)** dexamethasone/betamethasone 5 mg on 2 consecutive days weekly; or low-dose prednisolone; intralesional TA for limited lesions | Rapidly aborts active inflammation while limiting cumulative steroid toxicity |
| **Dapsone** | 100 mg/day | Anti-inflammatory/anti-neutrophilic and immunomodulatory; check **G6PD**, monitor Hb, reticulocytes, methaemoglobin, LFTs |
| **Isotretinoin** | Low dose (e.g., 10–20 mg/day) | Normalises keratinisation, immunomodulatory, reduces lichenoid activity; teratogenic — contraception mandatory |
| **Hydroxychloroquine** | 200–400 mg/day (≤5 mg/kg/day) | Well-reported in LPPig and in the FFA/LPP spectrum; antimalarials blunt lichenoid interface inflammation. Baseline and annual **ophthalmic** review |
| **Tranexamic acid** (reported/adjunct) | Oral low dose | Reduces inflammation-driven melanogenesis and angiogenesis; screen thrombotic risk |
| **Others (reported)** | Methotrexate, ciclosporin, oral antioxidants (vitamin E, polypodium leucotomos) | Reserved/adjunctive; evidence limited |

### D. Procedural — use with great caution
- **Q-switched Nd:YAG, picosecond lasers, chemical peels** have been tried for residual dermal pigment. In Fitzpatrick IV–VI skin they carry a **real risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or paradoxical worsening**, because any injury re-triggers the very interface inflammation you are treating.
- **Rule:** never attempt pigment-directed procedures while the disease is **active**. Achieve ≥6 months of quiescence first, test-spot, and use conservative settings.

## 12. Prognosis and What to Tell the Patient

- **Chronic and slowly progressive**, with **remissions and exacerbations** over years.
- Pigmentation **persists for months to years after inflammation resolves**, because melanophage clearance is intrinsically slow — this is a *biological* limitation, not treatment failure.
- **No mortality risk, no malignant potential.** The morbidity is **psychosocial**: facial dyspigmentation in young women is associated with measurable impairment of quality of life. Address this explicitly; camouflage cosmetics are a legitimate and valuable therapeutic tool.
- **Realistic treatment goals:** (1) halt progression, (2) prevent new lesions, (3) allow slow spontaneous fading, (4) camouflage meanwhile.
- If FFA/LPP coexists, remember the **alopecia is scarring and irreversible** — that arm of the disease is far more urgent than the pigment, and early aggressive treatment matters.

## 13. Common Misunderstandings — Let Me Clear These Up

1. **"LPPig is just post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation after lichen planus."**
   No. In PIH there *was* an antecedent, visible, resolved dermatosis; in LPPig the pigmentation **is** the presenting disease, the interface inflammation is **ongoing and subclinical**, and the process is **progressive**. A biopsy of LPPig still shows **active vacuolar interface change** — that is the whole point.

2. **"If there are no violaceous papules, it can't be lichen planus."**
   The papule is a *reaction pattern of the epidermis*, not the disease. LPPig proves that the same lichenoid immunology can produce a purely macular outcome.

3. **"Ashy dermatosis and LPPig are clearly distinguishable on histology."**
   They are essentially **indistinguishable histologically**. Both show vacuolar interface change with melanophages. The separation rests on **clinical morphology and distribution** (active raised erythematous rim + truncal, face/flexure-sparing = EDP). Many authorities consider them one spectrum. Say this — it is the sophisticated answer.

4. **"Hydroquinone will clear it."**
   Hydroquinone inhibits tyrosinase in **epidermal** melanocytes. LPPig pigment is **dermal, inside macrophages**. Expect minimal benefit, and remember prolonged use can cause **exogenous ochronosis** — converting one pigmentary problem into a worse, harder-to-treat one.

5. **"Laser the pigment away."**
   Premature or aggressive laser in active LPPig or in dark skin frequently **worsens** pigmentation. Quiescence first, test spot always.

6. **"Grey means dermal, brown means epidermal — always."**
   A good heuristic, not a law. Mixed depth is common, and confluent LPPig can look frankly brown. Use Wood's lamp/dermoscopy/biopsy, not colour alone.

7. **"Mucosal and nail involvement supports LPPig."**
   LPPig itself **spares** mucosa, palms, soles and nails. Mucosal disease reflects **coexisting oral LP**, which is an *association*, not a feature of the pigmented lesions.

## 14. Summary Anchor — The Core Idea in One Picture

Picture a **brown-skinned face in the sun, with mustard oil on the hairline.**

A quiet squad of **CD8+ T cells** slips along the **dermo-epidermal junction** and kills the **pigment-loaded basal cells**. The battle is short — so short the epidermis never thickens, and the patient never notices a rash. But the **floor of the epidermis is breached**, and a heavy load of **melanin falls into the papillary dermis**, where **macrophages swallow it and sit still for years**.

What you see is therefore not inflammation but its **shadow**: flat, ill-defined, **slate-grey to brown macules** on the **face and flexures**, asymptomatic, slowly spreading.

Because the pigment is **dermal**, it does not light up under Wood's lamp, does not yield to bleaching creams, and does not disappear when the inflammation stops.

So the whole of management follows from the mechanism:
> **Remove the trigger, shield from light, quench the T cells (tacrolimus/steroids/OMP/dapsone/isotretinoin/HCQ), and then wait — because the melanophages keep their own slow calendar.**

And the whole of the differential diagnosis follows from three questions:
> **Is the pigment dermal? Was there inflammation, and with what kind of border and where? And is the pigment even melanin?**

Answer those three, and ashy dermatosis, Riehl's melanosis, exogenous ochronosis, FDE, IEMP, melasma, ADM, macular amyloidosis and drug-induced pigmentation sort themselves out almost automatically.
