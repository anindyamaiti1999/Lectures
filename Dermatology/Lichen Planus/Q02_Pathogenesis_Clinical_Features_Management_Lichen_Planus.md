# Lichen Planus — A Mini-Lecture

*(Structured to answer: "Briefly discuss the pathogenesis, clinical features, and management of lichen planus." Sources: Rook's, Bolognia, Andrews'. Corrections to the source notes are flagged in-line with ⚠️.)*

---

## 1. The Big Picture: What Are We Actually Looking At?

Let me start with the single most useful idea in this whole topic.

**Lichen planus is not primarily a disease of the skin surface. It is a disease of the *floor* of the epidermis.**

Picture the epidermis as a multi-storey building. The ground floor is the **basal cell layer** — the layer of stem-like keratinocytes sitting on the basement membrane, which is the concrete slab separating epidermis (ectoderm) from dermis (mesoderm). In lichen planus, the immune system decides that the residents of the ground floor are enemies, and sends in assassins — cytotoxic T cells — to kill them one by one.

Everything else in the disease — the flat-topped shape of the papule, the white lacy lines, the intense itch, the grey-brown pigmentation that lingers for years, the scarring alopecia, the destroyed nails, the oral erosions — is downstream fallout from that one event: **immune-mediated apoptosis of basal keratinocytes.**

**Why does this matter clinically?**

1. Because the target is the basal layer, which contains the **stem cell reservoir** and **melanocytes**, damage in certain sites is *permanent*: destroyed hair follicle stem cells → scarring alopecia; destroyed nail matrix → pterygium and anonychia; melanocyte damage → long-lasting pigmentation.
2. Because the target tissue is **ectodermally derived**, the disease can appear anywhere ectoderm is found — skin, oral mucosa, genital mucosa, nails, hair, oesophagus, conjunctiva, anus. This is why LP is a *multi-site* disease and why the source note describes it as "an inflammatory dermatosis affecting ectodermal-derived tissue."
3. Because chronic interface inflammation of mucosa is a **premalignant state**, erosive oral and genital LP carries a small but real risk of squamous cell carcinoma.

**Where it fits in dermatology:** LP is the prototype of the **"lichenoid tissue reaction" / "interface dermatitis"** family — a group of diseases that all attack the dermo-epidermal junction. Its cousins are lichenoid drug eruption, discoid lupus erythematosus, graft-versus-host disease, erythema multiforme, fixed drug eruption, lichen nitidus, lichen striatus, and pityriasis lichenoides. If you understand LP mechanistically, you understand the whole family, because they differ mainly in *what triggers* the attack and *how far down* the inflammation extends.

**Etymology (a nice mnemonic in itself):** *Lichen* = tree moss (the lesions look like flat moss/lichen stuck on bark); *planus* = flat. So "flat moss" — which is literally the clinical description.

**Epidemiology in one line:** affects roughly 0.2–1% of adults, peaks between 30 and 60 years, oral disease is commoner in women; mucosal involvement occurs in over half of patients with cutaneous LP, and about 20–25% have mucosal disease *only*.

## 2. Pathogenesis — The Cause-and-Effect Chain, Step by Step

The formal answer is: **LP is a T cell–mediated autoimmune disease in which CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes recognise an altered self-antigen on basal keratinocytes and kill them.** The precipitating antigen is **unknown** ("idiopathic"), which is why we call it autoimmune rather than autoimmune-to-a-named-protein.

Now let's walk the chain slowly, because the exam rewards mechanism, not a list.

### Step 1 — Antigen alteration (the "wanted poster" goes up)

Something modifies the surface of basal keratinocytes so that they display a **neo-antigen** — a peptide the immune system has never tolerated. The candidate culprits:

- **A virus** whose peptides are expressed by, or cross-react with, keratinocyte proteins (HCV, HBV, HHV-6, HHV-7, VZV).
- **A drug metabolite** that haptenises (chemically sticks to) a keratinocyte protein.
- **A metal ion** from dental amalgam (mercury, copper), or an arecoline/areca alkaloid from betel nut.
- **Physical injury** — trauma, scratching, radiotherapy — which both exposes hidden antigens and releases inflammatory signals.

*Analogy:* imagine a citizen who unknowingly pins an enemy flag to his own jacket. The security forces now see a uniform they are programmed to attack, even though the person underneath is innocent.

### Step 2 — Antigen presentation and T-cell recruitment

Langerhans cells and dendritic cells increase in number, pick up the altered antigen, and present it. Early in the lesion, **CD4⁺ helper T cells** dominate and secrete **IFN-γ** and **IL-2**, which license the cytotoxic arm.

⚠️ *The source's mention of **γδ T cells** is correct but should be understood as a minor accessory population; the dominant effectors are αβ CD8⁺ cells.*

### Step 3 — The IFN-γ / CXCR3 amplification loop (this is the engine of chronicity, and the key to modern therapy)

This is the part most notes skip, and it is worth memorising because it explains the JAK inhibitors.

IFN-γ binds keratinocyte IFN-γ receptors → signals through **JAK1/JAK2 → STAT1** → keratinocytes are forced to produce the chemokines **CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11**. These three are the specific "come here" signals for **CXCR3⁺ cytotoxic T cells**. Those recruited T cells arrive and secrete *more* IFN-γ.

*Analogy:* the keratinocytes are being coerced into broadcasting their own location to the hit squad. The more T cells arrive, the louder the broadcast. This **self-sustaining feed-forward loop** is why LP does not simply burn out once the original trigger is removed, and why blocking the JAK–STAT relay station (tofacitinib, baricitinib) can switch the whole circuit off.

### Step 4 — Killing the basal keratinocyte (three parallel weapons)

Once the CD8⁺ T cell docks onto the basal keratinocyte, apoptosis is triggered by:

1. **Perforin + granzyme B** — perforin drills a pore in the target membrane; granzyme B is injected through it and activates caspases. (Note: the source writes "granzymes (perforin)" — they are two *separate* molecules working as a pair, not synonyms.)
2. **Fas–FasL ligation** — the T cell's FasL engages Fas (CD95) on the keratinocyte, activating the death receptor pathway.
3. **TNF-α** binding TNF-R1.

Additional accessories: **matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9)** released by T cells degrades collagen IV in the basement membrane, which both lets lymphocytes enter the epidermis and mechanically weakens the dermo-epidermal junction. Mast cell degranulation (chymase, TNF-α) further amplifies the process.

### Step 5 — Consequences of basal cell death (this is where histology and clinical signs are *generated*)

Follow each consequence carefully — every clinical sign you must remember is born here:

| Event at the basal layer | Histological result | Clinical result |
|---|---|---|
| Apoptotic keratinocytes shrink, lose nuclei, become eosinophilic hyaline globules | **Civatte / colloid bodies** (PAS-positive, IgM-positive on DIF) | — (microscopic marker) |
| Basal layer is destroyed → epidermis loses its anchoring floor | **Basal cell (liquefactive/vacuolar) degeneration**; **Max-Joseph spaces** (subepidermal clefts) | If severe/confluent → **bullous LP**; on mucosa → **erosive LP** |
| Melanocytes sit in the basal layer and are collateral damage; melanin spills into dermis and is engulfed by macrophages | **Pigment incontinence + melanophages** in papillary dermis | **Grey-brown post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation** lasting months to years (marked in skin of colour → *LP pigmentosus*) |
| Dense lymphocytes pile up against the junction | **Band-like ("lichenoid") lymphocytic infiltrate** hugging the DEJ | Firm, **indurated/palpable** papule |
| Surviving epidermis reacts with compensatory thickening | **Acanthosis**, **irregular saw-toothed elongation of rete ridges** | Thickened, plateau-like papule top → **flat-topped (planar)** shape |
| Focal, wedge-shaped thickening of the granular layer | **Wedge-shaped hypergranulosis** | **Wickham's striae** — the thickened, keratin-dense wedges reflect light differently, appearing as fine white lacy lines |
| Dense compact keratin above | **Hyperkeratosis with orthokeratosis** | Shiny, dry surface |
| Dilated dermal vessels + thick overlying keratin | — | **Violaceous/purple** colour (deoxygenated blood viewed through a translucent hyperkeratotic layer — the same optics that make veins look blue) |
| Inflammatory mediators stimulate C-fibres | — | **Intense pruritus** |

⚠️ **Correction 1 — Parakeratosis.** The source table says "hyperkeratosis with ortho/parakeratosis." In **classic cutaneous LP the keratin is compact and ortho**keratotic (i.e., nuclei are absent). **Parakeratosis (retained nuclei) is *not* a feature of classic LP** — if you see it, think **lichenoid drug eruption**, LP–lupus overlap, or another lichenoid dermatosis. (Mucosal LP is the exception, since mucosa is normally non-keratinised — there parakeratosis may be seen.) Eosinophils and a deeper, perivascular infiltrate are the other pointers to a drug cause.

⚠️ **Correction 2 — DIF.** The source says "globular deposits of IgG, IgM around DEJ." Standardise this to:
- **Shaggy/linear fibrinogen band along the basement membrane zone** — the *hallmark* and most consistent DIF finding in LP.
- **Globular ("cytoid body") deposits of IgM** (less often IgA, C3) within colloid bodies at the DEJ.
- **IgG is not the characteristic immunoreactant** — if you find linear IgG at the BMZ, you are dealing with **lichen planus pemphigoides**, and if you find granular IgG/IgM/C3 in a lupus band, reconsider DLE.

### Step 6 — Genetic susceptibility

Not everyone exposed to the same trigger develops LP, because **HLA type determines which peptides you can present**. Reported associations: **HLA-A3, HLA-B5, HLA-B7, HLA-Bw15, HLA-DR1, HLA-DR5**. Familial LP is associated particularly with HLA-B7 and DR1.

⚠️ **Correction 3 —** the source's "**HLA-5**" is not valid nomenclature; it should read **HLA-B5** or **HLA-DR5**.

### Step 7 — The special case of lost immune privilege (why LP scars in some sites)

The **bulge region of the hair follicle** and the **nail matrix** are normally *immune-privileged* sanctuaries — they downregulate MHC class I and express local immunosuppressants precisely so that their precious stem cells are never seen by cytotoxic T cells. In **lichen planopilaris**, this privilege collapses (in part through PPAR-γ dysfunction in sebaceous/infundibular lipid metabolism). Once the stem cells in the bulge are killed, the follicle **cannot regenerate** — it is replaced by fibrous tract. Hence:

- **Lichen planopilaris → permanent, cicatricial (scarring) alopecia.** Treatment can only halt progression, never regrow lost hair. *This is the single most important counselling point in LP of the scalp.*
- **Nail matrix LP → dorsal pterygium and anonychia**, also permanent.

## 3. Triggers — and Why Each One Makes Mechanistic Sense

The source lists these; here is the logic behind each group.

**Infections**
- **Hepatitis C virus** — the strongest and best-documented association, particularly with **oral LP**, and especially in Mediterranean, Japanese and South American populations. HCV replicates in oral epithelium; HCV-specific CD4⁺/CD8⁺ clones have been recovered *from within LP lesions*. Mechanisms: molecular mimicry and local viral antigen presentation. **Practical point: consider HCV serology in patients with oral or widespread/erosive LP.**
- **HBV, HHV-6, HHV-7, VZV** — weaker associations; HHV-6/7 have been detected in lesional skin.
- **Hepatitis B vaccination** — reported to precipitate LP, presumably via the same antigenic mimicry/immune-activation route. (This is a rare, well-documented but *not* a reason to withhold vaccination.)

**Drugs (→ "lichenoid drug eruption," the great mimic)**
Remember the source list as groups: **H₂ blockers, NSAIDs, antibiotics, antihypertensives (ACE inhibitors, β-blockers, thiazides), anticonvulsants, diuretics, metals (gold, mercury, arsenic), antimalarials, antidepressants**, plus the modern biologics/targeted agents: **imatinib, IVIg, adalimumab, etanercept** (the paradoxical anti-TNF lichenoid eruptions), and — worth adding for current practice — **immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1/PD-L1)**, which are now a leading cause of lichenoid eruption. Mechanism: drug or metabolite acts as a **hapten** on keratinocyte proteins, or non-specifically lowers the threshold for cytotoxic attack (checkpoint inhibitors literally remove the brakes on CD8⁺ cells).
*Clinical clue to a drug cause:* longer latency (months), photodistribution, more eczematous/psoriasiform look, absent Wickham's striae, eosinophils and parakeratosis on biopsy, resolution on withdrawal (which may take weeks to months).

⚠️ *Note on the source text: the line-break "diuretics, met-/als" reads as* **metals**, *not "metformin."*

**Contact/local factors**
- **Dental amalgam** → *oral lichenoid contact reaction*, classically a lesion **topographically fitting the restoration** on the buccal mucosa or lateral tongue. This is a **delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity** to mercury/copper; patch testing may be positive, and **removal of the amalgam cures a substantial proportion**. This is one of the few genuinely *curable* forms.
- **Betel nut / areca quid chewing** → chronic chemical and mechanical mucosal injury.
- **Lichen planus–like contact dermatitis** — e.g. to colour film developers (para-phenylenediamine derivatives), dental materials, metals.

**Physical and psychological**
- **Trauma/scratching → Koebner phenomenon** (new lesions in lines of injury; explains linear LP and lesions in scratch marks).
- **Radiotherapy** — releases sequestered antigens and creates a pro-inflammatory field.
- **Anxiety, stress, depression** — bidirectional; stress can precipitate flares (neuropeptide-mediated), and the disease itself (itch, disfiguring pigmentation, painful oral erosions) causes psychological morbidity. Address both.

## 4. Clinical Features

### 4.1 The classic cutaneous lesion — the "6 Ps"

> **P**ruritic · **P**olygonal · **P**lanar (flat-topped) · **P**urple (violaceous) · **P**apules · **P**laques

Each P is a direct read-out of the histology, as tabled above — so if you forget the list, re-derive it from the pathology.

- **Primary lesion:** firm, shiny, flat-topped, polygonal papules, **1–3 mm** (coalescing into plaques), red-violaceous, surfaced by **Wickham's striae** — fine white lines in a lacy/reticulate network, best seen after applying oil, water, or under **dermoscopy**.
- **Pruritus** is typically intense, but patients characteristically **rub rather than scratch** (unlike eczema) — hence lesions are usually *not* excoriated, though rubbing may produce hypertrophic/lichenified LP on the shins.
- **Distribution:** symmetrical, favouring **flexor wrists and forearms, dorsa of hands, ankles and shins, presacral/lumbar region, and genitalia**. Scalp, nails and mucosae are separately involved.
- **Koebner phenomenon: positive** — a shared feature with psoriasis and vitiligo.

### 4.2 Configurations

- **Grouped** (the commonest pattern)
- **Annular** — ring-like with an active raised edge and atrophic/pigmented centre; the **commonest site is the penis (glans/shaft)**, also axillae and groin
- **Linear** — either along lines of trauma (Koebner) or genuinely **blaschkoid/zosteriform**

### 4.3 Mucosal LP — the part that causes the most morbidity

Present in **50–70%** of patients with cutaneous LP; **20–25%** have mucosal disease alone. Always look in the mouth and at the genitals, even when the patient does not complain.

**Oral LP — six patterns, worth knowing by name:**
1. **Reticular** — the commonest: **white lacy Wickham's striae**, classically on the **buccal mucosa bilaterally**; often asymptomatic.
2. **Papular** — small white papules.
3. **Plaque-like** — irregular, fixed white plaques, especially on the **dorsum of the tongue** (this is the source's "tongue: irregular fixed white plaques"). Must be distinguished from leukoplakia and candidiasis.
4. **Atrophic** — erythematous thinned mucosa; on gingiva presents as **desquamative gingivitis**.
5. **Erosive/ulcerative** — the symptomatic and dangerous form: painful erosions with a white striated border; **burning pain provoked by hot, spicy, acidic foods, alcohol and toothpaste (sodium lauryl sulphate)** — exactly the "burning discomfort with hot and spicy foods" in the source note. Leads to poor intake, weight loss, poor oral hygiene.
6. **Bullous** — rare.

**Genital LP**
- **Men:** annular or reticulate lesions on the glans; erosive forms can cause phimosis.
- **Women:** the **vulvovaginal-gingival syndrome (Hewitt–Pelisse)** — erosive vulvitis + desquamative gingivitis + vaginitis, leading to **agglutination of labia, loss of architecture, introital stenosis, dyspareunia, apareunia, vaginal stricture and discharge**. The male counterpart is the **peno-gingival syndrome**.
- Note the important overlap/confusion with **lichen sclerosus** (which is whiter, sclerotic, "cigarette-paper" and extends around the anus in a figure-of-eight; LP more often involves the vagina, which LS spares).

### 4.4 Pigmentary sequelae

As lesions resolve, they leave **greyish-brown to slate-grey macules** caused by **melanin deposited in the superficial dermis within melanophages** (pigment incontinence). Key teaching points:

- This is **not active disease** — it is a *scar of pigment*.
- It may persist for **months to years**, and is far more prominent and distressing in **Fitzpatrick IV–VI**.
- Topical steroids and bleaching agents work poorly because the pigment is **dermal**, not epidermal. Counselling and photoprotection matter more than prescriptions.
- When pigmentation is the *presenting* feature with minimal papules, the diagnosis is **LP pigmentosus** (face, neck, flexures) — differentials: Riehl's melanosis, ashy dermatosis, drug-induced pigmentation.

### 4.5 Follicular and appendageal LP

⚠️ **Correction 4 —** the source line "*Around hair follicles: **Spring** – KP: Lichen planopilaris*" is a garbled OCR artefact. The intended meaning is: **spiny/keratotic follicular papules resembling keratosis pilaris (KP) develop around hair follicles — this is lichen planopilaris.** ("Spring" is not a dermatological term; read it as "spiny.")

**Lichen planopilaris (LPP)** — clinically: perifollicular erythema and perifollicular scale/keratotic spines at the periphery of expanding patches, **loss of follicular ostia** centrally, pain/burning/itch, positive anagen hair pull at the active edge, and **irreversible scarring (cicatricial) alopecia** of the scalp. Two important variants:
- **Frontal fibrosing alopecia (FFA)** — band-like recession of the frontotemporal hairline with **eyebrow loss** and lonely surviving hairs; strongly female, postmenopausal.
- **Graham-Little-Piccardi-Lassueur syndrome** — the classic triad:
 1. **Multifocal cicatricial scalp alopecia**
 2. **Non-scarring alopecia of the axillae and groin**
 3. **Keratotic/spinous lichenoid follicular papules** on trunk and limbs

### 4.6 Nail LP (~10–15% of patients; may be isolated)

Mechanism determines the sign: **nail plate thinning** and **longitudinal ridging/fissuring/splitting**, **trachyonychia (rough sandpaper nails; if all twenty are affected → "20-nail dystrophy")**, **pitting**, **onycholysis**, **subungual hyperkeratosis**, **longitudinal melanonychia**, and — when the **matrix** is destroyed — **dorsal pterygium** (the "butterfly wings" of proximal nail fold fusing to the bed) and **anonychia** (permanent nail loss). **Idiopathic atrophy of the nails** is a rapid, severe variant. Because matrix destruction is irreversible, nail LP is a **therapeutic emergency** — treat early and aggressively.

⚠️ **Correction 5 —** "longitudinal nails" in the source should read **longitudinal ridging** (or longitudinal splitting) of the nails.

### 4.7 Complications and the malignancy question

⚠️ **Correction 6 —** the source's complication columns appear shuffled ("Nails: ... squamous cell carcinoma, cicatricial conjunctivitis, lacrimal canalicular obstruction"; "Mucosa: SUHK, longitudinal nails, and pterygium"). The medically correct allocation is:

| Site | True complications |
|---|---|
| **Hair/scalp** | Lichen planopilaris → **permanent scarring alopecia** |
| **Nails** | 20-nail dystrophy/trachyonychia, pitting, longitudinal ridging, longitudinal melanonychia, **pterygium**, anonychia |
| **Mucosa (oral/genital/oesophageal)** | **Squamous cell carcinoma**, oesophageal **strictures/dysphagia**, vaginal/introital stenosis, phimosis |
| **Eye** | **Cicatricial conjunctivitis**, lacrimal canalicular obstruction, punctal stenosis |

⚠️ *"SUHK"* is not a recognised abbreviation; given the column shuffle it most plausibly stands for **SCC (squamous cell carcinoma)** under mucosa, but this should be verified against the original page rather than assumed.

**The malignancy point (high-yield):** **erosive and atrophic oral LP** carries a **malignant transformation risk of roughly 0.5–2% over about 10 years** (WHO classes oral LP as a potentially malignant disorder). Highest risk: erosive/ulcerative type, **tongue** lesions, smokers, alcohol users, betel chewers, HCV-positive patients. Therefore: **document, photograph, biopsy any non-healing or indurated area, and schedule long-term surveillance (6–12 monthly).** Vulval and penile erosive LP carry an analogous, smaller risk.

### 4.8 Associations (immune "company it keeps")

**Alopecia areata, vitiligo, ulcerative colitis, dermatomyositis, morphea, lichen sclerosus, paraneoplastic pemphigus, diabetes mellitus**, autoimmune thyroid disease, myasthenia gravis/thymoma, primary biliary cholangitis, and chronic GVHD (which can be clinically indistinguishable). The Grinspan triad (oral LP + diabetes + hypertension) is classically quoted but is probably confounded by antihypertensive/antidiabetic lichenoid drug reactions — a nice illustration of how association is not causation.

### 4.9 Variants — a quick tour with the logic

| Variant | Defining feature / logic |
|---|---|
| **Hypertrophic (verrucous) LP** | Thick, warty plaques on **shins/ankles**; result of chronic rubbing; most pruritic and most resistant; long-term risk of SCC within lesions |
| **Atrophic LP** | Few, resolving lesions with central atrophy; often post-annular |
| **Actinic LP (lichen planus tropicus)** | Sun-exposed face/neck/dorsal hands in young people of Middle Eastern/Indian/African descent; annular plaques with hypopigmented rim — mimics melasma/DLE |
| **LP pigmentosus** | Pigment-dominant, face/flexures; pigment incontinence outweighs papulation |
| **LP of palms and soles** | Firm yellowish hyperkeratotic papules/plaques, often painful, Wickham's striae absent → mimics psoriasis/callus/warts |
| **Annular LP** | Ring with active edge; **penis** commonest |
| **Linear LP** | Koebner or blaschkoid |
| **Guttate LP** | Small widely scattered papules |
| **Bullous LP** | Blisters **arising within** existing LP lesions where basal damage is so confluent that Max-Joseph clefts coalesce (this is the source's "with confluence of lesions") |
| **LP pemphigoides** | A *distinct* entity: blisters on **normal** as well as lesional skin, because basal damage exposes BP180/BP230 → **secondary autoantibody formation** (epitope spreading). DIF shows **linear IgG + C3 at BMZ**; salt-split skin shows epidermal binding. Requires bullous-disease treatment |
| **Lichen nitidus** | Tiny pinhead, shiny, skin-coloured papules; histology = well-circumscribed "**ball-in-claw**" granulomatous infiltrate — related to but distinct from LP |
| **Keratosis lichenoides chronica (Nekam disease)** | Rare, chronic, symmetrical keratotic papules in linear/reticulate arrays with seborrhoeic-like facial dermatitis |
| **LP–DLE overlap** | Features of both, often photodistributed on hands/scalp; histology and DIF are intermediate |
| **LP/lupus, LP/GVHD, drug-induced LP** | The mimic group — always in the differential |
| **Rare sites** | **Conjunctival, oesophageal, perianal** LP — remember to *ask* about dysphagia and red eyes |

## 5. Histopathology — Reading the Slide as a Story

If you are shown a slide, narrate it **top to bottom**; each feature is an inevitable consequence of the mechanism.

1. **Stratum corneum:** compact **hyperkeratosis with orthokeratosis** (⚠️ *not* parakeratosis in classic LP).
2. **Granular layer:** **focal wedge-shaped hypergranulosis** → the histological substrate of **Wickham's striae**.
3. **Epidermis:** **acanthosis** with **irregular, saw-toothed elongation of the rete ridges** — the "saw-tooth" appearance is caused by uneven basal destruction and reactive elongation.
4. **Basal layer:** **squamatisation** (basal cells lose their columnar shape and look flat/squamoid), **vacuolar/liquefactive degeneration**, and **apoptotic keratinocytes** = **Civatte / colloid / hyaline bodies** — homogeneous, eosinophilic, rounded, often in clusters; **PAS-positive** and remarkably **resistant to degradation**, which is why they persist. As they aggregate they may be extruded through the epidermis (**transepidermal elimination**).
5. **Dermo-epidermal junction:** artefactual/true subepidermal separation → **Max-Joseph spaces**.
6. **Papillary dermis:** **dense band-like ("lichenoid") lymphohistiocytic infiltrate** hugging the junction, with **melanophages** and **pigment incontinence**.
7. **DIF:** **shaggy fibrinogen at the BMZ** (hallmark) + **globular/cytoid IgM (± IgA, C3)** in colloid bodies (⚠️ correction as above; IgG is not typical).

**Negative features that argue against LP:** eosinophils, plasma cells, deep perivascular/periadnexal extension, parakeratosis, follicular plugging, thickened basement membrane on PAS, mucin in the reticular dermis — these steer you toward **lichenoid drug eruption** or **DLE**.

## 6. Investigations

- **Clinical diagnosis** in typical cases — Wickham's striae + 6 Ps + flexor distribution + oral lacy striae is usually enough.
- **Dermoscopy:** whitish **Wickham striae** in reticular/radial/annular patterns, with peripheral dotted/linear vessels; on scalp in LPP — loss of follicular openings, **perifollicular scale and erythema**, "target-like" blue-grey dots.
- **Histopathology (HPE)** — confirmatory; mandatory for atypical, hypertrophic, ulcerated or long-standing mucosal lesions (to exclude SCC).
- **DIF** — when bullous LP, LP pemphigoides, mucosal erosive disease, or desquamative gingivitis needs separating from pemphigus vulgaris/mucous membrane pemphigoid.
- **Blood work:** CBC, urine routine/microscopy (URM), **LFT**, ESR, CRP; plus **HCV (and HBV) serology**, fasting glucose/HbA1c, thyroid function where indicated. LFTs matter twice over — as a screen for hepatitis, and as a baseline before methotrexate/acitretin.
- **Patch testing** to metals/dental series when amalgam-related oral lichenoid lesions are suspected.
- **Referrals:** dentist (oral hygiene, amalgam), gynaecologist/urologist (genital erosive disease), ophthalmologist (ocular symptoms), gastroenterologist (dysphagia → oesophageal LP).

## 7. Management

### 7.1 Principles before prescriptions

1. **Set expectations honestly.** Limited cutaneous LP is often **self-limiting over 1–2 years**. Oral (especially erosive), genital, nail and scalp LP are **chronic and relapsing**. Treatment controls inflammation; it does **not** reverse scarring alopecia, pterygium or dermal pigmentation.
2. **Remove the trigger.** Review every drug (especially the classes listed above); consider amalgam replacement for lesions in direct contact with restorations; stop betel/tobacco/alcohol; treat HCV (direct-acting antivirals may improve associated LP).
3. **Symptom control matters as much as clearance:** sedating antihistamines at night for itch, emollients, avoidance of irritants (spicy/acidic food, SLS-containing toothpaste, soaps on genital skin), topical anaesthetic (2% viscous lignocaine) before meals in erosive oral LP.
4. **Match the aggressiveness of treatment to the risk of permanent damage.** Trunk papules can wait; **nail matrix, scalp and erosive mucosal disease cannot.**
5. **Surveil for malignancy** in erosive oral/genital LP and in long-standing hypertrophic lesions.

### 7.2 First line

| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Limited cutaneous LP** | **Very potent topical corticosteroid**, e.g. **clobetasol propionate 0.05%** BD, ± occlusion; on thick shin lesions use occlusion or **intralesional triamcinolone acetonide 5–10 mg/mL** every 3–4 weeks |
| **Widespread cutaneous LP** | **Oral prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day** for 2–4 weeks, then taper over 4–6 weeks. Fast, reliable, but *relapse on withdrawal is common* — so plan a steroid-sparing agent early |
| **Oral LP** | High-potency topical steroid in an adhesive/mucosal vehicle (clobetasol/fluocinonide in Orabase, betamethasone or dexamethasone mouth rinse), **topical tacrolimus 0.1%** for refractory erosions, intralesional triamcinolone for isolated ulcers; **prophylactic antifungal** cover because topical steroids in the mouth precipitate candidiasis |
| **Genital LP** | Ultrapotent topical steroid ointment ± topical calcineurin inhibitor; vaginal steroid pessaries/hydrocortisone foam; **vaginal dilators** to prevent stenosis; emollient soap substitutes |
| **Nail LP** | **Intralesional triamcinolone into the proximal nail fold/matrix** (2.5–10 mg/mL, monthly), or a short course of systemic steroid / **intramuscular triamcinolone acetonide 0.5–1 mg/kg monthly for 3–6 months** — treat *early* to prevent pterygium |
| **Lichen planopilaris / FFA** | Potent topical + **intralesional steroids**, plus **hydroxychloroquine 200–400 mg/day** (assess at 6–12 months); doxycycline, pioglitazone (PPAR-γ agonist, mechanistically rational), and **5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride/dutasteride) for FFA**; JAK inhibitors in refractory cases |

### 7.3 Second line (steroid-sparing / for widespread or resistant disease)

- **Methotrexate 15–20 mg once weekly** (with folic acid on non-MTX days) for **12 weeks, then reassess**. Monitor CBC, LFT, renal function; avoid in pregnancy, liver disease, significant alcohol use.
- **Acitretin 30 mg/day for 12 weeks, then reassess** — particularly useful in **hypertrophic** and **oral** LP (retinoids normalise keratinisation and are immunomodulatory). Monitor **lipids and LFTs**; **teratogenic — contraception required for 3 years after stopping** in women of child-bearing potential.
- **Phototherapy — NB-UVB or PUVA, 2–5 sessions per week**, alone or combined with a retinoid (**re-PUVA**). Rationale: UV depletes and apoptoses skin-infiltrating T cells and Langerhans cells locally. Caution: relatively contraindicated in **actinic LP** (UV is the trigger there) and can worsen pigmentation.
- **JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib), ~8 weeks and reassess** — the mechanistically elegant option: they interrupt the **IFN-γ → JAK1/2 → STAT1 → CXCL9/10/11 → CXCR3⁺ CD8 T cell** loop described in Step 3. Increasingly used in LPP, FFA, erosive and nail LP. Screen for TB/hepatitis, monitor lipids, cytopenias, thrombo-embolic and infection risk.

### 7.4 Third line / adjuncts

- **Antimalarials (hydroxychloroquine)** — best for **actinic LP, LP pigmentosus, oral LP, LPP**; requires baseline and annual **ophthalmic screening**.
- **Ciclosporin 3–5 mg/kg/day** — rapid and highly effective in severe erosive oral LP (also used as a swish-and-spit solution); monitor **BP and creatinine**; limit duration.
- **Azathioprine** — check **TPMT/NUDT15** where available; monitor CBC/LFT.
- **Mycophenolate mofetil** — well tolerated steroid-sparing option for erosive mucosal disease.
- Others reported: **dapsone, sulfasalazine, metronidazole, griseofulvin, thalidomide, apremilast, low-dose naltrexone, biologics; photodynamic therapy and CO₂/excimer laser for localised oral lesions; surgery** for oesophageal strictures (dilatation), introital stenosis, or SCC.
- **Psychological support** — CBT/anxiolytics where stress is clearly a driver; do not underestimate the quality-of-life burden of chronic itch, oral pain and dyspareunia.

## 8. Common Confusions — Cleared Up

1. **"Wickham's striae are scale."** No. They are an **optical effect of wedge-shaped hypergranulosis**, not surface scale. That is why they are seen *better* when you wet the surface with oil — you eliminate surface scatter and reveal the deeper pattern.
2. **"Purple means vasculitis or bleeding."** No. The violaceous hue comes from **dilated dermal vessels seen through a compact hyperkeratotic epidermis** — the same optics that make subcutaneous veins look blue. LP lesions do not blanch to purpura and there is no extravasated blood.
3. **"Post-LP pigmentation means the disease is still active."** No — it means the disease *was* active and melanin fell into the dermis. Look for a raised violaceous edge or new itch to judge activity; pigment alone does not need immunosuppression.
4. **"Granzyme is another name for perforin."** No — **perforin makes the hole, granzyme is the enzyme pushed through it.**
5. **"LP and lichenoid drug eruption are histologically identical."** Nearly, but not quite: the drug eruption shows **eosinophils, parakeratosis, a deeper perivascular infiltrate, and more dyskeratosis at higher epidermal levels**. Clinically it is more photodistributed, more psoriasiform/eczematous, and Wickham's striae are usually absent. Always take a drug history.
6. **"Oral white patches = leukoplakia."** Reticular bilateral buccal striae favour LP; a solitary, homogeneous or speckled plaque in a smoker favours **leukoplakia**; a wipeable white coating suggests **candidiasis**; unilateral corrugated white patch on the lateral tongue in HIV suggests **oral hairy leukoplakia**. Biopsy anything that doesn't fit.
7. **"Lichen planus of the genitals = lichen sclerosus."** Different: **LS** is porcelain-white, sclerotic, "cigarette-paper" wrinkled, involves a **figure-of-eight** perianal extension, and **spares the vagina**; **LP** is violaceous/erosive with striae and **can involve the vagina** (vulvovaginal-gingival syndrome).
8. **"Lichen simplex chronicus is a type of lichen planus."** No relation — LSC is purely a rubbing-induced lichenification with no interface dermatitis; the name shares only the word "lichen."
9. **"Bullous LP and lichen planus pemphigoides are the same."** Bullous LP = blisters **only within** LP lesions, from confluent basal damage, DIF negative for linear IgG. LP pemphigoides = blisters **also on normal skin**, DIF shows **linear IgG/C3 at the BMZ**, driven by genuine anti-BP180 autoantibodies.
10. **"Scarring alopecia from LPP can regrow with steroids."** It cannot. Treatment aims to **arrest the advancing edge**; surgical restoration is considered only after ≥2 years of quiescence.
11. **"LP always itches."** Palmoplantar LP is often *painful*, oral LP *burns*, nail LP may be asymptomatic, and reticular oral LP is frequently discovered incidentally.

## 9. Summary Anchor — The Core Idea in One Picture

> **Imagine a row of soldiers (basal keratinocytes) standing on a concrete slab (basement membrane), holding up a multi-storey building (the epidermis).**
>
> A trigger — a virus, a drug, a metal, an injury — **pins an enemy flag** on those soldiers (altered antigen).
> **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells** arrive and shoot them with **perforin/granzyme, FasL and TNF-α**, while an **IFN-γ → JAK/STAT → CXCL9/10/11** loudspeaker keeps calling in reinforcements — which is why it becomes chronic, and why **JAK inhibitors** work.
>
> The fallen soldiers become **Civatte bodies**. The slab cracks → **Max-Joseph spaces**, and if cracks join → **blisters/erosions**. The soldiers were carrying **melanin**, which spills into the dermis → **grey-brown pigmentation for years**. The T cells massed against the slab form the **band-like infiltrate** → a **firm** papule. The building above thickens unevenly → **acanthosis, saw-toothed rete ridges, a flat plateau top**, and focal **wedge-shaped hypergranulosis** → **Wickham's striae**. Blood vessels seen through compact keratin → **purple**.
>
> **= Pruritic, Polygonal, Planar, Purple, Papules & Plaques.**
>
> Where the destroyed soldiers were **stem cells** (hair follicle bulge, nail matrix), the loss is **permanent** → **scarring alopecia, pterygium, anonychia**. Where the battlefield is **mucosa**, decades of inflammation risk **squamous cell carcinoma**.
>
> **So management = remove the flag (stop the drug/amalgam/betel, treat HCV) + stop the shooting (steroids topical → systemic → MTX/acitretin/phototherapy/JAK inhibitors → HCQ/ciclosporin/azathioprine/MMF) + act fast where damage is irreversible (nail, scalp, mucosa) + watch mucosal LP for cancer for life.**

## 10. Exam-Ready Condensed Version (for a "briefly discuss" answer)

**Definition.** LP ("flat moss") is a chronic T cell–mediated inflammatory dermatosis of ectodermally derived tissue — skin, mucosa, hair, nails — characterised by pruritic, polygonal, planar, purple papules and plaques.

**Pathogenesis.** Idiopathic; genetically predisposed (HLA-A3, B5, B7, Bw15, DR1, DR5). A trigger (HCV/HBV/HHV-6,7/VZV, hepatitis B vaccine, drugs — H₂ blockers, NSAIDs, antibiotics, antihypertensives, anticonvulsants, diuretics, metals, imatinib, IVIg, anti-TNF, antidepressants, checkpoint inhibitors — dental amalgam, betel nut, contact allergens, radiotherapy, trauma/Koebner, stress) alters basal keratinocyte antigens. Langerhans cells present them; CD4⁺ cells release IFN-γ, which via **JAK1/2–STAT1** induces keratinocyte **CXCL9/10/11**, recruiting **CXCR3⁺ CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells** (with minor γδ populations) in a self-amplifying loop. These kill basal keratinocytes via **perforin/granzyme B, Fas–FasL and TNF-α**; MMP-9 degrades the basement membrane. Result: apoptotic Civatte bodies, basal degeneration, Max-Joseph clefts, pigment incontinence, band-like infiltrate, hypergranulosis → the clinical lesion. Loss of follicular/nail-matrix immune privilege causes irreversible scarring.

**Clinical features.** **6 Ps.** Flexor wrists/forearms, shins, ankles, presacrum, genitalia; **Wickham's striae**; Koebner positive; grouped/annular (penis)/linear configurations. Mucosa in 50–70% — reticular (buccal), papular, plaque (tongue), atrophic (desquamative gingivitis), **erosive** (burning with spicy/hot food), bullous; vulvovaginal-gingival and peno-gingival syndromes. Nails (10–15%): thinning, longitudinal ridging, trachyonychia/20-nail dystrophy, pitting, longitudinal melanonychia, **pterygium, anonychia**. Scalp: **lichen planopilaris**, FFA, **Graham-Little-Piccardi-Lassueur triad** (cicatricial scalp alopecia + non-scarring axillary/groin alopecia + keratotic lichenoid follicular papules). Resolves with **grey-brown dermal pigmentation**. Variants: hypertrophic, atrophic, actinic, pigmentosus, palmoplantar, annular, linear, guttate, bullous, LP pemphigoides, lichen nitidus, keratosis lichenoides chronica (Nekam), LP–DLE overlap; rare conjunctival, oesophageal, perianal sites. Complications: scarring alopecia, nail loss, oesophageal stricture, introital stenosis, cicatricial conjunctivitis/lacrimal obstruction, and **SCC in erosive oral/genital LP (0.5–2% over 10 years)**. Associations: alopecia areata, vitiligo, ulcerative colitis, dermatomyositis, morphea, lichen sclerosus, paraneoplastic pemphigus, diabetes.

**Investigations.** Clinical + dermoscopy; **HPE** (orthohyperkeratosis, wedge hypergranulosis, acanthosis, saw-toothed rete ridges, basal degeneration, Civatte bodies, Max-Joseph spaces, band-like lymphocytic infiltrate, melanophages); **DIF** — shaggy fibrinogen at BMZ with globular IgM in colloid bodies; CBC, URM, LFT, ESR/CRP, **HCV/HBV serology**, glucose; patch testing for amalgam.

**Management.** Identify and remove triggers; symptomatic care (antihistamines, emollients, avoid irritant foods/SLS toothpaste).
- **1st line:** ultrapotent topical steroid (clobetasol 0.05%) for limited disease; **prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day** with taper for widespread disease; intralesional triamcinolone for hypertrophic, nail-matrix and oral lesions; topical tacrolimus for oral/genital LP.
- **2nd line:** **methotrexate 15–20 mg/week × 12 weeks**; **acitretin 30 mg/day × 12 weeks**; **PUVA/NB-UVB 2–5×/week ± retinoid**; **JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib) × 8 weeks** — then reassess.
- **3rd line:** hydroxychloroquine (actinic LP, LP pigmentosus, LPP), ciclosporin, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil.
- Plus site-specific care: dental hygiene/amalgam removal, vaginal dilators, 5α-reductase inhibitors for FFA, oesophageal dilatation, and **long-term malignancy surveillance of mucosal LP**.
