# 🎓 Mini-Lecture: The Lichenoid Disorders — "Many Diseases, One Battlefield"

---

## 1. The Big Picture: Why This Question Even Exists

Let me start by telling you why examiners love this question, because once you see the *logic*, the list stops being a memory exercise and becomes almost self-generating.

In dermatology, we have two vocabularies that constantly overlap:

* A **clinical vocabulary** — what the lesion looks like to your eye.
* A **histological vocabulary** — what the tissue looks like under the microscope.

The word **"lichenoid"** is unusual because it lives in *both* worlds, and it means something slightly different in each. That double life is the source of nearly every confusion students have here.

**Clinically, "lichenoid"** means: *flat-topped (planar), sharply demarcated, often violaceous papules* — lesions that look like the flat, plate-like growth of lichen on a rock. That's literally where the name comes from: *lichen*, the flat crusty organism on tree bark and stones. Hence the classic "**6 P's**" of lichen planus: **P**urple, **P**olygonal, **P**lanar, **P**ruritic, **P**apules and **P**laques.

**Histologically, "lichenoid"** means something far more specific and far more useful: a **band-like (lichenoid) lymphocytic infiltrate hugging the dermo-epidermal junction (DEJ), so dense that it obscures the junction itself**, accompanied by damage to the basal keratinocytes. This is the defining member of a family of patterns called **interface dermatitis**.

Here's the crucial insight: **the lichenoid tissue reaction is not a disease. It is a *final common pathway*.** It is the histological signature of one particular kind of immunological event — *a cytotoxic T-cell attack on the basal layer of the epidermis*. Anything that makes the immune system decide that basal keratinocytes are the enemy will produce this pattern.

So the question "enumerate the lichenoid disorders" is really asking: **"What are all the reasons the immune system might attack the basal keratinocyte layer?"** And *that* has a logical answer with four buckets:

| Bucket | The immunological trigger |
|---|---|
| **I. Prototype** | Autoreactivity against an unknown self-antigen → Lichen planus & variants |
| **II. Pathogens** | Microbial antigen displayed on keratinocytes → infections |
| **III. Pills** | Drug or its metabolite acting as a hapten altering keratinocyte antigens → drug eruptions |
| **IV. Plus (systemic/miscellaneous)** | Autoantibodies, alloreactive donor cells, tumour antigens, contactants, UV-modified antigens |

Remember it as the **4 P's of causation: Prototype, Pathogens, Pills, Plus.** Four buckets is far easier to carry into an exam than forty names.

## 2. The Mechanism First — Because Everything Else Follows From It

Before the list, let's build the machine. If you understand this cascade, you can *derive* the clinical signs rather than memorise them.

### Step-by-step: cause → effect → outcome

**Step 1 — Antigen display.**
Something appears on or within the basal keratinocyte that the immune system reads as foreign. This may be a modified self-protein (lichen planus), a viral peptide (HSV, VZV), a drug-protein adduct (lichenoid drug eruption), a treponemal antigen (syphilis), a host HLA molecule seen by donor cells (GVHD), or a tumour-associated antigen cross-reacting with skin (paraneoplastic pemphigus).

**Step 2 — Recruitment.**
Keratinocytes release chemokines (notably CXCL9/10/11, driven by interferon-γ); dendritic cells present antigen; **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes** home in and mass along the DEJ. CD4⁺ cells sit behind them as helpers. This mass of cells *is* the "band-like infiltrate."

> 🔍 **Analogy:** Think of the DEJ as a border wall between epidermis (upper city) and dermis (lower city). The lymphocytes are an army massing along that wall — so densely packed that from a satellite photo (your low-power microscope view) you can no longer see the wall itself. That's what "the infiltrate *obscures* the DEJ" means. It's the single most characteristic low-power finding.

**Step 3 — Keratinocyte killing.**
The CD8⁺ cells kill basal keratinocytes by perforin/granzyme, granulysin, and Fas–FasL engagement. Each dying keratinocyte undergoes **apoptosis**, shrinking into a dense, glassy, eosinophilic round body. Pathologists call these **Civatte bodies / colloid bodies / hyaline bodies / apoptotic keratinocytes** — all the same thing, and their presence is the histological proof that cytotoxic killing happened.

**Step 4 — Basal layer failure → vacuolar change.**
As basal cells die, small fluid-filled spaces appear where they used to be: **vacuolar (or liquefactive) degeneration** of the basal layer. In milder, cell-poor reactions this vacuolar change is the *dominant* finding (as in lupus or erythema multiforme). In dense, cell-rich reactions, it's buried under the lymphocyte band (as in lichen planus). These are the two ends of one spectrum:

* **Cell-rich interface dermatitis = "lichenoid"** → lichen planus, lichenoid drug eruption, lichenoid GVHD
* **Cell-poor interface dermatitis = "vacuolar"** → lupus erythematosus, dermatomyositis, erythema multiforme, acute GVHD, TEN

This is Bolognia's organising axis, and it is the single most examinable histological concept in the whole topic.

**Step 5 — If killing is severe: the epidermis detaches.**
Enough basal destruction and the epidermis loses its anchorage, producing a **subepidermal cleft**. When this happens focally in lichen planus, the little clefts are named **Max Joseph spaces**; when extensive, you get clinical blisters → **bullous lichen planus**. When it happens across whole sheets of skin because of massive keratinocyte apoptosis, you get **toxic epidermal necrolysis**. Same principle, different intensity.

**Step 6 — The epidermis fights back (and this creates the clinical signs).**
The surviving epidermis responds with:

* **Compact orthohyperkeratosis** (thickened, non-flaky scale — hence the *shiny, smooth, dry* surface rather than a scaly one)
* **Wedge-shaped hypergranulosis** — focal V-shaped thickening of the granular layer. Because the granular layer is optically dense and whitish, these focal thickenings are visible through the surface as a fine white lacy network: **Wickham's striae**. So Wickham's striae are literally *hypergranulosis you can see with your naked eye* — a beautiful example of histology becoming a bedside sign.
* **Irregular acanthosis with pointed rete ridges** → the classic **"saw-tooth" appearance** of the lower epidermis.

**Step 7 — Why the papules are FLAT-TOPPED.**
Here's a question students rarely ask but should: why *planar*? Because the lesion is not a round ball of something pushing up from a point; it is a **flat plate of infiltrate laid down horizontally beneath the epidermis**, uniformly lifting it, topped by compact hyperkeratosis. A band elevates a plateau; a nodule elevates a dome. The geometry of the pathology dictates the geometry of the lesion.

**Step 8 — Why VIOLACEOUS.**
Two contributions. First, the dense cellular/vascular dermal infiltrate seen through a thickened horny layer scatters light toward blue-violet (the same physics that makes deep pigment look blue — a Tyndall-type effect). Second, **pigment incontinence**: when basal keratinocytes die, the melanin they were holding spills into the papillary dermis and is scavenged by **melanophages**. Dermal melanin reads as grey-brown-violet, not the crisp brown of epidermal melanin.

**Step 9 — The aftermath: post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.**
Those melanophages persist for months. This is why lichen planus resolves into stubborn slate-grey macules, and why it is so much more cosmetically distressing in darker skin phototypes. It also explains the whole entity of **lichen planus pigmentosus**.

**Step 10 — The scarring consequence: stem cells.**
This step is clinically the most important and the most often skipped. The basal layer is where **epidermal and adnexal stem cells** live. If the attack destroys a *replenishable* basal layer, the skin heals. But if it destroys the **stem-cell reservoirs**, the loss is permanent:

* Attack on the **follicular bulge** (hair follicle stem cells) → **lichen planopilaris** → *permanent, scarring (cicatricial) alopecia*.
* Attack on the **nail matrix** → longitudinal ridging, then **pterygium unguis** (dorsal pterygium) and **anonychia** — permanent nail loss.
* Attack on **mucosa** with ongoing chronicity → atrophy, erosions, and a small but real risk of **squamous cell carcinoma** in erosive oral and vulval disease.
* Repeated interface damage anywhere → **poikiloderma** (atrophy + telangiectasia + mottled dys-pigmentation) — which is simply *the scar of chronic interface dermatitis*, and that is why poikiloderma appears on the lichenoid list at all.

> 💡 **The one-sentence mechanism:** *Cytotoxic T cells line up along the DEJ and execute basal keratinocytes; the apoptosis produces Civatte bodies and vacuolar change, the detachment produces Max Joseph spaces and bullae, the epidermal reaction produces hypergranulosis (Wickham's striae) and saw-toothing, the spilled melanin produces violaceous colour and post-inflammatory pigmentation, and the loss of stem cells produces scarring alopecia, nail pterygium and poikiloderma.*

## 3. Bucket I — The Prototype: Lichen Planus and Its Variants

Lichen planus (LP) is the **archetype**, the disease against which every other lichenoid condition is compared. Its cause is not fully known: the current model is a T-cell response to an as-yet-unidentified **self-antigen on basal keratinocytes**, with reported associations including **hepatitis C virus** (strongly region-dependent), other autoimmune disease, and stress. Note that HCV association is variable by geography — robust in Japan, Mediterranean countries and parts of South Asia, weak or absent in Northern Europe.

Its variants are best understood as **the same pathology, varied by three things: site, depth, and intensity.**

### Varied by intensity and duration

**Hypertrophic (verrucous) LP.**
Thick, warty, intensely itchy plaques, classically on the **shins and ankles**. Mechanism: chronic lichenoid inflammation *plus* the patient's own relentless scratching adds lichenification on top. The result is massive hyperkeratosis and acanthosis. Two practical consequences: (a) it is the most treatment-resistant form, and (b) long-standing hypertrophic lesions on the leg carry a small risk of **squamous cell carcinoma**, so a non-healing nodule within one deserves biopsy.

**Atrophic LP.**
The opposite end. Here the epidermis, having lost its proliferative basal compartment, thins out. Lesions are flat or depressed, shiny, often on the trunk or lower limbs, and often represent *resolving* annular or hypertrophic lesions. Same attack, but the outcome is loss rather than hypertrophy.

**Bullous LP.**
As explained in Step 5: interface damage so intense that Max Joseph spaces coalesce into true blisters. Crucially, the blister forms **within pre-existing LP lesions** and the mechanism is purely **cell-mediated** — there are no pathogenic autoantibodies. Direct immunofluorescence shows only non-specific shaggy fibrinogen at the DEJ and colloid bodies staining for IgM.

**Lichen planus pemphigoides (LPP).**
Now contrast this carefully, because this is a classic exam discriminator. LPP is a genuine **two-hit disease**: the T-cell attack damages the basement membrane zone, exposing hidden epitopes of **BP180 (collagen XVII), particularly the NC16A domain**, and the patient then mounts a *true autoantibody* response against them. This is **epitope spreading** — the cell-mediated injury *creates* a humoral autoimmune disease.

> 🔍 **Analogy:** Bullous LP is a building whose foundations are so damaged that the ground floor collapses. LPP is a building whose damaged foundations *expose hidden wiring*, and now a second, independent demolition crew (autoantibodies) arrives to attack that wiring.

The clinical consequence follows directly: in LPP, **blisters arise on clinically normal skin as well as on lesions**, because circulating antibodies travel everywhere. And DIF shows **linear IgG and C3 at the BMZ** — the signature of an antibody-mediated subepidermal blistering disease. Remember: *bullous LP = one mechanism, blisters only on lesions, no linear IgG; LPP = two mechanisms, blisters also on normal skin, linear IgG present.*

### Varied by configuration

**Linear LP.**
Lesions in a line. Two very different reasons, and you must separate them:

* **True linear/zosteriform LP** following **Blaschko's lines** — reflecting a clone of keratinocytes derived from a single embryological migration pathway that is uniquely susceptible (genetic mosaicism).
* **Koebnerised LP** following a scratch, surgical scar or trauma line. LP is famously **Koebner-positive**: trauma releases antigen and recruits inflammation into the injured line. This is enormously practical — *tell your LP patient not to scratch, and avoid unnecessary procedures during active disease.*

**Annular LP.**
Ring-shaped lesions with an active, raised violaceous rim and an atrophic or hyperpigmented centre. Mechanism: centrifugal spread — the inflammatory front advances outward while the centre burns out. Favours the **axillae, groin and penis** (where annular LP on the glans is a classic and commonly-tested presentation).

**Guttate LP.**
*Gutta* = a drop. Myriad tiny, droplet-like papules, widely scattered — an eruptive, acute-onset pattern. Because it is small and monomorphic it is the form most easily mistaken for guttate psoriasis, pityriasis lichenoides, or a viral exanthem.

### Varied by site and depth

**Actinic LP (lichen planus actinicus / subtropicus / "LP tropicus").**
Here the trigger is clearly **ultraviolet radiation**: UV modifies keratinocyte antigens and increases their visibility to T cells. It presents in young adults in the **Middle East, India, East Africa and the tropics**, on **sun-exposed sites — face (especially periorbital and forehead), dorsal hands, neck** — as annular, dyschromic, hyperpigmented plaques with a hypopigmented halo. It is typically **less pruritic** than classic LP, tends to **remit in winter**, and rarely involves mucosa or nails. This is the same entity as the "lichen photosensitive / phototoxic / photodistributed lichenoid dermatosis" items that appear later in the miscellaneous list — which is exactly why that duplication occurs across textbook lists.

**Lichen planopilaris (LPP — follicular LP).**
The attack is directed at the **follicular epithelium** rather than the interfollicular epidermis. Clinically: perifollicular erythema and **perifollicular scale (acuminate keratotic spines) at the periphery of expanding patches of alopecia**, plus loss of follicular ostia and single-hair emergence. Because the target includes the bulge stem cells, the result is **irreversible scarring alopecia**. Its recognised presentations are: (1) classic patchy scalp LPP, (2) **frontal fibrosing alopecia** (frontotemporal recession with eyebrow loss — the most common cicatricial alopecia in post-menopausal women today), and (3) **Graham-Little–Piccardi–Lassueur syndrome** = scarring scalp alopecia + non-scarring axillary/pubic hair loss + follicular keratotic papules on the body. Teaching point: *LPP is a dermatological emergency in the sense that treatment is aimed at halting progression — hair already lost cannot be recovered.*

### The two "lichen" cousins that are conventionally grouped here

**Lichen nitidus.**
*Nitidus* = shining. Countless **1–2 mm, discrete, shiny, flat-topped, skin-coloured or hypopigmented papules**, favouring the forearms, abdomen, penis and hands, common in children, usually **asymptomatic** (contrast with the fierce itch of LP). Koebner-positive. Its histology is *not* a simple band: it is a sharply circumscribed, **focal lympho-histiocytic granulomatous infiltrate confined to one or two widened dermal papillae**, with the adjacent rete ridges curving around it like a claw — the celebrated **"ball-in-claw" or "ball-and-claw" configuration**, with overlying epidermal thinning and parakeratosis. Whether it is a true LP variant or a separate entity remains debated; the histology argues for "related but distinct," and exams reward you for saying exactly that.

**Lichen striatus.**
A **self-limited, acquired, linear inflammatory dermatosis of children**, appearing over days to weeks as a band of small pink or hypopigmented lichenoid papules running **along Blaschko's lines**, usually on a limb, occasionally the trunk or face, resolving spontaneously over **6–12 months** often leaving temporary hypopigmentation. The favoured mechanism is **postzygotic somatic mosaicism**: a clone of mutant keratinocytes distributed along a Blaschko line loses immune tolerance — often after a viral trigger — and is attacked. Histologically it is lichenoid *but* distinguished by a **deeper infiltrate that extends around eccrine glands and hair follicles** and is more patchy and spongiotic than classic LP. When it involves the nail unit it can cause temporary nail dystrophy. Contrast it firmly with linear LP: *lichen striatus = child, transient, deeper/perieccrine, self-resolving; linear LP = usually adult, persistent, classic LP histology, needs treatment.*

### The overlap

**Lupus erythematosus–lichen planus overlap syndrome.**
An uncommon but conceptually elegant entity: patients with lesions — typically on **acral sites, palms and soles** — that are clinically, histologically and immunologically *intermediate* between DLE and LP, and cannot confidently be assigned to either. You may see LP-like histology with LE-like DIF (linear granular IgG/IgM at the BMZ), or vice versa, sometimes with a positive ANA. Its existence is the strongest single argument for the concept we started with: **these are not separate diseases so much as different settings of the same interface-dermatitis machinery**, and a patient can sit between two settings.

## 4. Bucket II — Pathogens (Infections Producing Lichenoid Reactions)

The unifying logic: **a microbial antigen displayed on or near keratinocytes converts them into targets.** The T-cell response aimed at the microbe damages the host epidermis as collateral.

**Late secondary syphilis (lichenoid syphilis).**
Syphilis has earned the title "the great imitator," and its lichenoid form is a prime reason. In **late secondary** disease, papules become flat-topped, violaceous and scaly, and may closely mimic LP — including on palms and soles. Histologically you get a lichenoid interface infiltrate that is **rich in plasma cells** and accompanied by **endothelial swelling and a prominent perivascular component**, sometimes with granulomas in the latest stages. Immunostaining or Warthin–Starry may reveal spirochaetes.
➡️ **The clinical rule that comes out of this:** *any atypical, widely distributed lichenoid eruption — especially with palmoplantar, mucosal or lymphadenopathic features — warrants syphilis serology.* Missing it means missing a disease that is completely curable with penicillin and lethal if left to progress.

**Gianotti–Crosti syndrome (papular acrodermatitis of childhood).**
A **para-infectious** eruption of young children: an abrupt, symmetrical, **monomorphous** crop of flat-topped, lichenoid, sometimes purpuric papules on the **cheeks, buttocks and extensor limbs**, characteristically **sparing the trunk**. Classically linked to **hepatitis B** (the original Italian descriptions), but today most cases in vaccinated populations follow **EBV**, and also coxsackie, parvovirus B19, respiratory viruses and occasionally vaccines. Mechanism: circulating viral antigen deposits in skin and provokes an interface reaction. It is **self-limited over 2–8 weeks**; management is reassurance plus, where clinically indicated, hepatitis serology.

**Herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella-zoster virus (VZV).**
These viruses infect keratinocytes directly and display viral peptides on MHC I — which is precisely the "eat me" signal for CD8⁺ T cells. The resulting histology is therefore *inevitably* a florid interface/lichenoid pattern, but with **distinctive additional viral cytopathic features: ballooning degeneration, multinucleate keratinocytes, steel-grey nuclei with moulded chromatin, and acantholysis** forming the intraepidermal vesicle. Two further clinically relevant phenomena:
* Healing zoster can leave behind a **lichenoid/granulomatous reaction in the resolved dermatome** — one of the classic "isotopic responses" (Wolf's isotopic response), where a new dermatosis, including genuine LP, arises in a previously affected dermatome.
* Recurrent HSV can trigger **erythema multiforme**, itself a vacuolar interface dermatitis (see next bucket).

**PLEVA (pityriasis lichenoides et varioliformis acuta / Mucha–Habermann disease).**
An abrupt, recurrent crop of small **erythematous papules that vesiculate, crust, necrose and heal with varioliform (pox-like) scars**, in crops of different ages, on the trunk and flexures. The favoured pathogenesis is an exaggerated **cytotoxic T-cell response to an infectious antigen**, frequently with a demonstrable **clonal CD8⁺ T-cell population** — which is why PLEVA sits uncomfortably on the border between reactive dermatosis and a low-grade cutaneous lymphoproliferative disorder. Histology: a **wedge-shaped (apex-down) lichenoid infiltrate**, marked lymphocyte exocytosis into the epidermis, **parakeratosis, keratinocyte necrosis, extravasated red cells** and often frank epidermal necrosis. Its fulminant form (febrile ulceronecrotic Mucha–Habermann disease) is a true emergency. Its chronic sibling, **pityriasis lichenoides chronica (PLC)**, is milder, scalier ("mica scale"), and appears again in the miscellaneous list as "pityriasis lichenoides" — the same duplication issue we'll discuss below.

## 5. Bucket III — Pills (Drug-Induced Lichenoid Eruptions)

The mechanism: a drug or its reactive metabolite behaves as a **hapten**, covalently modifying keratinocyte proteins, or alters MHC-peptide presentation, so that basal cells are now read as foreign. Remove the drug and — eventually — the target disappears.

**Lichenoid drug eruption (LDE).**
Clinically it can be a near-perfect mimic of LP, which is why "what drugs is the patient on?" must be asked of *every* lichenoid rash. Features that shift suspicion toward a drug cause:

| Feature | Idiopathic LP | Lichenoid drug eruption |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution | Wrists, forearms, shins, sacrum; symmetrical | **More extensive, eczematous or psoriasiform, often photodistributed** |
| Wickham's striae | Typical | Usually absent or poorly formed |
| Mucosa / nails | Commonly involved | Usually spared |
| Latency | — | **Weeks to months** (sometimes >1 year) after drug start |
| Histology | Compact orthokeratosis, band-like infiltrate, no eosinophils | **Parakeratosis, eosinophils and occasional plasma cells, deeper and perivascular infiltrate, more apoptosis at higher epidermal levels** |
| Course | Chronic, relapsing | **Slow clearance over weeks–months after withdrawal**, often with marked residual pigmentation |

The offending drug classes worth knowing (Q2 of your source deals with this in detail): **antimalarials** (chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, quinacrine), **thiazide diuretics**, **ACE inhibitors** (captopril, enalapril), **β-blockers** (practolol historically, propranolol), **gold salts** and **penicillamine**, **NSAIDs**, **sulfonylureas**, **antituberculous drugs** (isoniazid, ethambutol), **quinine/quinidine**, **methyldopa**, **phenothiazines**, **TNF-α inhibitors**, **imatinib and other tyrosine-kinase inhibitors**, and — the modern superstar — **immune-checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1/PD-L1 such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab)**, which cause lichenoid eruptions in a substantial fraction of treated patients. That last one makes beautiful mechanistic sense: *checkpoint blockade releases the brakes on cytotoxic T cells, so the most common cutaneous toxicity is, predictably, a cytotoxic T-cell attack on the basal layer.*

**Fixed drug eruption (FDE).**
The defining feature is **recurrence at precisely the same anatomical site(s)** on each re-exposure, classically as a solitary or few dusky, round, violaceous plaques — often on lips, genitalia, hands — leaving intense, long-lasting slate-grey pigmentation. The mechanism is elegant: **resident intraepidermal memory CD8⁺ T cells** persist at the site of the previous reaction. They lie dormant; re-challenge reactivates them *in situ*; hence a stereotyped, site-fixed response. Histologically FDE is among the most florid interface reactions you will see: **marked vacuolar change with abundant apoptotic keratinocytes, a mixed superficial AND deep infiltrate containing eosinophils and neutrophils, and — the giveaway — heavy melanin incontinence with numerous dermal melanophages** reflecting previous episodes.

**Erythema multiforme (EM).**
Target/iris lesions on acral skin, most often triggered by **HSV** (yes — a drug-bucket entity whose commonest cause is actually an infection; drugs are a minority cause). Histology is the **cell-poor, vacuolar** end of the interface spectrum: prominent basal vacuolisation, scattered necrotic keratinocytes, and **satellite cell necrosis** (a lymphocyte hugging a dying keratinocyte — a cytotoxic T cell caught in the act, which is a wonderful teaching image). The concentric target morphology arises because the reaction begins around a central vessel and spreads outward in waves of differing intensity.

**Toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN).**
The extreme, life-threatening end of the same axis. Drug-specific cytotoxic T cells and NK cells release **granulysin, perforin/granzyme B and soluble FasL**, producing **confluent full-thickness keratinocyte apoptosis**. Histologically it is strikingly **cell-poor** — a handful of lymphocytes causing catastrophic damage, the immunological equivalent of a small demolition team with a very large charge. Clinically: extensive skin detachment, positive Nikolsky sign, mucosal erosions, and the need for intensive care.

> 🔍 **The intensity dial analogy:** Picture one dial labelled "cytotoxic attack on the basal layer." Turn it low and chronic → lichen planus (papules, hypergranulosis, hyperpigmentation). Turn it medium → erythema multiforme (targets, focal necrosis). Turn it to maximum and diffuse → TEN (sheets of skin separating). **One mechanism, three diseases, one dial.**

## 6. Bucket IV — "Plus": Systemic, Autoimmune, Neoplastic and Miscellaneous Causes

### The lupus erythematosus family

Lupus is the great example of **cell-poor, vacuolar interface dermatitis**, and it is the most important daily differential of LP — especially in oral disease and in scalp scarring alopecia.

* **SLE** — acute cutaneous LE: malar erythema, photodistributed eruption; histologically often a subtle vacuolar interface with abundant **dermal mucin** and a perivascular/periadnexal lymphocytic infiltrate.
* **SCLE (subacute cutaneous LE)** — annular-polycyclic or psoriasiform plaques on the upper trunk, shoulders, extensor arms, strongly **photodistributed**, strongly associated with **anti-Ro/SSA antibodies**, non-scarring but often leaving dyspigmentation, and frequently **drug-induced** (hydrochlorothiazide, terbinafine, calcium-channel blockers, TNF inhibitors, PPIs). Histologically it shows more epidermal atrophy and interface change than SLE, with less follicular plugging than DLE.
* **DLE (discoid LE)** — indurated, scaly plaques with **follicular plugging**, adherent scale ("carpet-tack" sign), central atrophy and **scarring**, favouring face, ears (conchal bowl) and scalp, where it causes cicatricial alopecia. Histology: interface change **plus thickened basement membrane (PAS-positive), follicular plugging, a deep periadnexal and perivascular infiltrate, and dermal mucin.** DIF shows the **lupus band** (granular IgG/IgM/C3 at the DEJ).
* **NLE (neonatal LE)** — transplacental **maternal anti-Ro/SSA (± anti-La/SSB, anti-U1RNP)** IgG reaching the fetus produces annular, SCLE-like, often periorbital ("owl-eye" / "raccoon-eye") lesions in the newborn, frequently UV-provoked, which resolve as maternal antibody is cleared over ~6 months. **The critical association is congenital heart block**, which is *not* reversible — hence every infant with NLE needs cardiac assessment, and every mother needs autoantibody testing and counselling for future pregnancies.

> ⚠️ **Correction embedded here:** the source text's "**SALE**" is a typographical/OCR error. There is no such abbreviation in dermatology. The intended term is **SCLE — subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus**. Likewise, the source lists **NLE twice** (positions 2 and 5) — a straightforward duplication. The correct, non-redundant list of cutaneous LE subtypes relevant here is **SLE (ACLE), SCLE, DLE (CCLE) and NLE**.

**How to separate LP from DLE — the practical table you will use forever:**

| | Lichen planus | Discoid LE |
|---|---|---|
| Interface pattern | **Cell-rich, lichenoid** (dense band) | **Cell-poor, vacuolar** |
| Granular layer | **Hypergranulosis** (wedge-shaped) | Thin/atrophic |
| Keratinisation | **Compact orthokeratosis** | **Follicular plugging**, parakeratosis |
| Basement membrane | Normal thickness | **Thickened (PAS +)** |
| Dermal mucin | Absent/scant | **Increased** |
| Infiltrate depth | Superficial, band-like | **Superficial *and* deep, periadnexal** |
| DIF | Shaggy fibrinogen; IgM on colloid bodies | **Granular IgG/IgM/C3 lupus band** |
| Photodistribution | Not typical (except actinic LP) | Typical |

### Immunodeficiency-associated disease: AIDS / HIV and "ID"

In advanced HIV disease, lichenoid eruptions are disproportionately common — particularly **photodistributed lichenoid drug eruptions** and **hypertrophic LP**, and especially in patients of African descent. Several forces converge: **polypharmacy** (antiretrovirals, co-trimoxazole, antituberculous therapy — a hapten-rich environment), **immune dysregulation with a relatively preserved or skewed CD8⁺ compartment attacking a CD4-depleted background**, acquired **photosensitivity**, and, following the start of ART, **immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS)** unmasking latent responses. Practical rule: *a florid, photodistributed or unusually extensive lichenoid eruption in an at-risk patient should prompt an HIV test.* ("ID" in the source list is shorthand for **immunodeficiency**-associated dermatoses generally, which includes primary immunodeficiency states as well.)

### Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)

This is the **purest human experiment** proving the mechanism we built in Section 2. Here there is no ambiguity about the antigen: **donor-derived alloreactive T cells recognise recipient (host) HLA and minor histocompatibility antigens on host keratinocytes and kill them.** No autoantibody, no drug, no microbe — just cytotoxic T cells versus basal keratinocytes. And the resulting histology is textbook interface dermatitis.

* **Acute GVHD** (classically <100 days post-transplant, though modern classification is clinical rather than by calendar): a morbilliform eruption with characteristic early involvement of **palms, soles, ears and upper trunk**, which can progress to a TEN-like desquamation. Histology is **vacuolar/cell-poor** with apoptotic keratinocytes and **satellite cell necrosis**; grading (Lerner) runs from basal vacuolisation (grade I) through cleft formation to frank epidermal separation (grade IV). Accompanied by hepatic and gastrointestinal involvement.
* **Chronic GVHD, lichenoid phase**: violaceous flat-topped papules with Wickham-like striae and oral lesions essentially **indistinguishable from lichen planus** clinically and histologically — differentiated by *context*, not morphology.
* **Chronic GVHD, sclerodermoid phase**: chronic inflammation drives fibroblast activation and dermal fibrosis → sclerotic plaques, fasciitis, joint contractures. A superb illustration of the progression **inflammation → fibrosis → functional disability.**

### Paraneoplastic pemphigus (paraneoplastic autoimmune multiorgan syndrome)

This is the entity that most students misfile, because it is on the *pemphigus* list and the *lichenoid* list simultaneously — and it deserves to be on both, because it is genuinely a **dual-mechanism disease**.

* **Humoral arm:** autoantibodies against **plakin family proteins — envoplakin, periplakin, desmoplakin, BP230, plectin, α2-macroglobulin-like-1** — plus desmogleins, producing **suprabasal acantholysis** and blistering.
* **Cell-mediated arm:** a simultaneous **lichenoid/interface attack**, which is why the histology is famously described as showing **acantholysis *and* lichenoid interface change *and* keratinocyte necrosis in the same biopsy** — a combination almost unique to this disease.
* **Clinically:** intractable, treatment-resistant **stomatitis** (often the first and most persistent feature), polymorphous cutaneous lesions spanning pemphigus-like, pemphigoid-like, EM-like and lichenoid-GVHD-like morphologies, and **bronchiolitis obliterans**, which is the leading cause of death.
* **Underlying neoplasms:** **Castleman disease** (especially in children and in Asian populations), **chronic lymphocytic leukaemia**, **non-Hodgkin lymphoma**, thymoma, Waldenström macroglobulinaemia, sarcomas.
* **Immunofluorescence clue:** indirect IF on **rat bladder transitional epithelium** is positive (because that tissue is plakin-rich and desmoglein-poor) — the classic discriminating test from ordinary pemphigus vulgaris.
➡️ **Why it matters:** a lichenoid/blistering eruption with refractory mucositis obliges you to hunt for an occult lymphoproliferative malignancy.

### Poikiloderma

As foreshadowed in Step 10, **poikiloderma is not a disease but an end-state**: the triad of **atrophy + telangiectasia + mottled hypo- and hyperpigmentation**. Mechanistically it is the scar of **chronic, low-grade interface dermatitis**: repeated basal-cell destruction thins the epidermis (atrophy), damages papillary dermal vessels (telangiectasia), and produces patchy melanocyte loss alongside pigment incontinence (mottled dyspigmentation). It therefore appears as the final chapter of many entities on this list. Causes to know: **poikilodermatous mycosis fungoides** (the one you must never miss — biopsy persistent poikiloderma in non-sun-exposed sites, especially the "bathing trunk" distribution), **dermatomyositis**, chronic DLE, **chronic radiodermatitis**, **poikiloderma of Civatte** (chronic photodamage of the lateral neck), the genodermatoses **Rothmund–Thomson syndrome, Kindler syndrome and dyskeratosis congenita**, and **poikiloderma vasculare atrophicans** (which is often early MF).

### Pityriasis lichenoides

Already met as **PLEVA** in the infections bucket; its chronic counterpart **PLC** presents with recurrent crops of small, red-brown papules bearing an adherent **"mica" scale** that can be lifted off intact, healing with hypopigmentation. The whole family (PLEVA ↔ PLC, with intermediate forms) is a **clonal-cytotoxic-T-cell spectrum**, which is why patients need **long-term follow-up** for the small risk of progression to mycosis fungoides, and why "lymphomatoid papulosis" belongs in the same differential conversation.

### Lichenoid contact dermatitis

A delayed-type hypersensitivity response in which the contactant behaves as the antigen driving an interface reaction rather than the usual spongiotic one. The classic culprits:
* **Dental metals — amalgam (mercury), gold, nickel, palladium, cobalt** — producing **oral lichenoid lesions** that are anatomically *keyed to the restoration*: a lesion on the buccal mucosa or lateral tongue lying precisely against an amalgam filling. This is a clinically vital distinction, because unlike idiopathic oral LP, **these lesions frequently resolve when the offending restoration is replaced.** Patch testing guides the decision.
* **Colour-film developers (para-phenylenediamine derivatives)** — the historical occupational cause.
* **Epoxy resins, nickel, musk ambrette and certain plant materials.**
➡️ **Teaching point:** for any unilateral or oddly localised lichenoid eruption, ask *"what is touching this exact spot?"*

### Lichen amyloidosis — the important "false friend"

Here is one of the most valuable teaching points on the entire page. **Lichen amyloidosis is clinically lichenoid but histologically NOT an interface dermatitis.** It presents as **intensely itchy, closely-set, dome-shaped or flat-topped brown papules in a rippled/"ripple-pattern" distribution on the shins, extensor limbs and upper back**. Its pathology is **deposition of keratin-derived amyloid (AK type, derived from degenerated keratinocyte tonofilaments, staining with Congo red and thioflavin T) in the papillary dermis**, with little or no lymphocytic band.

Why is it on the list, then? Because chronic **rubbing and scratching** (it belongs to the primary localised cutaneous amyloidoses, and friction/atopy are contributory) causes basal keratinocyte apoptosis, and those apoptotic keratin filaments are precisely what get converted into amyloid. So the *initiating event* has a cytotoxic, keratinocyte-death flavour even though the final histological picture is deposition, not interface inflammation. It is also worth knowing its association with **MEN2A (RET mutations)** and with familial primary cutaneous amyloidosis (*OSMR*, *IL31RA* mutations).
➡️ **Exam framing:** *lichen amyloidosis earns its place clinically (lichenoid morphology) rather than histologically (amyloid deposition) — and Congo red staining is what separates it in one step.*

### Photosensitive and phototoxic lichenoid dermatoses

This item in the source list overlaps with actinic LP and with photodistributed lichenoid drug eruption, and it is best understood as a single mechanistic family: **UV radiation modifies keratinocyte proteins (directly, or by generating reactive drug photoproducts), creating neoantigens in exactly the anatomical distribution the sun reaches.** Hence:
* **Lichen planus actinicus** (idiopathic, tropical/Middle Eastern, young adults)
* **Photodistributed lichenoid drug eruption** — thiazides, quinine, quinidine, antimalarials, tetracyclines, NSAIDs, chlorpromazine
* Lichenoid change occurring in **chronic actinic dermatitis** and in phototoxic reactions
➡️ **Practical rule:** a lichenoid rash confined to the V of the neck, dorsal hands, forearms, face and upper back, sparing the submental region, retroauricular skin and covered areas, *is telling you the trigger is light — and light usually implicates a drug.*

### Erythroderma (erythrodermic / generalised lichen planus)

Rarely, lichenoid disease generalises to involve **>90% of the body surface**, and it then joins psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, drug reactions, pityriasis rubra pilaris and cutaneous T-cell lymphoma on the erythroderma differential. This matters because erythroderma is a **systemic emergency**, not a cosmetic problem: loss of the skin's barrier produces **high-output heat loss and hypothermia, massive transepidermal water loss and dehydration, protein loss and hypoalbuminaemia, electrolyte disturbance, increased cutaneous blood flow with risk of high-output cardiac failure, and loss of the antimicrobial barrier with risk of sepsis.** Management is inpatient supportive care plus treatment of the cause — and *drug withdrawal* is the first move, since generalised lichenoid erythroderma is very often drug-driven.

### Worth adding, though absent from the source list

For completeness — these appear on modern lichenoid differentials and examiners occasionally introduce them:
* **Benign lichenoid keratosis (lichen planus–like keratosis)** — a *solitary* lichenoid papule, usually on the forearm or chest of a middle-aged adult, representing an immune-mediated regression of a pre-existing solar lentigo or seborrhoeic keratosis. It mimics basal cell carcinoma or Bowen's disease clinically and is usually diagnosed on excision.
* **Keratosis lichenoides chronica (Nekam's disease)** — persistent, symmetrical, linear and reticulate lichenoid keratotic papules on the limbs with a seborrhoeic-dermatitis-like facial eruption.
* **Lichen planus pigmentosus** and **erythema dyschromicum perstans (ashy dermatosis)** — the pigmentary variants, in which melanin incontinence dominates and the inflammatory phase is barely visible.
* **Lichenoid mycosis fungoides / lichenoid drug-induced pseudolymphoma.**
* **Hypertrophic lupus erythematosus** and **lichenoid regression of melanocytic lesions** (halo naevus, regressing melanoma) — where the interface attack is directed at melanocytes rather than keratinocytes.

## 7. Corrections and Verification — and *Why* Each One Is a Correction

The source page contains four issues worth flagging, and understanding *why* each is an error is more instructive than simply noting it:

1. **"SALE" → SCLE.** No abbreviation "SALE" exists in dermatological nomenclature. The intended entity is **subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus**, the anti-Ro-associated, photodistributed, non-scarring, annular/psoriasiform subtype. Almost certainly an OCR/typesetting corruption of SCLE. *(Rook's; Bolognia — classification of cutaneous LE.)*

2. **NLE duplicated.** The source reads "SLE, NLE, DLE, SALE, and NLE." **Neonatal LE** appears at both positions 2 and 5. The corrected, non-redundant set is **SLE, SCLE, DLE, NLE**. *(Bolognia — LE subtypes.)*

3. **Late secondary syphilis duplicated across two headings.** It appears under both *Infections* and *Miscellaneous*. Its proper home is **Infections**, since the lichenoid tissue reaction there is driven directly by treponemal antigen; its histological hallmark is a **plasma-cell-rich lichenoid infiltrate with endothelial swelling**. *(Rook's — Syphilis; Andrews'.)* The same duplication logic applies to **"lichen photosensitive"**, which appears **twice** in the miscellaneous list and additionally overlaps with **actinic LP** in the variants section — three entries for one mechanism.

4. **Structural flattening.** The source's "Miscellaneous" heading is an undifferentiated list, which obscures the fact that it actually contains at least five mechanistically distinct groups: **autoantibody-mediated (LE subtypes, paraneoplastic pemphigus), alloreactive (GVHD), immunodeficiency/polypharmacy-related (HIV/AIDS, ID), external-contactant (lichenoid contact dermatitis), UV-triggered (photosensitive/actinic), clonal-lymphocytic (pityriasis lichenoides), and end-state/reactive (poikiloderma, lichen amyloidosis, erythroderma).** Reorganising along mechanism — as we have done above, following Bolognia's interface-dermatitis framework and Rook's lichenoid-tissue-reaction taxonomy — makes the list both shorter to remember and more clinically usable.

## 8. Common Misunderstandings — The Traps

**Trap 1: "Lichenoid" is not "lichenified."**
* **Lichenoid** = flat-topped violaceous papules clinically; **band-like interface infiltrate** histologically; mechanism = *cytotoxic T-cell attack.*
* **Lichenification** = thickened skin with exaggerated skin markings; **hyperkeratosis, acanthosis and papillary dermal fibrosis** histologically; mechanism = *chronic rubbing and scratching.*
Lichen simplex chronicus is *lichenified*, not *lichenoid*. They differ in one letter and in everything else.

**Trap 2: Not every disease called "lichen ___" is lichenoid.**
Genuinely lichenoid: **lichen planus, lichen planopilaris, lichen nitidus, lichen striatus** (and, with caveats, lichenoid keratosis). **Not** interface diseases despite the name: **lichen sclerosus** (dermal sclerosis/hyalinisation with a band-like *sclerotic* zone, not a lymphocytic band — though it does have interface change, its defining feature is sclerosis), **lichen simplex chronicus** (lichenification), **lichen spinulosus** (follicular keratosis), **lichen myxoedematosus** (mucin deposition), **lichen amyloidosis** (amyloid deposition), **lichen scrofulosorum** (tuberculid — granulomatous). The word "lichen" in dermatology is a *descriptive* prefix borrowed for surface texture, not a mechanistic one.

**Trap 3: Thinking "lichenoid" and "vacuolar" are different diseases.**
They are **two ends of one continuum**, defined by infiltrate density. Say it as: *interface dermatitis, cell-rich (lichenoid) versus cell-poor (vacuolar).* Many diseases can show either, depending on the lesion's age — and a resolving cell-rich lesion looks cell-poor.

**Trap 4: Forgetting to take a drug history and a sexual history.**
Two of the four causal buckets — pills and pathogens — are diagnosed by *history and serology*, not by morphology. A lichenoid eruption is one of the situations in which a drug chart and a syphilis serology change management more often than another biopsy does.

**Trap 5: Assuming a biopsy will always distinguish LP from lichenoid GVHD or oral LP from lichenoid contact reaction.**
It often cannot. **Context does the work**: prior allogeneic transplant, an amalgam filling in exact contact, a new drug started 8 weeks ago, an HIV risk profile. Histology confirms the *pattern*; the clinician supplies the *cause*.

**Trap 6: Treating lichen planopilaris or erosive oral/genital LP as cosmetic.**
Both cause **irreversible** damage — permanent alopecia, nail pterygium, vaginal or oesophageal stenosis — and erosive mucosal disease carries a small **SCC risk** requiring long-term surveillance. Early, adequately aggressive treatment is the whole game.

**Trap 7: Bullous LP versus lichen planus pemphigoides.** Revisit Section 3 — the difference is *one mechanism vs two*, *blisters only on lesions vs also on normal skin*, and *no linear IgG vs linear IgG/C3 against BP180-NC16A*.

## 9. 🎯 Summary Anchor: The Core Idea in One Picture

Picture a **wall** — the dermo-epidermal junction. Along the top of that wall sits a single row of irreplaceable guards: the **basal keratinocytes**, who also happen to be the skin's **stem cells**.

Now picture an **army of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells massing along that wall, so thick that from above you can no longer see the wall at all.** That single image *is* the lichenoid tissue reaction.

**Why did the army come?** Only four possible reasons — and this is your entire answer to the question:

1. **The guards look foreign for unknown reasons** → **lichen planus and its variants** (hypertrophic, atrophic, linear, actinic, annular, guttate, bullous, LP pemphigoides, lichen planopilaris, LE–LP overlap, plus the cousins lichen nitidus and lichen striatus).
2. **The guards are displaying a microbe's flag** → **infections**: late secondary syphilis, Gianotti–Crosti syndrome, HSV, VZV, PLEVA.
3. **A drug has painted graffiti on the guards' uniforms** → **lichenoid drug eruption, fixed drug eruption, erythema multiforme, TEN.**
4. **Everything else that makes the guards a target** → **LE (SLE, SCLE, DLE, NLE), HIV/immunodeficiency, GVHD, paraneoplastic pemphigus, pityriasis lichenoides, lichenoid contact dermatitis, photosensitive/actinic lichenoid dermatoses**, with **poikiloderma, lichen amyloidosis and erythroderma** as the *end-states and look-alikes*.

**And what the army leaves behind explains every clinical sign:**
apoptotic guards → **Civatte bodies**; gaps in the wall → **vacuolar change**; wall collapse → **Max Joseph spaces and bullae**; panicked epidermal overbuilding → **hypergranulosis → Wickham's striae** and **saw-tooth rete**; spilled pigment → **violaceous colour and long-lasting hyperpigmentation**; dead stem cells → **scarring alopecia, nail pterygium, mucosal stenosis and poikiloderma**.

**One wall. One army. Four reasons it showed up. Every lesion you see is the wreckage.**
