# 🎓 Lichen Planopilaris — A Mini-Lecture

### *"When lichen planus goes after the hair follicle, and why that's a race against the clock"*

---

## PART 1 — The Big Picture: Why This Topic Matters

Let me start by placing this condition in a frame, because if you understand the frame, all the details will fall into place almost automatically.

You already know **lichen planus (LP)** as a disease in which the immune system — specifically cytotoxic T lymphocytes — attacks the **basal keratinocytes** of the epidermis in a band-like (lichenoid) fashion. On the skin it makes violaceous, flat-topped, pruritic papules. On mucosa it makes Wickham's striae. On nails it makes longitudinal ridging and pterygium.

Now ask yourself a simple question: **what else in the skin is lined by basal keratinocytes?** The answer is the **hair follicle** — which is, embryologically and structurally, nothing more than an *invagination of the epidermis that dived down into the dermis*. It has the same basal layer, the same keratinocytes, the same basement membrane.

So when the lichenoid immune attack is directed not at the flat surface epidermis but at the *follicular* epithelium, you get **lichen planopilaris (LPP)** — literally "lichen planus of the hair" (*pilus* = hair). It is also called **follicular lichen planus**.

And here is the single most important consequence, the one that makes LPP a *dermatological emergency of sorts*:

> The surface epidermis, if damaged, regenerates — because its stem cells survive. The hair follicle, if its **stem cell reservoir** is destroyed, **cannot** regenerate. The follicle is replaced by fibrous tissue. The hair is gone **forever**.

That is why LPP belongs to the family of **primary cicatricial (scarring) alopecias** — and within the NAHRS (North American Hair Research Society) classification, specifically to the **primary lymphocytic cicatricial alopecias**, alongside discoid lupus erythematosus, central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia, and pseudopelade of Brocq.

**Clinical translation:** every month you delay diagnosis, the patient permanently loses hair-bearing real estate. LPP is a disease where the goal of treatment is **not regrowth — it is arrest**. Tell your patients this explicitly, or they will judge your therapy a failure when it is actually a success.

---

### Epidemiology — who walks into your clinic?

* **Typical patient:** a **woman aged 40–60 years**. (LPP has a marked female predominance; the postmenopausal peak becomes even more striking for the FFA variant.)
* **Sites of involvement:** the **scalp** is the star of the show, but LPP can also involve the **trunk** and the **medial aspects of the proximal extremities** — flexural areas where follicular keratotic papules appear.
* **Prognostic figure to remember:** among patients with scalp involvement, roughly **40% progress to frank cicatricial alopecia**. In other words, scalp LPP is not benign — nearly half of these scalps will scar.
* LPP is a **rare** cause of alopecia overall, but a **disproportionately important** one, because it is progressive and irreversible.

## PART 2 — The Pathogenesis: The Mechanism That Explains Everything Else

I want to teach this *before* the clinical variants, because once you understand the mechanism, the clinical signs and the histopathology will look like inevitable consequences rather than lists to memorize.

### Step 1 — Meet the bulge

Run your mental finger down a hair follicle. Near the top you have the **infundibulum** (the funnel opening to the surface). Below that, the **isthmus**. At the level of the isthmus, where the **arrector pili muscle** inserts into the follicle and just below the sebaceous duct, sits a small, unassuming swelling of the **outer root sheath (ORS)** called the **bulge**.

The bulge is the **stem cell headquarters of the hair follicle**. It houses **CD34⁺, keratin-15 (K15)⁺ keratinocyte stem cells**. These cells are the master copy — the reserve from which the follicle rebuilds its entire lower half at the beginning of every new anagen (growth) cycle.

> **Analogy:** Think of the hair follicle as a factory that demolishes and rebuilds its own production floor with every business cycle (anagen → catagen → telogen → anagen). The bulge is the **blueprint vault** upstairs. Burn the production floor and you can rebuild it from the blueprints. Burn the **vault**, and the factory can never be rebuilt — no matter how peaceful things become afterwards.

### Step 2 — Immune privilege, and its loss

The bulge region normally enjoys **immune privilege** — a state of local immunological "diplomatic immunity," maintained by downregulated MHC class I expression, local immunosuppressive cytokines (TGF-β, α-MSH), lack of Langerhans cells in the deep follicle, and an intact basement membrane. The body deliberately hides these stem cells from immune surveillance because they are irreplaceable.

In LPP, this privilege **collapses**. MHC class I is upregulated, follicular antigens are presented, and the vault's doors are thrown open.

### Step 3 — The T-cell attack

* **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes** — and, notably, **γδ T cells** — recognise a (still not fully characterised) **follicular antigen in the bulge region of the outer root sheath**.
* They mount a **lichenoid interface attack**: apoptosis of **basal keratinocytes of the follicular epithelium**, exactly as happens to epidermal basal cells in cutaneous LP.
* This produces, histologically, **vacuolar/liquefactive degeneration of the basal layer of the ORS** and Civatte-body-like apoptotic keratinocytes.

### Step 4 — Why fat matters too: the sebaceous gland and PPAR-γ

There is a second, elegant strand to the pathogenesis. **Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ)** is a nuclear receptor essential for **sebaceous gland lipid metabolism**. In LPP, PPAR-γ signalling appears to be deficient. Loss of sebaceous gland function leads to accumulation of **pro-inflammatory lipids** around the follicle, which amplifies the immune attack and destabilises the follicular epithelium.

> This is not academic trivia — it is the **entire rationale for treating LPP with pioglitazone**, a PPAR-γ agonist. When you see pioglitazone in the treatment list later, you will know *why* a diabetes drug is on a hair-loss protocol.

### Step 5 — The point of no return

Once the bulge stem cells are destroyed:

1. **Regeneration is inhibited** — no new anagen can be initiated.
2. The follicular epithelium is replaced by **fibroblasts and collagen**.
3. The follicle collapses into a **vertical fibrous tract** — a scar in the shape of the hair that used to be there.
4. The **follicular ostium (pore) disappears** from the skin surface — which is exactly what you see clinically as a **smooth, shiny, poreless scalp**.

> **Cause → effect → outcome chain, in one sentence:** *Loss of follicular immune privilege → CD8⁺/γδ T-cell attack on bulge stem cells → apoptosis of basal follicular keratinocytes → destruction of the CD34⁺/K15⁺ stem cell reservoir → perifollicular fibrosis → permanent loss of the follicular ostium → irreversible cicatricial alopecia.*

**Key concept to carry forward:** everything else in this lecture — the shiny forehead, the loss of ostia on trichoscopy, the fibrous tracts on histology, the 2-year wait before hair transplant — is a downstream consequence of this one event: **destruction of the bulge**.

## PART 3 — The Clinical Variants: Four Faces of One Disease

There are four classically described variants. They differ in *where* the follicular attack occurs and *what accompanies it* — but the mechanism is identical in all.

---

### Variant 1 — Classical Lichen Planopilaris

This is the prototype: a **patchy, multifocal scarring alopecia of the scalp**, often on the vertex and parietal scalp.

**Early lesions (the inflammatory phase):**
* **Violaceous (perifollicular) papules** — the same colour as cutaneous LP, because it *is* cutaneous LP, just centred on follicles.
* **Perifollicular erythema** — a red halo around each hair emerging from the scalp.
* **Desquamation / perifollicular scale** — a collar of adherent keratotic scale hugging the hair shaft, the surface expression of follicular hyperkeratosis.

**Late / active phase:**
* **Follicular keratoses** — spiny, rough keratotic plugs.
* **Scarring** → **cicatricial alopecia**: patches that are smooth, shiny, ivory-white or skin-coloured, with **complete loss of follicular openings**.

**The single most useful examination pearl:** the disease is **most active at the margins**. Look for **follicular papules, perifollicular erythema and scale at the periphery of existing alopecic patches**. The centre is a burnt-out scar; the rim is the battlefront.

* This is where you **biopsy** (a central biopsy will show only scar and waste the patient's specimen).
* This is where you **inject** intralesional steroid.
* This is where the **anagen hair pull test** will be positive and where the patient will report **symptoms**: itching, burning, tenderness, and **trichodynia** (scalp pain) — symptoms that are often the patient's chief complaint and a good bedside marker of activity.

> **Analogy:** a forest fire. The blackened centre is beyond saving; the ring of flame at the edge is where you point the hose.

---

### Variant 2 — Frontal Fibrosing Alopecia (FFA)

This is now the most frequently *diagnosed* variant worldwide (its incidence has risen strikingly over the last two decades), and it deserves detailed attention.

**Demographic:** classically the **postmenopausal woman** — a very consistent association that hints at a hormonal contribution.

**The clinical picture, feature by feature:**

| Sign | What you see | Why it happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Frontotemporal hairline recession** | A **band-like, symmetrical, progressive recession** of the frontal and temporal hairline, often several centimetres deep | The lichenoid attack marches along the hairline follicles; the recession is the sum of destroyed follicles |
| **Shiny, tight, pale skin on the forehead** | The newly exposed band of skin looks **smooth, glossy, atrophic and poreless**, and often *paler* than the surrounding photoaged forehead | **Loss of follicular ostia** + dermal fibrosis. The band is pale because it was previously shaded by hair and never photoaged — a beautiful clue that this skin is *newly* exposed, not a receding male-pattern hairline |
| **Eyebrow loss (++)** | Loss of the **lateral eyebrows**, often **the very first symptom**, sometimes preceding hairline change by years | Eyebrow follicles are also attacked; because eyebrow follicles are short and fine, they scar early and inconspicuously |
| **"Lonely hair sign"** | One or a few **isolated terminal hairs** left standing in the middle of the newly bald band, in front of the receding line | Scattered follicles are spared; they stand alone like the last trees in a cleared field. **Highly specific for FFA** and a favourite exam point |
| **Body hair loss (rare)** | Loss of hair on **limbs, axillae, pubic area** | Generalised follicular targeting; indicates more extensive disease |

**Additional features worth knowing (they win marks and diagnoses):**
* **Facial papules** — small, skin-coloured, non-inflammatory papules over the temples/cheeks from involvement of facial vellus follicles.
* **Depression of frontal veins** — visible prominence of veins in the atrophic band.
* **Glabellar red dots** — an associated sign.

**FFA in men:** yes, men *are* affected, and you must actively look for it, because the pattern mimics androgenetic alopecia. The distinguishing features in men are **temporal/sideburn recession**, **eyebrow loss**, **facial papules**, and the **shiny, poreless, symptomatic** quality of the affected band.

> **Common misunderstanding #1:** *"FFA is just a female pattern of androgenetic alopecia."* **No.** Androgenetic alopecia causes **miniaturisation with preserved follicular ostia** and a *non-scarring*, patterned thinning, typically with retention of the frontal hairline in women. FFA causes **loss of ostia**, **perifollicular erythema and scale at the advancing edge**, **eyebrow loss**, **symptoms**, and **irreversible scarring**. Run your fingertip over the skin: androgenetic scalp still has pores; FFA skin feels like polished stone.

---

### Variant 3 — Graham-Little-Piccardi-Lassueur Syndrome (GLPLS)

This is LPP *plus* two extra-scalp components. Learn it as a **triad** — examiners love triads.

**The classic triad:**

1. **Cicatricial (scarring) alopecia of the scalp** — patchy and **multifocal**. This is the LPP component: *permanent*, with loss of ostia.
2. **Non-cicatricial (non-scarring) hair loss of the axillae and pubis** — note carefully: **no scarring here**. The axillary and pubic hair thins or disappears but the follicular openings are preserved, and this component may even be reversible.
3. **Follicular spinous (keratotic) papules** on the **trunk and extremities** (and also scalp) — rough, spiny, keratin-plugged follicular papules that feel like sandpaper.

**Distribution note:** these papules may occasionally follow a **linear (blaschkoid) distribution**, a curiosity worth mentioning.

> **Why the difference in scarring between scalp and axilla?** This is a genuinely instructive point. The answer lies in **follicular architecture and cycling**: terminal scalp follicles have long anagen phases and a heavily targeted bulge; the axillary/pubic follicles appear to suffer a milder, more superficial insult that spares the stem cell compartment. The lesson: **"scarring" is not a property of the disease alone — it is a property of the disease *at that anatomical site*.**

> **Common misunderstanding #2:** students often write "scarring alopecia of scalp, axilla and pubis." That loses the entire point of the syndrome. The **contrast** between cicatricial scalp loss and **non**-cicatricial axillary/pubic loss *is* the diagnosis.

---

### Variant 4 — Follicular LP Tumidus (Lichen Planopilaris Tumidus)

The rarest of the four, and the one most often misnamed.

* **Clinical appearance:** **boggy, oedematous plaques studded with follicular papules and comedo-like keratotic plugs** (*tumidus* = swollen). Cysts and milia-like structures may be present.
* **Characteristic site:** the **retroauricular area** (behind the ears) — an easily missed location, so make a habit of lifting the ears.
* Because it is swollen and comedonal, it is frequently mistaken for **acne, hidradenitis or a cystic process** until biopsy reveals the lichenoid folliculitis.

📌 **Nomenclature correction (important):** the term sometimes printed as *"Follicular LP follicularis tumidus"* is **tautological and incorrect** — "LP follicularis" already *means* follicular LP, so "follicularis" appears twice. The correct designations are **"Follicular LP tumidus"** or **"Lichen planopilaris tumidus."**

## PART 4 — Differential Diagnosis: Thinking Like a Diagnostician

The differentials fall into two natural groups, and the group depends on *which* clinical feature dominates.

### Group A — If **follicular keratotic papules** dominate (trunk/limbs, GLPLS-like presentation)

| Differential | How you tell it apart |
|---|---|
| **Keratosis pilaris** | Very common, **non-scarring**, non-inflammatory follicular papules on **extensor arms and thighs**; no violaceous hue; no alopecia; onset in childhood; "chicken skin" texture. The absence of inflammation and scarring is decisive |
| **Darier's disease** | Greasy, warty keratotic papules in a **seborrhoeic distribution** (chest, back, scalp margins, flexures); autosomal dominant, **ATP2A2** mutation (SERCA2 calcium pump); look for **nail V-shaped nicks** and **palmar pits**; histology shows **dyskeratosis with corps ronds and grains** and suprabasal acantholytic clefting — nothing lichenoid |
| **Follicular mucinosis (alopecia mucinosa)** | Boggy plaques with alopecia; histology shows **mucin deposition within the follicular epithelium** (Alcian blue/colloidal iron positive) rather than a lichenoid interface. **Critically, it may be a manifestation of mycosis fungoides** — so demand immunophenotyping and clonality studies in adults |
| **Lichen scrofulosorum** | A **tuberculid**: grouped lichenoid follicular papules on the trunk in a patient with underlying TB; **strongly positive tuberculin/IGRA**; histology shows **superficial perifollicular granulomas** (classically described as caseating/tuberculoid), not a lichenoid infiltrate |

### Group B — If **scarring alopecia** dominates (the crucial one)

| Differential | How you tell it apart |
|---|---|
| **Discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) of the scalp** | Larger, **atrophic, dyspigmented** plaques with **carpet-tack follicular plugging**, telangiectasia and mottled hypo/hyperpigmentation. **Histology:** infiltrate is **both superficial *and* deep**, **peri-follicular *and* peri-adnexal/perivascular**, with **thickened basement membrane**, **interstitial dermal mucin** and epidermal atrophy. **DIF (lupus band test) is positive** — granular IgG/IgM/C3 at the DEJ. Trichoscopy shows **large yellow dots and arborising vessels** |
| *(Contrast with LPP)* | LPP infiltrate is **confined to the upper third of the follicle**, is **exclusively folliculocentric**, spares the interfollicular epidermis, has **no basement membrane thickening or mucin**, and **DIF is negative or shows only shaggy fibrinogen / cytoid bodies**. Trichoscopy shows **perifollicular scale and erythema with absent ostia** |

> **Practical tip:** DLE and LPP are the two great lymphocytic scarring alopecias, and distinguishing them determines therapy (both respond to hydroxychloroquine, but DLE demands systemic lupus screening). When in doubt: **two biopsies** — one in formalin for H&E, one in saline/Michel's medium for DIF — taken from the **active margin**.

## PART 5 — Histopathology: Reading the Scar

Here is where the mechanism becomes visible. I will give you the features, but more importantly, I will tell you **why each one is there**.

### Where to biopsy and how

* Take a **4 mm punch**, **oriented parallel to the hair shafts**, from the **actively inflamed margin**.
* **Horizontal (transverse) sections** are invaluable for counting follicles and assessing fibrous tracts; **vertical sections** best display the interface change. Ideally request both (or two punches).

### The features

| Feature | What it looks like | **Why it is there (mechanism)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Follicular plugging** | Compact **hyperkeratosis filling the follicular infundibulum** | Inflammation disrupts normal infundibular keratinisation, so corneocytes are retained rather than shed. *This is the microscopic basis of the clinically visible perifollicular scale and spiny keratotic papules* |
| **Liquefactive (vacuolar) degeneration** | Vacuolation and hydropic change of the **basal cell layer of the outer root sheath**, with apoptotic/necrotic keratinocytes (colloid/Civatte bodies) | The direct footprint of **CD8⁺ T-cell–mediated basal keratinocyte apoptosis** — the same interface change as cutaneous LP, just relocated to the follicle |
| **Perifollicular lymphocytic infiltrate** | A **dense, band-like (lichenoid) lymphocytic infiltrate hugging the upper third of the follicle** — the **infundibulum and isthmus** | The isthmus is where the **bulge** lives. The immune system is attacking the stem cell niche, so the infiltrate is *anatomically wherever the target antigen is*. **This level-specificity is the diagnostic signature of LPP** |
| **Concentric lamellar fibroplasia** | **Onion-skin-like concentric rings of fibrosis** encircling the follicle, seen in established disease | Chronic inflammation recruits fibroblasts, which lay down collagen in the perifollicular sheath. *This is the microscopic equivalent of the clinically shiny, indurated perifollicular skin*, and it is the **single most characteristic finding** |
| **Loss of hair follicles → fibrous tracts** | Follicles are absent and replaced by **vertical/linear fibrous tracts** (also called follicular "scars" or streamers), often with retained **naked hair shafts** and a **foreign-body giant cell reaction** | Once the stem cells are gone, the follicle involutes and collagen fills the vacated space. **The fibrous tract is the tombstone of the follicle.** Its presence on histology = irreversibility |
| **Sebaceous gland loss** | Early and often complete **absence of sebaceous glands** | Consistent with the **PPAR-γ hypothesis**; sebaceous glands are destroyed early, sometimes before obvious follicular destruction |
| **Interfollicular epidermis** | **Normal** (particularly in non-scalp sites) | LPP is **exclusively folliculocentric**. The disease has an address, and that address is the follicle. *This is the key negative finding that separates LPP from DLE, where the epidermis is atrophic with basement membrane thickening* |

> ### 🔑 Histological Pearl — the diagnostic triad of LPP
> 1. **Perifollicular lichenoid lymphocytic infiltrate at the isthmus/infundibulum** (the *right cells in the right place*)
> 2. **Vacuolar interface change at the follicular basal layer** (the *mechanism of killing*)
> 3. **Concentric lamellar fibroplasia with follicular destruction and fibrous tract formation** (the *permanent consequence*)

**Direct immunofluorescence:** typically **negative**, or shows **shaggy fibrinogen along the basement membrane** and **cytoid (colloid) bodies staining for IgM** — a useful way to exclude DLE, where DIF is strongly and granularly positive.

> **Common misunderstanding #3:** *"Scarring alopecia means you see a keloid-like scar."* You do not. The "scar" of cicatricial alopecia is **subtle perifollicular and tract-like fibrosis** — often best appreciated with an elastic tissue stain (loss of elastic fibres in the fibrous tract) rather than on routine H&E. Clinically, the "scar" manifests simply as **smooth skin with no visible pores**.

## PART 6 — Management: Arrest the Fire, Don't Wait for the Forest to Regrow

### The three aims — state them to the patient on day one

1. **Reduce inflammation** and halt active disease progression.
2. **Alleviate symptoms** — pruritus, burning, pain, **trichodynia**.
3. **Prevent progression** of scarring alopecia — i.e., **preserve** what remains.

> **Set expectations explicitly:** "I cannot regrow hair in the smooth, shiny areas — those follicles are gone. My job is to stop the process before it takes the rest." A patient who understands this will stay adherent; a patient who does not will abandon effective treatment as useless.

**How do you know if you are winning?** Use objective markers of **activity**, not hair count:
* Resolution of **symptoms** (itch, burning, trichodynia)
* Disappearance of **perifollicular erythema and scale**
* **Negative anagen hair pull test** at the margin
* Stable **standardised photographs** and **hairline/eyebrow measurements**
* Formally: the **LPP Activity Index (LPPAI)** score

---

### 🥇 First Line — Corticosteroids (topical and intralesional)

**Topical corticosteroids**
* **Ultra-potent**: **clobetasol propionate 0.05%** solution/foam/lotion, applied to affected scalp — particularly the **active margins**.
* Vehicle matters: solutions, foams and lotions penetrate hair-bearing scalp far better than ointments and are cosmetically tolerable.
* Watch for **scalp atrophy, folliculitis and telangiectasia** with prolonged use; consider pulsed or weekend dosing for maintenance.
* May be combined with **topical calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus ointment) as a steroid-sparing adjunct, especially on the forehead in FFA where atrophy is cosmetically unacceptable.

**Intralesional corticosteroids**
* **Triamcinolone acetonide 10 mg/mL** (range 5–10 mg/mL), injected into the **active inflammatory margins**, **every 4–6 weeks**.
* Typically continued for a **minimum of 6–12 months**, guided by activity.
* Technique: small (0.1 mL) intradermal aliquots ~1 cm apart along the advancing edge; cap total dose per session (commonly ≤20 mg) to limit systemic absorption.
* **Adverse effects to warn about:** **local cutaneous atrophy** (often reversible over months), hypopigmentation, and rarely **telangiectasia**.

📌 **Important correction:** the protocol is triamcinolone **"every 4–6 weeks"**, referring to the **injection interval** — *not* "for 4–6 weeks", which would imply a total course duration. This distinction matters clinically: LPP needs **months** of suppression, not weeks.

---

### 🥈 Second Line — Systemic Therapy

**Indication:** disease that **progresses despite 6–12 months of corticosteroid therapy**, or that is **rapidly progressive / extensive** at presentation (in which case, do not waste time — start systemic therapy up front).

**Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ)** — *the workhorse systemic agent for LPP and FFA*
* **Dose: 200 mg twice daily** (approximately 5 mg/kg/day of actual body weight — this weight cap is the modern retinopathy-safety standard).
* **Give it a full 6-month trial before judging response.** Antimalarials are slow; a 6-week verdict is meaningless.
* *Mechanism:* lysosomotropic accumulation raises endosomal pH, impairing **antigen processing/presentation** and blunting **Toll-like receptor (TLR7/9)** signalling — i.e., it turns down the antigen-presentation machinery that broke the follicle's immune privilege in the first place.
* **Monitoring:** baseline and periodic **ophthalmologic examination** (retinopathy), full blood count, liver and renal function; screen for **G6PD deficiency** where relevant.

**Systemic corticosteroids**
* **Prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day** as a **short course** for **acute, rapidly progressive flares**, or as a bridge while a slower agent (HCQ, MMF) takes effect.
* Highly effective but **not a maintenance strategy** — relapse on withdrawal is the rule, and cumulative toxicity is unacceptable. Think of it as a fire extinguisher, not a sprinkler system.

---

### 🥉 Third Line — Immunomodulators and Adjuncts

These are for refractory, rapidly progressive, or steroid/HCQ-intolerant disease. Learn them as a table, but learn the **logic column** too.

| Agent | Dosage | Mechanism / Rationale | Key cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cyclosporine** | **3–5 mg/kg/day** | Calcineurin inhibition → blocks IL-2 and **T-cell activation**; attacks the effector arm directly. Fast and effective | **Short-term use only**; **nephrotoxicity**, hypertension, gingival hyperplasia, hypertrichosis; monitor BP, creatinine, potassium, drug levels |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)** | **500 mg BD for 5–6 months** (often escalated to 1 g BD) | Inhibits **inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase** → selectively starves lymphocytes of guanosine nucleotides. Excellent **steroid-sparing** agent with a **good safety profile** | GI upset, cytopenias, infection risk; **teratogenic** — reliable contraception required |
| **Thalidomide** | **50 mg** (up to 100 mg/day) | **Anti-TNF-α**, anti-angiogenic and immunomodulatory | **Teratogenicity** — mandatory pregnancy prevention counselling and programme enrolment; **peripheral neuropathy**; thrombosis risk |
| **Pioglitazone (PPAR-γ agonist)** | **15–30 mg once daily** | Restores **PPAR-γ signalling** in sebaceous glands → corrects the lipid-metabolic defect implicated in LPP pathogenesis. **The most mechanistically elegant drug on this list** | Weight gain, fluid retention, **heart failure**, bone fracture risk, bladder cancer concerns; monitor accordingly |
| **Doxycycline (tetracyclines)** | **100 mg** (once or twice daily) | Used at **anti-inflammatory, not antimicrobial, dose** — inhibits **matrix metalloproteinases**, neutrophil chemotaxis and pro-inflammatory cytokines | Photosensitivity, GI upset; avoid in pregnancy/children |
| **Isotretinoin (systemic retinoid)** | **0.5–1 mg/kg/day** | **Normalises follicular keratinisation** — directly targets the follicular plugging/hyperkeratosis | Teratogenic; mucocutaneous dryness; lipids and LFTs |
| **Acitretin** | **0.2–0.6 mg/kg/day** | Same keratinisation rationale; **particularly useful for hyperkeratotic/spinous variants** (GLPLS-type spiny papules, LP tumidus) | Teratogenic with **3-year post-treatment conception ban**; hyperlipidaemia |
| **Minoxidil 5% topical** | Twice daily | **Purely supportive** — prolongs anagen and stimulates **surviving, non-scarred** follicles, and helps any coexisting androgenetic alopecia | ⚠️ **Does NOT reverse scarring.** It is a cosmetic optimiser, never a disease-modifying drug |

> **Common misunderstanding #4:** *"Minoxidil is a treatment for LPP."* It is not an anti-inflammatory and cannot resurrect a destroyed follicle. It is **adjunctive**, aimed at maximising the cosmetic output of the follicles you managed to save. Similarly, remember that many LPP/FFA patients have **coexisting androgenetic alopecia** — treating both gives the best cosmetic result.

---

### Special Case — Management of Frontal Fibrosing Alopecia

FFA has its own therapeutic identity, and this is a high-yield exam point:

* **5-alpha reductase inhibitors are the current standard of care for FFA:**
  * **Finasteride 2.5–5 mg/day**, or
  * **Dutasteride 0.5 mg/day** (increasingly favoured; some regimens use weekly or intralesional dutasteride)
* They are typically **combined with hydroxychloroquine** and topical/intralesional steroids.
* *Why would an anti-androgen work in an inflammatory, lichenoid disease?* Two reasons: (1) the striking **postmenopausal predominance** suggests a hormonal permissive factor in follicular immune privilege and sebaceous function; and (2) many patients have coexisting androgen-dependent hair loss. The evidence base is largely observational but consistent and strong enough that omitting 5ARIs from an FFA answer is a significant gap.
* **Counselling points:** teratogenicity (absolute contraception requirement in women of childbearing potential), possible mood/libido effects, and the need for **long-term** therapy.
* **Additional FFA-specific measures:** aggressive **photoprotection** of the newly exposed, atrophic band; cosmetic camouflage; **eyebrow** management with topical steroid/minoxidil/tattooing once stable; and consideration of **oral minoxidil** or **pioglitazone** in resistant cases.

📌 **Note:** 5ARIs are not mentioned in the original source text — this is a genuine omission, corrected here from Rook's and Bolognia, and it is essential exam-relevant content.

---

### ✂️ Surgical Management — Hair Transplantation

* **Absolute prerequisite: ≥2 years of complete disease stability (quiescence).**
* **Confirm quiescence both clinically and histologically** — no perifollicular erythema, no scale, no symptoms, no progression on serial photographs, and ideally a **repeat biopsy showing no active inflammation**.
* **Why the strict rule?** Transplanting into an immunologically primed scalp invites **graft failure**: the same T-cell attack that destroyed the native follicles will destroy the grafts. Worse, **surgical trauma can trigger a flare** — the **Koebner phenomenon** is a hallmark of lichen planus and its variants, and LPP is no exception.
* **Options:** follicular unit transplantation/extraction, scalp reduction, or — for FFA specifically — **eyebrow transplantation** in long-stable disease. **Cosmetic camouflage** (hair fibres, scalp micropigmentation, wigs, hairpieces) remains an important and immediately available option for every patient.

> **Analogy:** You do not rebuild a house while the wildfire is still smouldering in the hills. You wait until the fire is demonstrably, durably out — and in LPP, "demonstrably" means two years.

## 🎯 PART 8 — The Core Idea in One Picture

If you remember nothing else, remember this chain — every fact in this lecture hangs from it:

> **Lichen planus finds the hair follicle.** The lichenoid CD8⁺/γδ T-cell attack targets the **bulge** at the follicular isthmus — the home of the **CD34⁺/K15⁺ stem cells** — after that region loses its **immune privilege** (with a contributing **PPAR-γ/sebaceous lipid** defect).
>
> **Clinically**, the attack shows up as **perifollicular erythema, scale and violaceous papules at the *margins*** of expanding patches — whether that pattern is **patchy** (classical LPP), **band-like along the frontotemporal hairline with eyebrow loss and a lonely hair** (FFA), **scalp-scarring plus non-scarring axillary/pubic loss plus spiny papules** (GLPLS), or **boggy retroauricular plaques** (LP tumidus).
>
> **Histologically**, the same attack reads as **follicular plugging + vacuolar interface change at the ORS + a lichenoid infiltrate confined to the upper third of the follicle + concentric lamellar fibroplasia**, ending in follicles replaced by **vertical fibrous tracts** — with the **interfollicular epidermis untouched**.
>
> **Because the stem cells die, the alopecia is permanent.** Therefore treatment is not about regrowth — it is about **switching off inflammation fast**: steroids (topical, intralesional, short systemic) → **hydroxychloroquine** → immunomodulators (cyclosporine, MMF, thalidomide, **pioglitazone**, doxycycline, retinoids), with **5-alpha reductase inhibitors** for FFA, **minoxidil** only as cosmetic support, and **hair transplantation only after two years of proven quiescence**.
>
> **LPP is a race between your anti-inflammatory therapy and the patient's stem cell reserve. Every week counts.**
